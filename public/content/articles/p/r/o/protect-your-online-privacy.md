## What Are Trackers?

![Protect Your Online Privacy Overview](/content/images/protect-your-online-privacy/protect-your-online-privacy-overview.webp "Protect Your Online Privacy Overview")

Trackers are scripts or cookies that websites use to collect information about your online activities, such as browsing history, search queries, and personal data. Some serve legitimate purposes — a shop remembering your cart, a site measuring which articles get read. But plenty of trackers exist purely to build cross-site profiles of you, and those are the ones worth blocking.

### Types of Trackers

There are several distinct types you'll encounter:

- **Cookies**: Small text files stored on your device to remember your browsing history and preferences. Third-party cookies are the classic cross-site tracking mechanism.
- **Scripts**: JavaScript code that runs on every page you load and can record clicks, scrolls, form inputs, and time spent on each section.
- **Beacons**: Tiny images or scripts — often invisible — that fire when a page or email loads, confirming that you opened it and reporting back to the tracker's server.

## How Do Trackers Work?

![Diagram-like visualization of how tracking scripts follow users across websites](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1563013544-824ae1b704d3?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1200&q=80)

Trackers work by collecting information about your online activities and sending it back to a server. The data can include:

- **Browsing history**: The websites you visit and the pages you view, stitched together across sites that all embed the same third-party tracker.
- **Search queries**: The searches you perform and the results you click on, which reveal interests and intent.
- **Personal data**: Anything you submit through forms on pages where the tracker runs — sometimes including your name and email address.

Modern trackers also use **fingerprinting**, which combines signals like your screen resolution, fonts, browser version, and hardware details into a unique ID that survives even if you delete cookies. Blocking these scripts at the network level is more reliable than trying to spoof each signal individually.

Technically, tracker blockers do their work by inspecting and filtering the network requests a page makes — a capability built on the extension APIs described in the <a href="https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">official Chrome for Developers extension documentation</a>. If you want to go deeper on reducing your exposure without gutting browser speed, our guide to [stopping trackers on Chrome without slowing down](/blog/stop-trackers-on-chrome-without-slowing-down) covers the performance-conscious approach.

## Why Block Trackers?

![Protect Your Online Privacy Features](/content/images/protect-your-online-privacy/protect-your-online-privacy-features.webp "Protect Your Online Privacy Features")

Blocking trackers is one of the highest-impact privacy upgrades you can make. By blocking trackers, you can:

- **Prevent targeted advertising**: Stop trackers from assembling your browsing history into ad profiles that follow you around the web.
- **Protect your personal data**: Keep trackers from collecting and storing personal details in databases you can't see or audit.
- **Improve your online security**: Malicious scripts often travel through the same ad and tracking infrastructure, so blocking those requests also cuts off a common malware delivery path.
- **Speed up page loads**: Every blocked tracker is a request your browser doesn't have to make — pages frequently load noticeably faster as a side effect.

## How to Choose a Chrome Extension to Block Trackers

![Running a permissions and publisher review before installing a tracker blocker](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1563986768609-322da13575f3?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1200&q=80)

Before installing any privacy extension, run this quick checklist:

1. **Verify the publisher**: Check who maintains the extension and whether updates ship regularly — abandoned privacy tools quickly become ineffective.
2. **Read the permissions**: A tracker blocker needs network-request access, but it shouldn't demand access to everything.
3. **Test for breakage**: Browse your top ten sites after installing; a good blocker stops trackers without breaking logins, video players, or checkout flows.
4. **Prefer transparency**: Look for tools that show you what they blocked, so you can verify the protection is real.
5. **Install from the <a href="https://support.google.com/chrome_webstore/answer/2664769?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Chrome Web Store</a> only**: Sideloading extensions from random sites is how most "privacy" malware gets in.

If you use aggressive ad blocking as well, note that some blockers overlap in function — our breakdown of [the power of ad-block extensions for Google Chrome](/blog/the-power-of-extension-adblock-google-chrome) explains where ad blocking and tracker blocking differ.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently asked questions about **Chrome extensions to block trackers**:

### What is the best Chrome extension to block trackers?

[Redirect Shield](/extension/redirect-shield) is one of the most effective options because it combines real-time tracker blocking with redirect-chain protection. Pair it with Light Popup Blocker for popups and overlay ads, and you cover both major tracking surfaces in the browser.

### How do I install a Chrome extension to block trackers?

Visit the Chrome Web Store, search for the extension you want, review its permissions and reviews, then click **Add to Chrome** and confirm. The extension starts protecting you immediately; check its settings to whitelist any sites where blocking causes problems.

### Are Chrome extensions to block trackers free?

Yes, many are free, including [Redirect Shield](/extension/redirect-shield) and [Light Popup Blocker](/extension/light-popup-blocker). Free tools are usually supported by updates from the developer rather than by your data — which is exactly the business model you want in a privacy product.

### Can I use multiple Chrome extensions to block trackers at the same time?

You can, but more blockers isn't automatically better. Overlapping extensions can conflict with each other and waste memory, so choose two with complementary jobs — trackers and popups — rather than three tools that do the same thing.

### How often should I update my Chrome extension to block trackers?

Keep automatic extension updates enabled in Chrome. Tracker networks change constantly, and updates are how a blocker learns new patterns; an outdated tool quietly stops protecting you.

### Can I use a Chrome extension to block trackers on other browsers?

Chrome extensions are built for Chrome and Chromium-based browsers. Some Chromium relatives can install them, but Firefox and Safari use different extension systems, so look for a dedicated version of the tool for those browsers.
