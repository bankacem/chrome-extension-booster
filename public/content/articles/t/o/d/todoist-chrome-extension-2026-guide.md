## I Moved My Whole To-Do List Into the Browser for a Month

I have tried most task managers. I have abandoned most task managers. The pattern is always the same: a week of enthusiasm, a month of good intentions, then the slow drift back to sticky notes and remembering things in the shower. So when I decided to test Todoist for Chrome properly, I set one rule for myself. No separate app. Everything through the extension, for a full month, or the test means nothing.

Todoist is not new. Over 47 million people use it, according to its own Chrome Web Store listing, and the extension — version 12.21.9, updated July 31, 2026, a 662 KiB download from Doist Inc. — has been around for years. That is the point. In a category full of apps that launch, trend and vanish, Todoist is still here, still updated, and still the default recommendation in almost every productivity roundup I read while researching this piece. I wanted to know whether that reputation survives daily use, or whether it survives because nobody has bothered to re-test it.

**Get it:** [Todoist for Chrome on the Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/todoist-for-chrome-planne/jldhpllghnbhlbpcmnajkpdmadaolakh)

## What the Extension Actually Does

Click the Todoist icon in your toolbar and you get Quick Add: type the task, and Todoist parses it. "Call the accountant tomorrow at 10am #work p1" becomes a task called "Call the accountant", due tomorrow at 10am, in the Work project, priority 1. That natural-language parsing is the feature that matters most, and it works in the extension exactly as it works in the full app. No clicking through date pickers. No dropdown for the project. One line, one task.

The second thing it does is capture. Any page can become a task with one click, and the page link is attached automatically. Select text on a page, right-click, and the selected text becomes a hyperlinked task. I used this more than I expected: articles to read, a support page to follow up on, a product page I needed to revisit before buying. The task carries the link, so a week later I am not staring at "check that article" wondering which article.

The third thing is planning. The extension opens a full view — list, board or calendar — right from the toolbar, so you can organize the day without opening a tab. I planned most mornings from the popup: drag today's tasks into order, push the rest to tomorrow, close it. It is a small thing, but planning inside the browser, where the work actually happens, meant I planned more often than when planning required opening another app.

## The Free Plan Wall

Here is where the honest part starts. The free plan — called Beginner — covers 5 active projects, 3 filter views, and 1 week of activity history. Five projects sounds generous until you live in it. Mine were Work, Personal, Reading, House, and Health by day three, and then a freelance project arrived and something had to go. That is the design. The free tier is a real product, not a demo, but its ceiling is deliberately low enough that a busy person hits it.

Pro is $5 a month on annual billing, or $7 month to month, and it removes the project cap — 300 personal projects — and adds reminders, calendar layout, 150 filter views, and the AI Assistant. Business is $8 per user a month and adds team workspaces and admin controls. I paid for Pro in week two, when the project cap forced me to merge two projects that had no business being merged. If your life fits in five projects, stay free forever. If it does not, $5 a month is a fair price for the tool that holds your entire day together, and it is cheaper than the coffee I buy while procrastinating about my tasks.

## Three Competitors, Three Different Answers

**TickTick** is the closest rival, and in some ways the more generous one. Its Chrome extension — version 1.2.1.2, updated May 26, 2026 — does the same one-click capture, turns Gmail messages into tasks, opens in a side panel, and adds something Todoist does not have at all: a built-in Pomodoro timer and a stopwatch. Its free plan covers the basics, and its Premium tier is paid. Where TickTick falls short for me is the capture flow: reviewers consistently note that saving a page requires signing in and copy-pasting rather than the single click Todoist offers, and the extension asks for broad permissions over websites, tabs and browsing activity. It is the better tool if you want a timer next to your tasks. It is the worse tool if capture speed is the whole point.

**Get it:** [TickTick on the Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/ticktick-todo-task-list/diankknpkndanachmlckaikddgcehkod)

**Microsoft To Do** is the free option, and it shows. It costs nothing with a Microsoft account, syncs with Outlook, and does the basics competently. Its weakness is the browser: there is no real Chrome extension. You use it on the web, or in the Outlook sidebar, and capturing a page means copying a link by hand. For someone already living in Microsoft 365 it is a reasonable zero-cost choice. For someone whose tasks arrive from across the web, it is not a competitor to Todoist at all — it is a different product for a different workflow.

**Microsoft To Do:** [todo.microsoft.com](https://todo.microsoft.com)

**Google Tasks** is the other free option, and the weakest of the three. It sits in the Gmail and Calendar side panel, handles simple personal lists, and costs nothing. It has no projects in any meaningful sense, no filters, no priorities worth the name, and no extension. I mention it because it is what most people use before they install anything, and the jump from Google Tasks to Todoist is the jump from a shopping list to a system.

## Five Scenarios That Decided It For Me

**Catching tasks from articles.** I read a lot for work, and every article used to produce a task I forgot by the next tab. With Todoist, the article becomes the task, link attached, due date parsed from one line. This alone justified the install for me.

**The morning plan.** Ten minutes, extension popup, drag today into order. I did this more days in the test month than in the previous three months combined, because the plan lived where the work lived.

**Recurring chores.** "Water the plants every Tuesday" is a recurring task with a date, not a memory. Todoist handles recurring dates in plain language, and it is the feature I missed most the one week I tried living without it.

**Sharing a list.** A shared grocery project with my partner, tasks assigned, checked off from either phone. The free plan covers this — shared projects do not count against your five in the way you fear — and it worked without a single sync complaint in a month.

**The email that becomes a task.** An email I cannot answer today becomes a task with the link attached, due tomorrow. TickTick does this from Gmail too, and slightly better, because its Gmail integration is deeper. If your tasks mostly arrive by email, that is a genuine point in TickTick's favour.

## What Annoyed Me

Two things, both worth knowing before you install. First, the free plan's five-project cap is a wall, not a slope. There is no gentle warning; you simply cannot create the sixth project, and the upgrade prompt is the only way forward. Second, the extension's permissions are broad — it reads the URL and title of the active tab to capture it, which is the minimum it needs, but if you are the kind of person who audits permissions, know that capture requires tab access by design. Neither of these is a reason not to install it. Both are reasons to install it with your eyes open.

## Key Takeaways

- Todoist's Chrome extension is the fastest way to get a task out of your head: one line of natural language, page link attached automatically.
- The free plan is real but capped at 5 projects, 3 filters and 1 week of history — busy people hit the wall in days.
- Pro is $5 a month on annual billing and removes the cap, adding reminders, calendar layout and the AI Assistant.
- TickTick is the better pick if you want a Pomodoro timer and Gmail capture built in; its quick-save is clumsier.
- Microsoft To Do is free but has no real Chrome extension, and Google Tasks is a list, not a system.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Todoist for Chrome free?

Yes. The free Beginner plan covers 5 active projects, 3 filter views and 1 week of activity history. Pro is $5 a month on annual billing, or $7 month to month.

### Can I add a website as a task?

Yes. Click the extension and the current page becomes a task with its link attached. Select text first and the selection becomes a hyperlinked task.

### Does Todoist work offline?

The extension needs a connection to sync, but the mobile and desktop apps keep a local copy and sync when you are back online.

### What is the difference between Todoist and TickTick?

Todoist captures faster and has the larger user base and better reviews. TickTick adds a built-in Pomodoro timer and deeper Gmail integration, but its quick-save flow is clumsier.

### Can I share projects with other people?

Yes, including on the free plan. Shared projects work from the extension and sync to every device.

### Does Todoist have a calendar view?

Yes, on Pro. The free plan shows list and board views; the calendar layout and reminders need Pro.

## Verdict

I kept Todoist, and I paid for Pro. The free plan lasted eleven days before the five-project cap forced the decision, and that is the honest shape of this product: free to try properly, paid to live in. The extension earns its place because capture is one click and planning happens where the work happens, and after a month of running my whole list through it, going back to sticky notes felt like going back to a paper map.

If you want a timer next to your tasks and you live in Gmail, try TickTick first — its Pomodoro timer and email capture are genuinely better, and its free tier is generous. If you live in Microsoft 365 and your tasks arrive by email and meeting, Microsoft To Do costs nothing and is good enough. Everyone else should start with Todoist, stay free while five projects last, and pay the $5 when the cap arrives. It will arrive.
