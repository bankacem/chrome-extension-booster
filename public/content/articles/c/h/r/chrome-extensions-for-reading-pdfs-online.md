Most "best PDF extensions" lists jump straight into a list of ten tools without asking the one question that actually matters: what are you trying to do with the PDF? Reading a report is a different problem than annotating a research paper, and both are different from filling out and signing a form. This guide is organized by that question instead of by popularity, and it covers a few things most PDF-extension roundups skip entirely — like whether you need an extension at all, and what permissions you're actually granting when you install one.

## Do You Actually Need a PDF Extension?

Before installing anything: Chrome already has a built-in PDF viewer. Click a PDF link or open a local PDF file, and Chrome displays it natively — no extension required. It supports basic zooming, page navigation, printing, and simple text search out of the box.

If all you do is *read* PDFs occasionally, you may not need a third-party extension at all. The extensions below earn their place when you need something Chrome's built-in viewer doesn't do: real annotation, form-filling, merging files, or syncing highlights across devices.

## Best All-Around PDF Reader Extensions

**[Xodo PDF Reader & Editor](https://chromewebstore.google.com/search/Xodo%20PDF)** goes beyond Chrome's built-in viewer with a cleaner reading experience, basic annotation tools, and page-organizing features like merging or reordering pages — a solid general-purpose upgrade if you read PDFs often enough that the native viewer feels limited.

**[Kami](https://chromewebstore.google.com/search/Kami%20PDF)** is built with students and educators in mind, offering a clean reading layout alongside annotation and collaboration tools, which makes it a common choice in classroom settings where multiple people need to mark up the same document.

## Best for Annotating and Highlighting

**Kami** (again) is worth a second mention here specifically for annotation: highlighting, sticky notes, and freehand drawing on top of a PDF, with changes saved to your account rather than only on the device you're using.

**[DocHub](https://chromewebstore.google.com/search/DocHub)** focuses on a similarly annotation-first workflow, with the addition of e-signatures — useful if you regularly need to mark up a document and then sign it in the same session, instead of switching tools partway through.

A genuinely useful check most PDF-extension guides skip: if you need your highlights and notes to show up the same way on your laptop *and* your tablet, check whether the extension stores annotations to a cloud account (most of the extensions above do) versus only locally in the browser — the ones without account sync will not carry your annotations to another device.

## Best for Filling and Signing Forms

**DocHub** covers this well, letting you type into form fields, add a signature, and export the completed PDF without printing anything.

**[Adobe Acrobat (Chrome extension)](https://chromewebstore.google.com/search/Adobe%20Acrobat)** is the more full-featured option here if you regularly work with complex forms — it handles form recognition and signing with the reliability you'd expect from the tool most PDF standards are built around, at the cost of being heavier than the lighter alternatives above.

## Offline Access and Permissions to Check Before Installing

Two things most "best PDF extensions" articles don't mention, and both are worth two minutes before you install anything:

**Offline access.** Several PDF extensions are built around a cloud account and may prompt you to sign in or sync before opening even a PDF saved locally on your computer. If you frequently work without a reliable internet connection, test opening a local file right after installing — before you're relying on it mid-flight or on a train.

**Permissions.** When you install a PDF extension, Chrome shows you what it's asking for. A PDF reader legitimately needs access to files you open — but if an extension also requests broad access to "read and change all your data on all websites," that's worth pausing on. That level of access isn't necessary just to read or annotate a PDF, and it's a reasonable reason to look for an alternative with a narrower permission request.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do I need an extension just to open and read a PDF in Chrome?**
A: No — Chrome's built-in PDF viewer handles basic reading, zooming, and printing with zero installation. Extensions add value once you need annotation, form-filling, or cross-device syncing.

**Q: Will my highlights and notes show up if I switch from my laptop to my phone or tablet?**
A: Only if the extension saves annotations to a cloud account rather than just locally in the browser. Check this specifically if cross-device access matters to you — it's not true of every PDF extension.

**Q: Can I fill out and sign a PDF form without printing it?**
A: Yes — DocHub and the Adobe Acrobat Chrome extension both support typing into form fields and adding a signature directly, then exporting the completed file.

**Q: Should I be concerned about the permissions a PDF extension asks for?**
A: It's worth a quick look. A PDF extension legitimately needs access to the files you open, but broad "access all website data" permissions go beyond what a PDF reader needs — a reasonable signal to consider an alternative.

## Conclusion

Start with Chrome's built-in viewer — you may not need anything else. If you find yourself wanting to annotate, fill forms, or keep your highlights in sync across devices, pick the one extension above that matches that specific need rather than installing several that overlap, and take thirty seconds to check its permissions and offline behavior before you rely on it.
