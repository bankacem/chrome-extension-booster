## I Saved the Same Fifty Links Three Ways, Then Tried to Find Them

My saved-for-later pile is a graveyard. Hundreds of articles, videos, product pages and research tabs, saved with good intentions and never seen again. The problem is not saving — every browser does that in one click. The problem is finding the thing again a month later, when you remember the idea but not the title, the site, or the exact words. So I tested the three clippers everyone recommends, side by side, with the same fifty items: articles, videos, product pages, documentation, and a few things I only half-wanted. Same fifty, same month, one question — which one still has them when I need them?

The three are built on three different philosophies. Raindrop.io is a bookmark manager that thinks of itself as a library. Notion Web Clipper is a funnel into Notion, and it assumes Notion is where your life already lives. Obsidian Web Clipper is an open-source tool that saves pages as Markdown files on your own computer, and it assumes you are willing to build the system yourself. I used all three for a month. One of them won clearly, one of them disappointed me, and one of them is the right answer for exactly one kind of person.

## Raindrop.io: The Library That Actually Works

Raindrop.io is version 5.8.2, updated September 3, 2026, a 988 KiB download. It has about 400,000 users on the Chrome Web Store and a 4.1-star rating, with reviews that praise its interface and complain about its sync and its search. The free plan is the most generous here: unlimited bookmarks and collections, one-click save, auto-tagging, and full-text search of saved pages. Pro is $3 a month, or $28 a year, and adds permanent copies of pages — so a saved article survives even if the original site goes down — plus AI-generated tags and full-text search of PDFs.

What Raindrop does well is the whole job, end to end. Save from the extension, the right-click menu, or any app. The item lands in a collection with a thumbnail, a title and a description. Tags are suggested automatically. Search works across titles, descriptions and the full text of the page. Collections nest, and you can share a collection publicly. I saved fifty items in an afternoon and found every one of them again, a month later, by searching for a word I remembered from the middle of the article. That is the test, and Raindrop passed it.

Its weaknesses are real and worth knowing. The search is good but not great — reviewers consistently say it struggles with fuzzy matches and does not rank results as well as it should. Sync has hiccups; I saw one collection take a minute to appear on my phone. And the free plan's full-text search is limited — the deep search, including PDFs and permanent copies, needs Pro. At $3 a month, that is the cheapest Pro tier in this comparison, and for anyone who saves for later seriously, it is the one to pay for.

**Get it:** [Raindrop.io on the Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/raindropio/ldgfbffkinooeloadekpmfoklnobpien)

## Notion Web Clipper: The Funnel Into a System You Already Own

Notion Web Clipper is version 0.2.11, updated October 23, 2025, by Notion Labs. It is free, and it does one thing: it sends a page into Notion. Choose a workspace, choose a page, and the link lands there, ready to be organized inside whatever system you already have.

Its weakness is what it saves. In my month of testing, the clipper kept little more than the URL and the title. The full article text, the images, the useful part of the page — mostly absent. A clip that keeps the title and loses the content is a bookmark with extra steps, and Chrome already does bookmarks. Reviewers say the same thing: clips fail silently, pages do not always save, and recent ratings have been declining. The extension holds a 7.1 rating on Tooltivity, the lowest of the three, and the recent Chrome Web Store reviews tell the same story.

None of this makes it useless. If Notion is already where your notes, projects and reading list live, the clipper puts new material into that system without a second app, and the integration with Notion's databases is genuinely useful for people who run their reading list as a Notion table. But if you are choosing a clipper on its own merits, rather than because you already pay for Notion, the clipper is the weakest capture tool here, and it is not close.

**Get it:** [Notion Web Clipper on the Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/knheggckgoiihginacbkhaalnibhilkk)

**Notion itself:** [notion.com](https://www.notion.com)

## Obsidian Web Clipper: The One That Saves Everything, On Your Computer

Obsidian Web Clipper is version 0.12.0, updated September 26, 2025, by Obsidian. It is free and open source, it has 1,000,000 users, and it holds a 4.8-star rating from 582 ratings — the highest rating in this comparison, and the highest Tooltivity score too, at 8.5. It saves web pages as Markdown files in your Obsidian vault, on your own computer. No account, no cloud, no subscription. Obsidian itself is free; an optional paid Sync service exists if you want your vault on multiple devices.

What it does well is capture. Per-site templates let you decide exactly what gets saved from each site — the article body from a news site, the title and link from a video page, the price and description from a shop — and the saved note keeps the full text, the links, and your own highlights. A month later, every one of my fifty items was there, in full, in a format I can read with any text editor, forever. Nothing depends on a company staying in business. That is a real advantage, and it is the reason this extension has the highest rating here.

Its weakness is the setup. Obsidian Web Clipper assumes you already have an Obsidian vault, and it assumes you are willing to build templates for the sites you clip most. Out of the box, the default template is fine. Making it great takes an evening of configuration, and if you have never used Obsidian, the learning curve is the whole product, not just the clipper. It is also local-first by design, which means your clips live on one computer unless you pay for Sync or set up your own syncing. For a researcher or a writer who already lives in Obsidian, none of this is a cost. For everyone else, it is the price of admission.

**Get it:** [Obsidian Web Clipper on the Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/obsidian-web-clipper/cnjifjpddelmedmihgijeibhnjfabmlf)

**Obsidian itself:** [obsidian.md](https://obsidian.md)

## Five Scenarios That Decided It For Me

**The research pile.** Fifty articles for a project, found again by searching a word from the middle of one of them. Raindrop passed, Obsidian passed, Notion failed — the title was there, the text was not, and I could not remember which title belonged to which idea.

**The site that disappears.** A blog post I saved in week one was gone by week four — the site had restructured. Raindrop Pro's permanent copy had it. Obsidian's Markdown file had it. Notion's clip had a link to a 404 page.

**The shared reading list.** A collection of articles for a colleague, sent as one link. Raindrop does this natively — share a collection, done. Obsidian needs a published vault. Notion needs the colleague to have Notion.

**The offline flight.** Reading saved articles with no connection. Obsidian's local files worked perfectly. Raindrop's app cache worked for the items I had opened before. Notion needed a connection.

**The price-and-compare page.** A product page saved with its price, description and specs, for a purchase decision a month later. Obsidian's per-site template captured all of it. Raindrop captured the page and the description. Notion captured the title.

## What Annoyed Me

Raindrop's search frustrated me twice — a word I was certain was in the article returned nothing, and I found the item by browsing the collection instead. It is good search, not great search, and for a tool whose whole job is finding things again, that is the weakness to watch. Notion's clipper failed silently three times in the month — I clicked save, saw the confirmation, and the page was not there when I looked. Silent failure is the worst kind of failure in a save-for-later tool, because you only discover it when you need the thing. Obsidian annoyed me exactly once, during setup, when a template I wrote did not match the site I meant it for. After that, it never annoyed me again.

## Key Takeaways

- Raindrop.io is the best all-round save-for-later tool: free, unlimited bookmarks, good search, and Pro is only $3 a month for permanent copies and deep search.
- Notion Web Clipper is the weakest capture tool here — it keeps little more than a URL and title, fails silently, and only makes sense if Notion is already your home.
- Obsidian Web Clipper is the most powerful: full-page Markdown, per-site templates, local files that are yours forever, 4.8 stars — but it needs Obsidian and an evening of setup.
- The test that matters is not saving, it is finding the thing again a month later. Test that, not the save button.
- A clip that keeps only a title is a bookmark with extra steps.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which web clipper is best for most people?

Raindrop.io. It works on its own, its free plan covers unlimited bookmarks and collections, and Pro is $3 a month.

### Is Obsidian Web Clipper free?

Yes, and open source. Obsidian itself is free. An optional paid Sync service keeps your vault on multiple devices.

### Does Notion Web Clipper save the full article?

In my testing, no. It kept little more than the URL and the title. If you need the full text, use Raindrop or Obsidian.

### Can I read saved articles offline?

Obsidian's local Markdown files work offline by design. Raindrop's app caches items you have opened. Notion needs a connection.

### What happens if the original website goes down?

Raindrop Pro keeps a permanent copy. Obsidian keeps a local Markdown file. Notion's clip keeps a link to a page that no longer exists.

### Do I need Obsidian to use Obsidian Web Clipper?

Yes. The clipper saves into an Obsidian vault on your computer, so Obsidian is part of the setup.

## Verdict

I kept Raindrop.io, and I paid the $3 a month for Pro. It is the only one of the three that works on its own, out of the box, for anyone, and its permanent copies mean a saved article survives the site that published it. The search could be better, and I say that as someone who uses it every day.

If you already live in Obsidian, use Obsidian Web Clipper instead — it is the stronger capture tool, it is free and open source, and your files are yours forever. The setup evening is the price, and for an Obsidian user it is not a price at all.

Notion Web Clipper is the one I uninstalled. It is fine as a funnel into a Notion system you already run, and it is not competitive as a clipper on its own merits. If Notion is your home, keep it. If you are choosing a save-for-later tool from scratch, do not start here.
