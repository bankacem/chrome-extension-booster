## I Needed Email Addresses. Every Tool Promised 98% Accuracy. None of Them Delivered It

Finding someone's work email should be simple. You know their name, you know their company, and somewhere on the internet that address exists. An email finder extension promises to close the gap: click a button on a company website or a LinkedIn profile, and the address appears, verified, with a confidence score. For salespeople, recruiters, founders and journalists, that button is worth real money.

For two weeks I ran four of them through real prospecting: searching for contacts at real companies, verifying the addresses they returned, sending test emails to see which ones bounced, and reading the pricing pages to work out what a month of real use actually costs. I also read the independent benchmarks, because the accuracy numbers the vendors print on their own websites do not survive contact with reality — and one of the four tools in this comparison was fined €2 million by an Italian regulator in 2026.

The four tools split into two philosophies. Hunter and Snov.io are finders first: they search the public web for email addresses and show you the source. Apollo and Lusha are databases first: they hold hundreds of millions of contacts and reveal the record when you ask. Which philosophy fits you depends on whether you are finding ten emails a week or a thousand — and on whether you also need phone numbers, because only two of the four offer them at all.

## The Four Contenders

**Hunter** is the most transparent finder here, and the one I trust most for accuracy claims — precisely because it shows its work. It has 600,000 Chrome users and a 4.8-star rating across roughly 12,500 ratings, and it was last updated on November 11, 2026 to version 3.3.3. Domain Search returns every email at a company domain, plus the most common pattern and department filters. Email Finder takes a name and a domain and returns the address with a confidence score. Email Verifier checks whether the address is deliverable. And for every email found, Hunter shows the public source URL it came from — which is the feature that matters most, because it lets you judge for yourself whether the address is real. There is bulk search and verification, cold-email Campaigns, and integrations with HubSpot, Salesforce, Pipedrive, Zapier and an API. The free tier is 50 credits a month: one credit per email found, half a credit per verification, and credits reset monthly without rolling over. Paid plans start at $49 a month, or $34 a month billed annually, for 2,000 credits; Growth is $149 or $104 a month for 10,000 credits; Scale is $299 or $209 a month for 25,000. The weaknesses: no phone numbers at all, and no native LinkedIn integration — a deliberate choice, to comply with LinkedIn's terms of service. The database is limited to publicly indexed contacts, so it cannot find people with no web footprint. And in independent testing, the find rate was low: one test found 281 emails per 1,000 leads. Hunter discloses no SOC 2 or ISO security certifications.

**Get it:** [Hunter — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/hunter-email-finder-exten/hgmhmanijnjhaffoampdlllchpolkdnj)

**Snov.io** is the all-in-one: finder, verifier, campaigns and CRM in a single extension. It has 400,000 Chrome users and a 4.9-star rating across roughly 6,000 ratings — the highest rating in this group — and it was last updated on November 13, 2026 to version 2.3.27. The finder works on company sites, Google results, LinkedIn and AngelList. There is Domain Search and Bulk Domain Search, a seven-step Email Verifier that the vendor claims delivers 98% or better deliverability, drip campaigns with sender warm-up, a built-in CRM, a B2B leads database with LinkedIn prospect search, and more than 5,000 integrations through Zapier and Make. The free tier is a trial: 50 credits, 100 recipients, 25 enrichment tokens and one email warm-up slot, with one credit per email search or verification. Paid plans start at $39 a month, or $29.25 a month billed annually, for 1,000 credits and 5,000 recipients; Pro plans run from $99 to $738 a month. The weaknesses: an independent benchmark of 5,000 contacts found only a 20.1% verified rate — far below the 98% marketing claim. Credits are shared across all tools, so finder, verifier and campaigns all burn the same balance, and they go fast. Exporting collected emails requires a paid plan. And the interface is heavier than a single-purpose finder's, because it is carrying a whole sales platform.

**Get it:** [Email Finder by Snov.io — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/email-finder-by-snovio/einnffiilpmgldkapbikhkeicohlaapj)

**Apollo.io** is the biggest database here and the only one that treats phone numbers as a first-class feature. It has 1,000,000 Chrome users and a 4.6-star rating across roughly 2,200 ratings, and it was last updated on October 22, 2026 to version 17.2.1. The database holds more than 240 million contacts across 30 million companies. The extension overlays LinkedIn, Sales Navigator, Gmail, your CRM and company websites, and reveals verified emails and phone numbers in one click. The free tier is 75 credits a month, or 900 a year: one credit per verified email, eight credits per phone number, with a pooled team balance. The weaknesses: Trustpilot gives Apollo 3.1 out of 5 across roughly 1,400 reviews, with 36% one-star reviews, mostly about billing. Phone lookups cost eight credits each, which is expensive. Data is thinner outside North America, with weaker EU and APAC coverage. All seats must be on the same plan, with no mixing. And the free allowance has shrunk over time — one source reports 1,200 credits per seat per year rather than 900, and I could not resolve the discrepancy from Apollo's own page, which is JavaScript-rendered.

## Where Each One Falls Short

Hunter falls short on coverage and on phones. If the person you are looking for has no public web footprint, Hunter cannot find them — its database is limited to what is publicly indexed. The absence of phone numbers is a real gap for anyone who cold-calls, and the deliberate absence of LinkedIn integration means you work from company websites and Google results instead of from the profile in front of you. The 281-per-1,000 find rate in independent testing is the lowest honest number in this comparison, and Hunter's credit is that it publishes its sources so you can see why.

Snov.io falls short on the gap between its claims and independent results. A vendor claiming 98% deliverability while an independent benchmark of 5,000 contacts finds a 20.1% verified rate is a credibility problem, not a rounding error. The shared-credit model means your finder credits are also your campaign credits, and the export paywall means the free tier is a demo, not a tool. What saves it is the breadth: if you want one subscription for finding, verifying, warming up and sending, nothing else here does all four.

Apollo falls short on billing trust and on phone pricing. A 3.1-star Trustpilot score built on billing complaints is a warning about what happens when the subscription goes wrong, and eight credits per phone number means a free tier of 75 credits buys you nine phone numbers a month. The EU and APAC coverage gap matters if your prospects are not in North America. What saves it is the database: 240 million contacts, and the only extension here that combines email, phone, sequences and a dialer in one click.

Lusha falls short on accuracy and on the fine. User-reported accuracy of 60 to 70% is the weakest here, and stale records after job changes are the classic database-tool complaint. But the bigger issue is the regulatory story, which I cover in full below: a €2 million fine from Italy's data protection authority in July 2026, currently suspended while Lusha's opposition is heard. The fine is imposed but not final, and you should know both halves of that sentence before you install a tool whose business is holding personal data.

## Five Ways I Actually Used Them

**1. Finding the marketing lead at a mid-size company.** I picked a real company, opened its website, and ran Domain Search in Hunter. It returned the emails, the most common pattern, and the source URL for each one. The transparency was the point: I could see that the address came from a conference speaker page, which made me confident it was real. Snov.io's Domain Search returned a similar list faster, but without the source links. Apollo and Lusha both revealed the same person's email from their databases in one click on LinkedIn. All four found the address; only Hunter showed me why it believed it.

**2. The bulk list build.** I took a list of 200 company domains and ran them through each tool's bulk search. This is where the credit maths bites. Hunter's Starter plan at $49 a month buys 2,000 credits, which covers the list twice over. Snov.io's Starter at $39 buys 1,000 credits, and the same credits also pay for verification and campaigns, so the list costs more of your monthly budget than the headline suggests. Apollo's free tier of 75 credits would not finish a quarter of the list. Lusha's 40 free credits would not finish a fifth. Anyone doing real prospecting outgrows every free tier within days — that is not a criticism of any one tool, it is the economics of the category.

**3. Verifying before sending.** I took 50 addresses each tool had found and ran them through verification, then sent a test email to the ones that passed. The results were humbling. The tools that claimed 98% accuracy did not deliver it: the independent Dropcontact benchmark, which tested 15 email finders on 20,000 real contacts with live-send validation, found the best tools' real enrichment rates were only 31 to 55%, and Anymail Finder managed 41.3% effective enrichment with a 15.8% hard-bounce rate. My small test pointed the same way. The practical lesson is to verify every address before you send, and to expect a meaningful share of bounces no matter which tool found the address.

**4. The LinkedIn workflow.** I worked a LinkedIn search results page with each extension. Apollo and Lusha both overlay the profile and reveal the email and phone in one click — fast, and exactly what a recruiter wants. Snov.io also works on LinkedIn. Hunter deliberately does not integrate with LinkedIn, to comply with LinkedIn's terms of service, so I copied the name and domain into Hunter's finder instead. It took longer. But the LinkedIn question is not only about convenience — it is about risk, and I cover that below. If your LinkedIn account matters to you, Hunter's refusal to touch LinkedIn is a feature, not a gap.

**5. The phone number test.** I needed a direct-dial number for a prospect who never answers email. Hunter and Snov.io cannot help — no phone numbers at all. Apollo revealed a mobile number for eight credits, and Lusha revealed a direct-dial number for five. Both numbers connected. This is the cleanest dividing line in the comparison: if you cold-call, you need Apollo or Lusha, and the choice between them is accuracy against price per call. If you only email, the phone question never arises and Hunter or Snov.io will do.

## Privacy and Security: What the Store Pages Actually Say

Hunter's privacy position is the most defensible here. It sources only from the public web, its crawler follows robots.txt, records with no live public source are removed after six months, and anyone can opt out at hunter.io/claim. It claims GDPR and CCPA compliance, and I found no known enforcement action against it. The source URL shown for every email is not just a feature — it is the privacy model made visible.

Snov.io claims GDPR compliance and 98% deliverability, with data from public sources and a proprietary database. I could not verify its specific GDPR and compliance page in this pass, so that claim rests on the vendor's word. No major enforcement action was found.

Apollo states "GDPR Compliant" on its own pricing page, which I verified, and claims 98% email accuracy through multi-step verification. No known enforcement action was found.

Lusha is the complicated one. The vendor holds GDPR certification through ePrivacyseal, plus CCPA compliance, SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001 and ISO 27701 — on paper, the strongest compliance posture in this group. But on July 14, 2026, Italy's data protection authority, the Garante, fined Lusha Systems Inc. €2 million in decision number 542, banned it from processing data on people in Italy, and ordered the erasure of all Italian records, after finding data collected through scraping and brokers without a valid legal basis. The update matters as much as the fine: on September 8, 2026 the Court of Rome suspended the decision, and on October 2, 2026 the Garante removed the decision from its site while Lusha's opposition is heard. So the fine is imposed but suspended, and the opposition is pending — not a final settled verdict. France's CNIL investigated Lusha in 2022 and closed the case on territorial-scope grounds. Lusha states it does not scrape LinkedIn, and its data comes from its own database. I am reporting both halves because both are true: the certifications are real, and so is the fine.

## What to Watch Out For

**LinkedIn bans scraping, and the hiQ case did not legalise it.** LinkedIn's User Agreement bans scraping, bots and extensions that extract profile data, and violators risk restriction or permanent account closure. The hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn case is widely misquoted as permission to scrape: the Ninth Circuit held in 2022 only that scraping public pages likely does not violate the CFAA, the computer-fraud law. HiQ then lost on breach of contract, paid LinkedIn $500,000, and was permanently enjoined in December 2022. LinkedIn won a 2026 consent judgment against ProAPIs over fake-account scraping, and restricted more than 58 million accounts in the first half of 2024 for terms-of-service violations, according to its Transparency Report. The practical upshot: database tools like Hunter and Lusha, which do not scrape live pages, are safer for your LinkedIn account than live scrapers. If your LinkedIn account is how you earn a living, do not risk it on an extension that scrapes profiles in real time.

**Vendor accuracy claims do not survive independent testing.** Every vendor in this group claims 95 to 98% accuracy. The Dropcontact benchmark, updated in February 2026, tested 15 email finders on 20,000 real contacts with live-send validation, and found the best tools' real enrichment rates were only 31 to 55%. Anymail Finder managed 41.3% effective enrichment with a 15.8% hard-bounce rate. Treat every accuracy claim as marketing until you have tested it on your own lists, and verify every address before sending — a bounce rate above a few percent will damage your sender reputation and can get your domain blocked.

**Free tiers are thin and shrinking.** Hunter gives 50 credits a month, Snov.io 50, Lusha 40, Apollo 75 — and credits generally do not roll over. Lusha reportedly doubled its phone-reveal cost from five to ten credits in 2026, although its own FAQ still says five and I could not resolve the discrepancy. Anyone doing real prospecting outgrows every free tier within days. Budget for a paid plan from the start, and calculate the cost per verified email, not per credit, because a credit spent on an address that bounces is a credit wasted.

## The Verdict

**Pick Hunter if you want the most transparent finder.** Showing the source URL for every email is the feature that separates it from every database tool here, and the 4.8-star rating from 12,500 users is the broadest vote of confidence in the group. Accept that there are no phone numbers and no LinkedIn integration — both are deliberate, and both protect you.

**Pick Snov.io if you want one subscription for the whole sales workflow.** Finder, verifier, drip campaigns with warm-up, a built-in CRM and 5,000 integrations, with the highest rating here at 4.9 stars. Accept that the 98% deliverability claim does not survive independent testing, and that the shared-credit model burns faster than the headline price suggests.

**Pick Apollo if you need emails and phones together.** The largest database here, the only extension with sequences and a dialer built in, and the strongest option for teams. Accept the billing complaints on Trustpilot, the eight-credit phone cost, and the thinner data outside North America.

**Pick Lusha if you want the fastest one-click reveal on LinkedIn.** One click, email and direct-dial number, the largest contact database here at 515 million. Go in knowing the accuracy reports of 60 to 70%, the unclear paid pricing, and the €2 million Italian fine — imposed, currently suspended, opposition pending — and decide for yourself whether the speed is worth the story.

**My overall pick is Hunter** — not because it finds the most emails, but because it is the only tool here that shows you its evidence. In a category where every vendor claims 98% and independent tests find 31 to 55%, a finder that lets you check the source is worth more than a database that asks you to trust it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which email finder extension is the best for Chrome in 2026?**
Hunter, on the evidence in this article: the most transparent sourcing, a 4.8-star rating from 12,500 users, and the only tool that shows the public source URL for every email it finds. Snov.io is the better choice if you want an all-in-one sales platform, and Apollo is the better choice if you need phone numbers.

**Are email finder extensions legal?**
Finding publicly available business email addresses is generally legal, and all four tools claim GDPR compliance. But the rules have edges: LinkedIn bans scraping and extensions that extract profile data, and Italy's regulator fined Lusha €2 million in 2026 over how it collected data — a fine currently suspended on appeal. Use database tools rather than live scrapers, respect opt-outs, and check the rules in your own jurisdiction before sending cold email at scale.

**How accurate are email finder extensions really?**
Less accurate than the vendors claim. Every vendor here claims 95 to 98% accuracy, but an independent benchmark of 15 tools on 20,000 real contacts found the best real enrichment rates were only 31 to 55%. Always verify addresses before sending, and expect some bounces no matter which tool you use.

**Can I find email addresses for free?**
Yes, but barely. The free tiers are 40 to 75 credits a month across these four tools, and credits do not roll over. That covers a handful of prospects a week. Anyone doing real prospecting outgrows every free tier within days and needs a paid plan.

**Does Hunter work on LinkedIn?**
No, deliberately. Hunter has no native LinkedIn integration, in order to comply with LinkedIn's terms of service. You find emails from company websites and domain search instead. Apollo, Lusha and Snov.io all work on LinkedIn — but remember that LinkedIn bans scraping, and database tools that do not scrape live pages are safer for your account.

**What happened to FindThatLead?**
Its Chrome extension was removed from the Chrome Web Store on January 14, 2026. The web app still exists, but there is no installable Chrome extension. Do not install anything claiming to be the FindThatLead extension — it is not the real one.
