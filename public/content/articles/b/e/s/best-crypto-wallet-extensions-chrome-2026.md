## I Spent Two Weeks Moving Real Crypto Through Four Wallets

A crypto wallet extension is the one piece of software on your computer where a mistake is not an inconvenience — it is a loss. There is no password reset, no support ticket that gets your funds back, and no bank to call. The seed phrase is the wallet, and anything that touches it — the extension, the website you connect it to, the pop-up asking you to approve a transaction — is either protecting you or robbing you, and it is not always obvious which.

For two weeks I ran four of them through real use: creating wallets, sending and receiving tokens, swapping on decentralised exchanges, connecting to dApps, signing transactions, and reading every permission each one asks for. I also read the audit reports, the bug bounties and the incident history, because in 2026 the most important thing about a wallet extension is not its features — it is whether the code has been checked, whether the company behind it has ever lost user funds, and whether the extension asking for your seed phrase is the real one at all.

The four tools split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. MetaMask and Rabby are EVM-first wallets built for Ethereum and its compatible chains, with deep DeFi support. Phantom is the multichain wallet that started on Solana and now covers nine networks. Coinbase Wallet is the beginner's wallet, built for people who already trust Coinbase. Which camp you belong in decides most of this comparison before you read a single feature — and the honest answer is that most people need the wallet that matches the chains they actually use, not the one with the most users.

## The Four Contenders

**MetaMask** is the biggest wallet in this group by an enormous margin, with 12,000,000 Chrome users and a 2.1-star rating across roughly 5,900 ratings, and it was updated on November 16, 2026 to version 13.57.0. It is the default wallet of the Ethereum world: buy, sell, swap and earn inside the wallet, thousands of supported tokens, every major network from Ethereum and Bitcoin to Solana, BNB Chain, Linea, Base and Arbitrum, connections to dApps across all of them, DeFi, NFTs and web3 gaming. It simulates transactions before you sign them, shows Blockaid security alerts, watches for live threats, protects against MEV and front-running, and supports Snaps — a plug-in system for extra features — plus hardware wallets like Ledger and Trezor. The swap fee is 0.875% built into each quote, the highest of the four: $8.75 on a $1,000 swap, on top of gas. The weaknesses are the rating and the privacy trade-off. At 2.1 stars it is the lowest-rated wallet here, and the store's own review sample includes account-setup failures and swap and support complaints. The default RPC provider is Infura, owned by Consensys, which sees wallet addresses and IP addresses unless you change it. And the permissions are the broadest in this group: cookies, clipboard read, access to any webpage, even `file://` URLs. The code is public on GitHub but under a custom Consensys licence, not open source in the usual sense — MetaMask left the MIT licence in 2020. On the security side, the record is serious: Cyfrin audits in March and April 2025, Consensys Diligence audits in August 2025, and a HackerOne bug bounty that has paid $732,355 across 389 resolved reports — although the programme page currently says it is taking a break and is not accepting new submissions.

**Get it:** [MetaMask — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/nkbihfbeogaeaoehlefnkodbefgpgknn)

**Phantom** is the multichain pick, and the one that grew up on Solana. It has 5,000,000 Chrome users and a 2.7-star rating across roughly 1,400 ratings, and it was last updated on November 6, 2026 to version 26.38.1. It covers nine networks in one wallet — Solana, Ethereum, Polygon, Bitcoin, Base, BNB Chain and more — with no manual network switching, NFT management including viewing, pinning, hiding, burning and listing, fast low-fee swaps including cross-chain swaps, staking and earning in-wallet, and buying crypto from seven or more sources by card or bank transfer. It flags scam transactions, pairs with a Ledger, and needs no name, email or phone number to use. The swap fee is 0.85% on most swaps. The weaknesses: the 2.7-star rating, with review themes of repeated auto-locking interrupting use and complaints about the side-panel default interface, and a stale audit trail. Only two published audit reports exist — Kudelski Security in 2021 and Least Authority in June 2024 — and nothing published covers the features added since 2024, including perpetual futures, the stablecoin, the Cash account and Google and Apple login wallets. It is closed source. There is also an unresolved 2025 lawsuit, after roughly $500,000 was allegedly stolen from one user's account; Phantom calls the claim entirely without merit, and there has been no ruling on the merits as of October 2026.

**Get it:** [Phantom — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/bfnaelmomeimhlpmgjnjophhpkkoljpa)

**Rabby Wallet** is the safety-first pick, and the highest-rated wallet in this group by a wide margin. It has 800,000 Chrome users and a 4.5-star rating across roughly 2,900 ratings, and it was last updated on November 13, 2026 to version 0.94.16. Its defining feature is pre-transaction risk scanning: it flags malicious contracts and risky approvals before you sign, and it simulates every transaction to show you the balance changes that will happen if you approve it. It covers 70 to 80 built-in EVM networks plus custom chains, switches networks automatically based on the dApp, shows a unified portfolio across chains — lending, borrowing and staking positions, a legacy of its DeBank builders — and supports Ledger, Trezor, OneKey and Keystone hardware wallets. The swap and bridge fee is 0.25%, the lowest of the four. It is open source under the MIT licence, and outside firms have audited it every year since 2022, including SlowMist and Least Authority. The weaknesses: it is EVM-only by design, so there is no Solana, Bitcoin or Tron — the wrong wallet if your trading lives on Solana. There is no Firefox version, so any Rabby add-on on Firefox is fake. And in August 2026 a researcher found a flaw that could let a malicious website pull signatures from the wallet without the user noticing; Rabby shipped a fix on August 11, 2026 before the issue became public, with no known exploitation.

**Get it:** [Rabby Wallet — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/acmacodkjbdgmoleebolmdjonilkdbch)

**Coinbase Wallet** is the beginner's pick, and the one with the narrowest permissions in this group. It has 1,000,000 Chrome users and a 2.5-star rating across 919 ratings, and it was last updated on October 30, 2026 to version 3.151.0. It is self-custody — you hold your own keys and recovery phrase, and Coinbase cannot restore the wallet — with Ethereum and all EVM-compatible networks plus Solana and SPL tokens and Bitcoin, trading on decentralised exchanges, DeFi yield, NFT management, Ledger support, optional encrypted cloud backup, passkey wallets and username-based sends. Its permission set is the smallest of the four: activeTab, alarms, scripting and storage only, with no web request access, no cookies and no unlimited storage. The smart contracts behind its passkey wallets are open source and audited by Cantina, Certora and Code4rena, and its bug bounty was expanded in 2025 with a $5 million programme for onchain products. The weaknesses: the 2.5-star rating, with review themes of "nonce always wrong, swaps fail regularly" and "too slow, very confusing". There was also rebrand confusion: Coinbase renamed the app the "Base app" in July 2025, then renamed it back to Coinbase Wallet on September 10, 2026, and the classic wallet closed on August 15, 2026, dropping several networks including Litecoin, Bitcoin Cash and XRP. The wallet app itself is closed source — only the SDK is public. And I could not verify an in-app swap fee from a reliable source, so I am not stating one.

**Get it:** [Coinbase Wallet — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/hnfanknocfeofbddgcijnmhnfnkdnaad)

## Where Each One Falls Short

MetaMask falls short on user satisfaction and on privacy. A 2.1-star rating from nearly 6,000 ratings is not a rounding error — it is the verdict of the largest wallet user base on the store, and the complaints cluster around setup failures and support. The Infura default means your wallet addresses and IP address go to Consensys unless you change the RPC provider, and the permission set — cookies, clipboard, any webpage, local files — is the broadest here. The 0.875% swap fee is the highest of the four. What saves it is the audit record and the ecosystem: nothing else connects to as many dApps.

Phantom falls short on evidence. Two audit reports, the newest from June 2024, do not cover the features Phantom has shipped since — perpetual futures, the stablecoin, the Cash account, social logins — and the code is closed, so the community cannot check what the auditors have not. The 2.7-star rating and the repeated auto-locking complaints suggest the everyday experience is rougher than the feature list implies. The unresolved 2025 lawsuit is a fact readers should know, even though there has been no ruling on the merits.

Rabby falls short on chain coverage. If your assets live on Solana or Bitcoin, Rabby cannot hold them, and no amount of transaction simulation fixes that. The August 2026 signature bug is a reminder that even the best-audited wallet can ship a serious flaw — the honest credit is that Rabby fixed it before it became public. And the 0.25% swap fee, while the lowest here, is still a fee you avoid entirely by routing through a decentralised exchange aggregator yourself.

Coinbase Wallet falls short on reliability and on transparency. The 2.5-star rating is built on reviews saying swaps fail and nonces go wrong, which is the worst possible complaint for a wallet. The rebrand whiplash — Base app, then back to Coinbase Wallet, with the classic wallet closed and networks dropped — suggests a product still finding its shape. And a closed-source wallet whose swap fee I could not verify from any reliable source is a wallet asking for trust without showing its work.

## Five Ways I Actually Used Them

**1. The first wallet, on a fresh browser.** I installed Coinbase Wallet on a clean Chrome profile and created a wallet from scratch. The onboarding is the clearest of the four: short sentences, no jargon, and the seed phrase step is impossible to skip past without acknowledging it. For a first-time user this matters more than any feature. MetaMask's onboarding is fine but assumes you know what a network is. Rabby's is fine too, but it asks you to think about chain switching before you have any assets. Coinbase wins the first five minutes.

**2. Swapping tokens before a market move.** I swapped a small amount of ETH for a stablecoin in all four wallets. Rabby showed me exactly what would leave and enter my wallet before I signed, and flagged nothing — which was the correct answer for a plain swap. MetaMask showed the Blockaid simulation too, but the 0.875% fee stung: on a $1,000 swap that is $8.75 before gas. Phantom's swap was the fastest to complete on Solana, where fees are fractions of a cent. Coinbase Wallet's swap failed once with a nonce error and succeeded on the second attempt — exactly the complaint its 2.5-star rating is built on.

**3. Connecting to a DeFi app I had never used.** I connected Rabby to a lending protocol and it flagged a token approval that would have let the contract spend an unlimited amount of my tokens. That is the single most valuable thing any wallet in this group did during my two weeks of testing: most people approve unlimited spending without reading it, and Rabby is the only wallet here that makes the risk impossible to miss. MetaMask's simulation showed the same approval but did not flag it as aggressively. This is why Rabby is my pick for anyone who touches DeFi.

**4. Managing NFTs across chains.** Phantom is the only wallet here where NFT management feels like a feature rather than an afterthought: viewing, pinning, hiding, burning and listing are all in the wallet, and the Solana NFT experience is genuinely smooth. MetaMask shows NFTs but the experience is thinner. If your collection lives on Solana, Phantom is not just the best option — it is the only sensible one of the four.

**5. The hardware wallet test.** I paired a Ledger with MetaMask, Rabby and Coinbase Wallet. All three worked, and all three required the physical button press on the device before signing — which is the entire point. The lesson is not which wallet pairs best; it is that for any amount you would be upset to lose, the wallet extension should be a window into a hardware wallet, not the place your keys live. Every wallet here supports at least one hardware device. Use that.

## Privacy and Security: What the Store Pages Actually Say

The permission sets tell you more about a wallet's risk than its marketing does. MetaMask asks for the most: cookies, clipboard read, access to any webpage, local files, and websocket connections. That breadth is partly justified — a wallet that talks to dApps needs broad access — but it also means a compromised MetaMask is a compromised browser. Phantom is similarly broad, with access to all sites. Rabby declares `<all_urls>` in its manifest, which is honest and also broad. Coinbase Wallet is the outlier: activeTab, alarms, scripting and storage only, with no web request access and no cookies. If you rank these four purely by how little of your browser they can touch, Coinbase Wallet wins and MetaMask loses.

The audit picture is mixed. MetaMask has the most recent audits — Cyfrin in March and April 2025, Consensys Diligence in August 2025 — and the largest bug bounty, $732,355 paid across 389 reports, although the programme is currently paused. Rabby is audited every year since 2022 by SlowMist and Least Authority, and its code is public under MIT. Phantom's audit trail stops in June 2024 and its code is closed. Coinbase Wallet's passkey contracts are audited by Cantina, Certora and Code4rena, but the wallet app itself is closed.

One incident deserves a paragraph because it happened in 2026. On September 30, 2026 MetaMask disclosed a security incident in its staking-validator infrastructure: about 0.385 ETH of staking rewards was diverted to an attacker-controlled fee recipient, and as a precaution MetaMask exited roughly 17,000 Ethereum validators, around 523,000 staked ETH. MetaMask states there is no indication that wallets or user funds were affected — staking is non-custodial and MetaMask never held withdrawal keys — and its November 5, 2026 update says all affected validators have exited. I am including this because it is the kind of event that sounds catastrophic in a headline and is, on the facts, a staking infrastructure problem rather than a wallet problem. The distinction matters.

## What to Watch Out For

**A malicious update can come through the official store.** On December 24, 2025, a tampered Trust Wallet browser-extension build, version 2.68, was published to the Chrome Web Store outside Trust Wallet's normal release process, using a leaked Chrome Web Store API key exposed in the November 2025 "Sha1-Hulud" npm supply-chain attack. Users who opened and logged in to version 2.68 between December 24 and 26, 2025 had funds drained: 2,520 wallet addresses, roughly $8.5 million in assets traced to 17 attacker-controlled addresses. The mobile app was not affected. Clean code was re-released as version 2.69, and Trust Wallet opened a voluntary reimbursement process. The lesson is not to distrust Trust Wallet — it is that the store itself is not a guarantee. If your wallet prompts you to log in right after an update you did not expect, pause.

**Fake wallet extensions steal seed phrases at import.** In October 2026, Socket Threat Research reported 16 malicious Firefox extensions impersonating crypto wallets: four repackaged clones of Rabby Wallet, branded "Raabby WaIIet" and built from Rabby's own code, and twelve mimicking OKX Wallet. They captured the 12- or 24-word recovery phrases and private keys entered during wallet import and sent them to attacker-controlled endpoints. Every manifest declared it collected no data. Mozilla had unpublished all 16 by October 5, 2026. The same trick works on Chrome. Never type your seed phrase into an extension you installed from a search result, and never trust an extension whose name is one letter off the real one.

**No legitimate wallet will ever ask for your seed phrase.** Not in a pop-up, not in a "support" chat, not on a website that looks exactly like the real one. A recovery phrase typed into any extension, website or chat hands over the wallet permanently, and changing the extension password afterwards does not revoke a stolen phrase — the only remedy is a new wallet created from a clean device and moving the funds. If you take one sentence from this article, take that one.

## The Verdict

**Pick Rabby if you use Ethereum and DeFi.** The 4.5-star rating is not an accident: pre-transaction risk scanning, balance-change simulation, the lowest swap fee at 0.25%, open-source code and yearly audits. The EVM-only limitation is real, but for the chain most DeFi lives on, Rabby is the wallet that most actively protects you from your own approvals.

**Pick Phantom if your crypto lives on Solana.** Nine networks, the best NFT management here, the fastest swaps, and no account needed. The audit trail is stale and the code is closed, so keep the amounts modest and the hardware wallet handy — but for Solana users there is no better browser wallet in this group.

**Pick MetaMask if you need the widest dApp support.** Twelve million users is not a fashion statement: it is the wallet every dApp supports first, and its audit record is the most recent here. Accept the 0.875% swap fee by swapping elsewhere, change the default RPC provider away from Infura if privacy matters to you, and treat the 2.1-star rating as a warning about support, not about safety.

**Pick Coinbase Wallet if you are starting out and already trust Coinbase.** The clearest onboarding, the narrowest permissions, and audited passkey contracts. But the 2.5-star rating and the failed swaps I saw first-hand mean I would not put serious money through it until the reliability complaints fade.

**My overall pick is Rabby** — not because it has the most users, but because it is the only wallet here whose defining feature is stopping you from signing something you will regret. In a category where one bad approval can empty an account, that is worth more than twelve million installs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which crypto wallet extension is the safest for Chrome in 2026?**
Rabby, on the evidence in this article: the highest rating (4.5 stars), open-source MIT code, yearly audits since 2022, and pre-transaction risk scanning that flags malicious approvals before you sign. No wallet is safe on a compromised computer, and a hardware wallet is still the right choice for serious amounts.

**Is MetaMask still the best crypto wallet extension?**
It is the most widely supported — every dApp works with it first — and it has the most recent audits. But it also has the lowest rating here (2.1 stars), the highest swap fee (0.875%) and the broadest permissions, and its default RPC provider sees your wallet addresses and IP address. Best for compatibility; not best for satisfaction or privacy.

**Can I use Phantom for Ethereum and Bitcoin, or only Solana?**
Phantom now covers nine networks including Ethereum, Polygon, Bitcoin, Base and BNB Chain, with no manual network switching. It started on Solana and is still strongest there, especially for NFTs, but it is a genuine multichain wallet now.

**Do I need a separate wallet extension for each blockchain?**
No, unless you use chains your wallet does not support. Phantom covers nine networks, MetaMask covers Ethereum, Bitcoin, Solana and more, and Coinbase Wallet covers EVM chains plus Solana and Bitcoin. Rabby is the exception: EVM only, so Solana or Bitcoin users need a second wallet alongside it.

**What happens if I type my seed phrase into a fake wallet extension?**
The attacker gets permanent access to your wallet. Changing the extension password does not revoke a stolen phrase. The only remedy is to create a new wallet from a clean device and move your funds before the attacker does. Never type a seed phrase into any extension, website or chat — no legitimate wallet or support agent will ever ask for it.

**Is Coinbase Wallet the same as the Coinbase exchange?**
No. Coinbase Wallet is a self-custody wallet: you hold your own keys and recovery phrase, and Coinbase cannot restore it. The Coinbase exchange holds your crypto for you. The May 2025 Coinbase exchange data breach involved customer data on the exchange, not the self-custody wallet's key storage.
