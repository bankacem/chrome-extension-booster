## I Spent Three Weeks Editing PDFs in Chrome

I have a rule about PDFs: if I have to print a document to sign it, the tool has failed. I broke that rule twice last year — once for a freelance contract I signed, scanned, and emailed back like it was 2009, and once for a tax form I filled in by hand because the online version would not let me type in the boxes. Both times the problem was the same. The PDF was fine. My tools were not.

So for three weeks I ran four PDF editor extensions through my real working life: a freelance contract that needed a signature, a stack of forms, a 120-page scanned report that needed searchable text, a research paper I had to mark up, and a board pack assembled from six separate PDFs. I counted what the free plans actually let me do, timed how long the cloud tools took to upload and return a file, and watched where each one asked for money.

The four tools split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. Smallpdf, Kami and Lumin are cloud tools that live in your browser: you open a PDF, your file goes to their servers, and the edited version comes back. PDFgear is the opposite — its Chrome extension is a thin launcher, and the real editor is a free desktop app that does most of its work on your own machine. That difference decides almost everything else in this comparison: price, privacy, speed, and what happens when your internet drops.

## The Four Contenders

**Smallpdf** is the biggest name in browser PDF tools, and its Chrome extension has about 3 million users with a 4.2-star rating across roughly 3,000 reviews. It is a Swiss company, founded in Zurich in 2013, and the extension is the front door to a suite of more than 20 web tools: edit, convert, compress, merge, split, sign, OCR, and an AI assistant that can summarise a document. In testing it was the fastest tool to learn — I opened a PDF, clicked Edit, and was typing in it within a minute, with no tutorial and no account needed for the first file. The free plan gives you 2 tasks a day, and that counter is shared across every tool in the suite: convert a file in the morning and compress it after lunch, and you are done until tomorrow. The Pro plan costs about $9 to $10 a month on annual billing, or $15 a month if you pay monthly, with a 7-day free trial. One thing I liked immediately: Smallpdf says uploaded files are deleted from its servers within an hour of processing, and that policy is stated plainly on its site rather than buried in a PDF of its own.

**Get it:** [Smallpdf — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/smallpdf%E2%80%94edit-convert-com/ohfgljdgelakfkefopgklcohadegdpjf)

**Kami** is the classroom giant of this group — roughly 18 million users, and the only tool here built for teachers first and everyone else second. It turns any PDF into an interactive document you can highlight, draw on, type on, and comment on with text, voice, or video, and it plugs straight into Google Classroom, Canvas, and Schoology, saving back to Google Drive or OneDrive. Its store rating tells a more complicated story than its size: 3.8 stars across about 8,400 ratings, down from a 4.6-star lifetime average, with the most recent 100 reviews averaging around 3.8 as well. The free Basic plan covers about 10 essential tools, and it includes OCR — genuinely unusual, since most tools in this category charge for it. The Teacher plan costs $149 a year, or $14.99 a month, with a 30-day trial, and there is a separate Pro plan at $85 a year for people who are not teachers. What Kami does that none of the others attempt is accessibility: text-to-speech, translation, and dyslexia-friendly fonts sit on top of any document, though text-to-speech is a paid feature.

**Get it:** [Kami — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/kami-for-google-chrome/ecnphlgnajanjnkcmbpancdjoidceilk)

**Lumin PDF** is the Google Workspace native of this group, built in Christchurch, New Zealand, by a company that reported passing 100 million users across its products in late 2024. The extension itself is the weakest-rated tool here by a clear margin: 3.0 stars across about 9,000 ratings on the Chrome Web Store. But the raw rating hides a real strength. Lumin is the only one of these four where editing the actual text of a PDF, creating fillable form fields, and redacting sensitive passages are core features rather than afterthoughts, and it syncs finished documents straight back to Google Drive. The free plan lets you complete 3 documents a month — a document counts as completed once you share, download, print, or sync it. Paid plans start at $9 a month, with the Pro tier at $19 a month adding full text editing and document restructuring. The extension is a substantial download at 29.68 MB, works in 15 languages, and was updated to version 6.7.0 on 30 October 2026.

**Get it:** [Lumin — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/lumin-pdf-editor-edit-sig/dbkidnlfklnjanneifjjojofckpcogcl)

**PDFgear** is the odd one out, and I mean that as a compliment. The Chrome extension itself is tiny — 44.93 KB, version 1.0, last updated 14 August 2025 — with only about 9,000 users and a 5.0-star average across just 9 ratings, which is far too small a sample to mean anything. The extension does not really edit PDFs at all: it opens PDFgear's online tools in your browser. The real product is the free desktop app for Windows and Mac, and it is the most generous free PDF editor I have ever tested. Everything is unlocked: direct text editing, OCR, batch conversion to and from more than 30 formats, annotations, signatures, form filling, redaction, and AES-256 encryption — no account, no watermark, no ads, no daily quota, no file-size cap. Most of it runs offline on your own machine; only the AI features and some advanced conversions send files to PDFgear's servers. The catch, and the company says this itself, is that the free-for-now model is funded by investment, and it may eventually charge for AI tools, high-accuracy OCR, and heavy server-side conversions. There is no timeline for that, and no paid plan exists today.

**Get it:** [PDFgear — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/pdfgear-pdf-read-edit-con/loeihpckhhjnallmllkbhmlccgoglgei)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Smallpdf's weakness is the free plan, and it is not close. Two tasks a day sounds manageable until you realise a single real job is usually two or three tasks: convert, then edit, then compress. I hit the wall on the second day of testing, mid-contract, and had to wait until the next morning or pay. The paid plan is also the most expensive per month of the three cloud tools here at $15 on monthly billing. And one feature people assume is included is not: editing the existing text of a PDF — changing a sentence rather than adding a text box on top of it — needs Pro. Reviewers also raise a recurring complaint worth knowing about: several report difficulty cancelling subscriptions and getting refunds after being charged, so if you take the 7-day trial, set a reminder to cancel before it ends.

Kami's weakness is that it is a classroom tool wearing a general-purpose editor's clothes. Outside Google Classroom, half of what makes it special — assigning work, collecting student submissions, grading — simply does not apply, and you are left with an annotator that is very good at marking up worksheets and only average at everything else. The falling rating is the other warning sign: a 4.6-star lifetime average sliding to about 3.8 in recent reviews, with the most-criticised theme being that the extension stops working or loses state. I did not lose work in my testing, but I did have the extension fail to load a 40 MB file on the first attempt and succeed on the second, which is the kind of flakiness that costs you a deadline. And the pricing is confusing — the Teacher plan is $149 a year, the Pro plan is $85 a year, and the company's own pages have shown different figures in different places, so confirm the price at checkout.

Lumin's weakness is the gap between what it promises and what its users say actually happens. A 3.0-star rating across 9,000 ratings is not a rounding error; it is a verdict, and the recurring complaints are specific: editing happens online rather than locally and may require an account or a subscription, saving or exporting changes can fail, and working with form fields is fiddly. I ran into the form-field problem myself — positioning a text box precisely inside a small form field took three attempts. The free plan is also the tightest of the three cloud tools in a different way: 3 completed documents a month means three finished files, and if you open a document, annotate it, and download it, that is one of your three gone. For anyone who touches PDFs daily, that is a trial, not a plan.

PDFgear's weakness is the browser experience itself. Judged purely as a Chrome extension, it is the weakest tool here: a 45 KB launcher that hands you off to web tools, with no real editing inside the browser at all. The online tools are slower than the desktop app — independent testing has found the web version sluggish and missing text editing — so if you install the extension expecting to edit a PDF in a tab, you will be disappointed. The other weakness is the business model: "free forever" is a promise, not a contract, and the company has said openly that advanced features may become paid. There is also a trust question to settle honestly. PDFgear faced public accusations about its software's behaviour, and in response it hired Cure53, an independent security firm, to examine the flagged components; the report, published 19 March 2026, concluded that no malicious functionality was identified. I installed it only from pdfgear.com, and I would not install it from anywhere else.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The contract that needed a signature.** A freelance contract arrived as a PDF on a Tuesday morning, and it needed my signature and a filled-in payment section by noon. Lumin was the smoothest here: I opened it, typed into the form fields, drew my signature, and sent it back without leaving the browser. Smallpdf did the same job in about the same time, but the signature and the form fill counted as two of my two daily tasks, so the contract used up my entire free day. Kami cannot do this job properly — it annotates on top of a document rather than filling its fields. PDFgear's desktop app handled it perfectly, but it meant downloading the file, opening a separate app, and uploading the finished version myself. For a one-off signature in a browser tab, Lumin wins; for a signature you do every week, the desktop detour stops mattering.

**The 120-page scanned report.** A client sent me a scanned report — 120 pages of images, no selectable text, no search. This is where the free plans separate brutally. Kami's free plan includes OCR, and it turned the scan into a readable, searchable PDF without asking me to pay. Smallpdf's OCR is a Pro feature, so the free plan could not do it at all. Lumin can search scanned text, but only on a paid plan, and the job would have used one of my 3 free documents for the month. PDFgear's desktop app ran OCR on the whole file locally, for free, and it was the fastest of the four at it. If your work involves scans, Kami and PDFgear are the only free options here, and PDFgear is the faster one.

**The board pack from six PDFs.** I had six separate PDFs — agenda, minutes, budget, two reports, and a cover sheet — that needed to become one document in the right order. Smallpdf merged them beautifully, and then told me I had used my tasks for the day. Merging six files and then compressing the result is two tasks, so a single board pack can end your free day before lunch. Lumin merged them too, and it cost one of my 3 monthly documents. Kami does not merge at all. PDFgear did the merge, the page reorder, and the compression in one sitting, offline, for free, with no counter anywhere. This is the scenario that made me keep PDFgear installed: the day you need to assemble a document is the day the free cloud plans run out.

**The research paper I had to mark up.** I annotated a 30-page research paper with highlights, margin notes, and a few hand-drawn arrows, then shared it with a colleague. Kami was the best tool for this by a clear margin: the highlight colours, the comment threads, the voice note I left on one page, and the way it saved back to Google Drive without me thinking about it. Smallpdf's annotation is fine but basic — highlight, draw, add a text box — and the file went through its cloud. Lumin annotated well but the document counted against my monthly three. PDFgear's desktop annotation is capable, but sharing a marked-up file meant exporting and emailing it myself, while Kami's Drive save meant my colleague saw it the moment I finished.

**The sensitive document.** A document with client financial details needed a paragraph redacted and a password added before I could share it. I did not want it on anyone's server, so the three cloud tools were out before I started — Smallpdf, Kami and Lumin all process your file on their servers. PDFgear's desktop app redacted the passage and set the password locally, and the file never left my machine. Lumin does have a redaction tool, and Smallpdf can password-protect a file, but both require uploading the document first. If the document is confidential, the answer is not a better cloud tool; it is a local one.

## What to Watch Out For: Permissions and Privacy

A PDF editor is a tool you hand your documents to, and you should treat it like one. Three of these four tools process your files on their servers — that is how cloud editing works — which means your contract, your tax form, or your medical letter passes through someone else's infrastructure before it comes back to you. Smallpdf is the most transparent of the three: it says uploaded files are deleted from its servers within an hour of processing, and its Swiss base puts it under GDPR and Switzerland's own data protection law. Kami and Lumin do not state a deletion window as plainly, and Lumin's Chrome Web Store privacy disclosure is the broadest here: it declares that it handles personally identifiable information, location, web history, user activity, and website content. Read that list twice before you install it.

The permissions matter too. Kami's extension requests access to all URLs, which is how it can open PDFs wherever they appear in your browser — and also how it can see a great deal of your browsing. That is not evidence of wrongdoing; it is the price of the feature, and you should know you are paying it.

Two practical rules I follow with all four. First, never upload a document you would not be comfortable explaining to the person it is about — if it is genuinely sensitive, use a local tool like PDFgear's desktop app, or redact it before it leaves your machine. Second, install PDF tools only from the Chrome Web Store or the vendor's own site. PDFgear's own guidance is explicit on this point: install only from pdfgear.com or the official app stores, because copycat downloads are where the real risk lives.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If you already live in Adobe's world, the official Adobe Acrobat extension is the incumbent nobody in this comparison beats on raw reach — it is one of the most-installed PDF extensions in the store, and it opens, comments on, and converts PDFs without leaving Chrome. But editing real text, converting files, and most of the serious tools sit behind a paid Acrobat subscription, and the free tier is mostly a reader with benefits. It is the safe default if your employer already pays for Acrobat, and a poor reason to pay if they do not.

Sejda is the quiet alternative I keep coming back to for quick web jobs. Its free tier allows 3 tasks per hour, files up to 50 MB, and documents up to 200 pages, and it edits real text directly in the browser — the one thing Smallpdf charges for and Kami cannot do at all. The paid Desktop+Web plan costs $63 a year, and there is a $5 week pass if you need a single heavy week. Sejda has no Chrome extension of its own, which is why it is not one of the four contenders here, but as a website it is the fastest way I know to fix a typo in a PDF without installing anything.

## Key Takeaways

- Smallpdf is the best all-round PDF editor extension for Chrome: the cleanest interface, more than 20 tools, 3 million users, and Pro at about $9 to $10 a month on annual billing — but the free plan stops at 2 tasks a day, and editing real text needs Pro.
- Kami is the best choice for teachers and students: the only free OCR here, voice and video comments, and deep Google Classroom integration — but it is an annotator, not an editor, and its 3.8-star rating has slid from a 4.6 lifetime average.
- Lumin PDF is the best choice for filling in forms and signing documents in Google Workspace: real text editing, fillable fields, and Drive sync — but it has the lowest rating here at 3.0 stars, and the free plan covers only 3 finished documents a month.
- PDFgear is the best free PDF editor, full stop: the desktop app does everything the others charge for, offline, with no watermark and no account — but the Chrome extension itself is just a launcher, and the free model may not last forever.
- If a document is confidential, do not upload it to a cloud editor. Use a local tool.
- Never install a PDF tool from anywhere except the Chrome Web Store or the vendor's own site.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which PDF editor extension for Chrome is actually free?

PDFgear, by a distance. Its desktop app is completely free with no account, no watermark, no ads, and no daily limit, and it includes text editing, OCR, and batch conversion. Among the browser-only tools, Kami's free Basic plan is the most useful because it includes OCR, while Smallpdf's free plan stops at 2 tasks a day and Lumin's at 3 finished documents a month.

### Can I edit the actual text of a PDF in Chrome for free?

Yes, but not with the big names. PDFgear's free desktop app edits text directly, and Sejda's free web tier lets you edit text in the browser with a limit of 3 tasks an hour. Smallpdf charges for text editing — it is a Pro feature — Kami cannot edit existing text at all, and Lumin puts it on paid plans.

### Is Smallpdf really limited to 2 tasks a day on the free plan?

Yes. The free plan allows 2 tasks a day across the whole suite, and the counter is shared between tools, so converting a file and then compressing it uses both tasks. The limit resets daily, and the Pro plan removes it for about $9 to $10 a month on annual billing or $15 a month on monthly billing.

### Which PDF extension is best for teachers and students?

Kami. It is built for classrooms: it plugs into Google Classroom, Canvas, and Schoology, saves to Google Drive and OneDrive, and lets teachers leave voice and video comments on student work. Its free plan includes OCR, which no other free tool here offers. If you are not a teacher, most of what makes Kami special does not apply to you.

### Is it safe to upload my PDFs to these online editors?

It depends on the document. Smallpdf deletes uploaded files within an hour and is based in Switzerland under GDPR. Kami and Lumin do not state a deletion window as plainly, and Lumin's store listing discloses that it handles personal information, location, web history, and user activity. For anything confidential, use a local editor like PDFgear's desktop app, where the file never leaves your machine.

### Does PDFgear really have no paid plan?

That is correct as of November 2026: there is no paid version of the desktop app, no subscription, and no watermark on saved files. The company has said openly that it may eventually charge for AI tools, high-accuracy OCR, and heavy server-side conversions, with advance notice before anything changes. The core editing tools are promised to stay free, but free today is not a contract for free forever.

## Verdict

Install **Smallpdf** if you want one PDF tool in your browser that does nearly everything and you are willing to pay for it. It has the cleanest interface of the four, the widest set of tools, and the most transparent privacy policy. The 2-tasks-a-day free plan is a taster, not a working tool, so budget for Pro at about $9 to $10 a month on annual billing — and set a calendar reminder if you take the 7-day trial, because cancelling is the complaint reviewers raise most.

Install **Kami** if you teach, study, or mark up documents for other people. Free OCR, voice and video comments, and the best Classroom integration anywhere make it the only real choice for education, even with a rating that has slipped to 3.8 stars. Accept that it annotates rather than edits, and that it is a classroom tool first.

Install **Lumin PDF** if you live in Google Workspace and your daily PDF work is forms and signatures. Real text editing, fillable fields, and documents that sync straight back to Drive are genuinely useful — but go in knowing the 3.0-star rating, the 3-document monthly free limit, and the form-field fiddliness I ran into myself.

And install **PDFgear** if you want the most PDF editing for no money. Its desktop app does everything the other three charge for — text editing, OCR, batch conversion, redaction — offline, with no watermark and no account. The Chrome extension is only a launcher, so judge it on the desktop app, not the extension, and install it only from pdfgear.com.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep Smallpdf in the browser and PDFgear on the desktop — the first for the two-minute jobs, the second for the day the free plans run out. If I could only keep one of the two, it would be PDFgear, because the tool that never asks for money is the one that is still there when the deadline lands.
