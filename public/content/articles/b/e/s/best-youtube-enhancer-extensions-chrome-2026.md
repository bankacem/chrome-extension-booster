## I Spent Two Weeks Watching YouTube With Four Enhancers Running

I watch about two hours of YouTube a day. Tutorials, documentaries, music, the occasional rabbit hole I did not plan. Over a year that is more than 700 hours, and a surprising amount of it is not the video: it is sponsor reads, intros, subscribe reminders, end screens, and a player that keeps resetting my volume and quality. That is the honest use case for a YouTube enhancer: not a hack, but the daily work of making the player behave the way you want it to.

For two weeks I ran four of them through my actual viewing: skipping sponsor segments, checking dislike counts before I committed to a tutorial, setting default quality and playback speed, taking screenshots of frames, and watching how each one behaved when YouTube changed its layout underneath it. I also watched what each one costs — all four are free — and what each one does with your data, because in 2026 one of these tools has a privacy problem worth knowing about.

The four tools split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. Return YouTube Dislike and SponsorBlock each do one thing brilliantly and nothing else. Enhancer for YouTube and Magic Actions try to do everything. Which camp you belong in decides most of this comparison before you read a single feature — and the honest answer is that most people need one from each camp.

## The Four Contenders

**Return YouTube Dislike** is the biggest tool in this group, with 5,000,000 Chrome users and a 4.8-star rating across roughly 24,900 ratings, and it was updated on November 7, 2026 to version 4.1.7. It does exactly one thing: it puts the dislike count back beside the Like button, where YouTube removed it in December 2021, and it shows a like-to-dislike ratio bar under the video. It works from archived pre-December 2021 data covering roughly a billion videos, plus live estimates collected from the extension's own users voting as they watch. It is free and open source under the GPL, funded by voluntary contributions — the store listing flags in-app purchases, but those are donations, not a paywall. The weakness is accuracy: anything posted after December 2021 is an estimate, and for unpopular or brand-new videos the estimate is noticeably less reliable. It is also single-purpose by design, so it must be paired with another enhancer if you want player controls. One flag: a screenshot dated October 15, 2026 showed this same listing at 2.5 stars and 19,900 ratings, while the live listing today shows 4.8 stars and 24,900 ratings. That is an unresolved discrepancy, and it is worth re-checking before you trust either number completely.

**Get it:** [Return YouTube Dislike — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/return-youtube-dislike/gebbhagfogifgggkldgodflihgfeippi)

**SponsorBlock for YouTube — Skip Sponsorships** is the time-saver, and the one that changed my viewing the most. It has 2,000,000 Chrome users and a 4.5-star rating across roughly 3,600 ratings, and it was last updated on June 26, 2026 to version 6.1.6. It crowdsources the boring parts: users submit timestamps for sponsor reads, intros, outros, subscribe reminders and non-music sections of music videos, and everyone else's player skips them automatically, with coloured markers on the timeline showing what was skipped. Lookups are privacy-preserving, and it also works with Invidious. It is free and open source under the GPL-3.0 licence, funded by donations, with no in-app purchases. The weaknesses: it depends entirely on crowd coverage, so new or small-channel videos often have no segments marked at all; YouTube's server-side ad injection can shift video timestamps and desync the skips; and it is not an ad blocker — it skips the sponsor read inside the video, not YouTube's own ads, which is a distinction people get wrong constantly.

**Get it:** [SponsorBlock — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/sponsorblock-for-youtube/mnjggcdmjocbbbhaepdhchncahnbgone)

**Enhancer for YouTube™** is the player-control pick, and the most polished tool here. It has 1,000,000 Chrome users and a 4.5-star rating across roughly 17,600 ratings, and it was last updated on July 15, 2026 to version 3.0.19. It is free, with no paid tier and no in-app purchases anywhere on its listing. The feature list is the longest in this group for pure player control: default playback speed from 0.07x to 16x with mouse-wheel and keyboard shortcuts, Auto HD and default quality for videos, playlists and embeds, volume boost up to 10x, cinema mode, custom themes, video filters, loop, screenshots, and the ability to hide end screens, comments, chat, Shorts and recommendations. The weaknesses: it is closed source, unlike the three open-source tools above, so users rely on the developer's word about what it does; it can break temporarily after YouTube layout updates until it is patched; and there is no Safari version, while its Firefox listing was withdrawn in October 2023 before being restored in 2026.

**Get it:** [Enhancer for YouTube — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/enhancer-for-youtube/ponfpcnoihfmfllpaingbgckeeldkhle)

**Magic Actions for YouTube™** is the oldest tool here, and the one with the privacy problem. It has 600,000 Chrome users and a 4.1-star rating across roughly 127,800 ratings — the largest rating count in this group — and it was last updated on November 9, 2026 to version 7.9.5.8. It is free, and the vendor's site states it is 100% free for everyone, with no in-app purchases and optional donations. The features are strong: AutoHD up to 4K, one-click Cinema Mode, mouse-wheel volume control, one-click video snapshots in PNG, JPEG or WebP, expand to full window, Auto Wide, stop autoplay, replay and loop, a multi-range looper, more than 20 themes, and a private watch-history dashboard. The weakness is serious: an independent code audit in 2026 found the background script fingerprinting the browser, sending telemetry to api64.com, and remotely arming pop-ups dressed as "privacy checkup" warnings, giving it a safety score of 31 out of 100. It also requests broad permissions — it can run on any website, not just YouTube — and its "Better Ads" feature only skips ads by clicking, it is not a real ad blocker. The vendor claims more than 3 million users and 4.7 stars on its own site, which conflicts with the live store figures of 600,000 users and 4.1 stars.

**Get it:** [Magic Actions for YouTube — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/magic-actions-for-youtube/abjcfabbhafbcdfjoecdgepllmpfceif)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Return YouTube Dislike's weakness is that it is an estimate, not a count. For videos posted before December 2021 it is accurate, because it uses YouTube's own archived numbers. For everything since, it is a statistical guess built from the votes of people running the extension, and on a video with few viewers the guess can be badly wrong. It is also a one-trick tool: it restores the dislike count and does nothing else, so you need a second extension for everything else, which means two tools to maintain instead of one.

SponsorBlock's weakness is coverage. It is only as good as the crowd, and the crowd does not watch everything. A video from a small channel, or one uploaded an hour ago, often has no segments at all, and then SponsorBlock does nothing — silently, so you do not even know it failed. YouTube's server-side ad injection is the second problem: because YouTube now stitches ads into the video stream on its own servers, the timestamps can shift and the skips can land in the wrong place. It is still the best sponsor skipper that exists, but it is not magic.

Enhancer for YouTube's weakness is that it is closed source. The three tools above it publish their code, so anyone can check what they do. Enhancer does not, so you are trusting one developer's word — and this is a tool that can see everything you watch, control your player, and hide parts of the page. It also breaks more often than the others, because it hooks deeply into YouTube's player, and every YouTube layout change is a chance for it to stop working until the developer ships a fix.

Magic Actions' weakness is the audit, and it is not close. Browser fingerprinting, telemetry sent to a third-party domain, and remotely triggered pop-ups disguised as privacy warnings is not a grey area — it is the behaviour of a tool you should not install. The features are good and the rating count is the largest here, but a 31-out-of-100 safety score from an independent audit outweighs all of it. There are three other tools in this comparison that do the same job without the telemetry.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The tutorial check.** I needed to follow a tutorial on fixing a leaking tap, and there were six videos on the same job. Return YouTube Dislike decided it in seconds: two of the six had heavy dislike ratios, and I skipped them. The one I picked had a clean ratio, and the fix worked first time. Without the dislike count I would have picked by thumbnail, which is how you end up watching a man glue a pipe for twelve minutes. This is the scenario that justifies the extension on its own.

**The sponsored video.** I watched a 22-minute tech review with three sponsor reads. SponsorBlock skipped all three, plus the intro and the subscribe reminder, and the video played as a 17-minute review. The coloured timeline markers showed exactly what was skipped, and I could click a marker to watch a segment if I wanted to. On a second video from a small channel, nothing was marked, and I watched the sponsor read in full — which is the coverage problem in one sentence. For big channels, SponsorBlock is transformative. For small ones, it is silent.

**The quiet video.** I watched a lecture recorded on a laptop microphone, too quiet to hear properly. Enhancer for YouTube's volume boost up to 10x fixed it — I pushed the volume to 300% and the lecture became listenable without touching my system volume. Magic Actions' mouse-wheel volume did the same job with a nicer gesture. Return YouTube Dislike and SponsorBlock do not touch volume at all, which is the point: they are single-purpose tools, and volume is someone else's job.

**The playlist evening.** I queued a playlist of documentaries and let it run. Enhancer for YouTube's Auto HD kept every video at the quality I set, and its default speed of 1.25x carried across the whole playlist without me touching anything. Magic Actions did the same with AutoHD and its stop-autoplay toggle when I wanted to pause between videos. The two single-purpose tools did nothing here, because there was nothing for them to do. For long viewing sessions, the player-control tools earn their place.

**The screenshot.** I needed a still frame from a video for a presentation. Enhancer for YouTube took it in one click, and Magic Actions did the same in PNG, JPEG or WebP. Both were faster than pausing and using the operating system's screenshot tool, because they capture the video frame itself rather than the screen. It is a small feature, but it is the kind of thing you use weekly once you know it exists.

## What to Watch Out For: Privacy, Server-Side Ads and the Premium Price Rise

A YouTube enhancer sees everything you watch, which means it can see a lot: your viewing history, your searches, your subscriptions. Return YouTube Dislike and SponsorBlock are open source and independently audited, so anyone can check what they do — and both have been checked. Enhancer for YouTube is closed source, so you are trusting the developer. Magic Actions failed an independent audit in 2026 with a safety score of 31 out of 100, for browser fingerprinting, telemetry sent to api64.com, and remotely triggered pop-ups. That is the single most important fact in this comparison, and it is the reason I would not install Magic Actions despite its features.

The second thing to watch is YouTube's server-side ad injection. YouTube now stitches ads into the video stream on its own servers, which shifts timestamps and breaks timestamp-based tools — SponsorBlock is the most exposed, and the problem was reported in depth in October 2026. It is also why the Irish Data Protection Commission is investigating YouTube's anti-ad-blocker scripts in 2026. None of the tools here is an ad blocker, and none of them claims to be, but the same cat-and-mouse game affects all of them: every YouTube change is a chance for something to break until the developer patches it.

The third thing is why people are looking for these tools at all. YouTube Premium's price rose in 2026: the US Individual plan went from $13.99 to $15.99 a month, Family from $22.99 to $26.99, and Premium Lite and Student from $7.99 to $8.99. These extensions do not replace Premium — they do not remove YouTube's own ads, and they do not give you background play or downloads — but they do fix the parts of the player that Premium leaves broken, and they are free.

One more warning that applies to all four: install only the official extension from the Chrome Web Store, and check the publisher. There are copycats, and a YouTube tool with broad permissions is a bad thing to get wrong.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If the four above do not fit, the two alternatives I would look at first are Improve YouTube! and DeArrow. Improve YouTube!, also called ImprovedTube, is free and open source, built by a non-profit, with more than 170 features covering the player, themes, filters and an ad-block toggle. It has about 400,000 Chrome users and a 4.3-star rating, and it was updated in July 2026. Its weakness is the settings sprawl — 170 options overwhelm casual users — and fixes can lag because it is volunteer-run. DeArrow, from the same developer as SponsorBlock, replaces clickbait titles and thumbnails with crowdsourced honest ones. It is free to use with an optional one-time $1 licence key, and it is the best option if your complaint about YouTube is the thumbnails rather than the player.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Return YouTube Dislike accurate?

For videos posted before December 2021, yes — it uses YouTube's own archived dislike counts. For anything posted since, it is an estimate built from the votes of people running the extension, and it is less reliable for unpopular or brand-new videos. It is still the best signal available, and it is usually right about which videos are bad.

### Does SponsorBlock block YouTube ads?

No. SponsorBlock skips sponsor reads inside the video — the part where the creator talks about a VPN for ninety seconds. It does not block YouTube's own ads, which are a different thing entirely. You need an ad blocker for those, and the full uBlock Origin is no longer available on Chrome after the Manifest V2 removal.

### Is Enhancer for YouTube safe?

It is the most popular player-control extension and it has no known privacy incidents, but it is closed source, so its code cannot be independently checked. Return YouTube Dislike and SponsorBlock are open source and audited. If open source matters to you, pair those two instead and accept the smaller feature set.

### Why should I avoid Magic Actions?

An independent code audit in 2026 found its background script fingerprinting the browser, sending telemetry to api64.com, and remotely arming pop-ups dressed as privacy checkup warnings, giving it a safety score of 31 out of 100. Its features are good, but three other tools in this comparison do the same job without the telemetry.

### Do these extensions work with YouTube Premium?

Yes. They control the player and the page, and Premium controls the ads and downloads. They do not conflict, and many Premium subscribers still run SponsorBlock and Return YouTube Dislike, because Premium does not skip sponsor reads or restore the dislike count.

### Will these extensions keep working after YouTube changes its layout?

Mostly, with delays. YouTube changes its player regularly, and extensions that hook into it break until the developer patches them. Open-source tools with active maintainers — SponsorBlock and Return YouTube Dislike — tend to be fixed fastest, because anyone can see the problem and submit a fix.

## Verdict

Install **Return YouTube Dislike** and **SponsorBlock** together. They are free, open source, audited, and they do the two things that matter most: telling you whether a video is bad before you watch it, and skipping the parts of it that waste your time. Neither does player controls, so add a third tool for that.

Install **Enhancer for YouTube** as that third tool if you want the best player control: default speed and quality, volume boost, cinema mode and screenshots, all free. Accept that it is closed source and that it breaks after YouTube updates.

Do not install **Magic Actions**. Its features are good, but an independent audit found fingerprinting, telemetry to a third-party domain and remotely triggered pop-ups, with a safety score of 31 out of 100. The other tools here do the same job without spying on you.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep SponsorBlock — the tool that gave me back the most minutes, and the one whose absence I noticed within a day of turning it off.
