## I Spent Three Weeks Letting Four Grammar Checkers Read Everything I Wrote

I once sent a proposal to a client with the wrong "their" in the second paragraph. The client never mentioned it. We still won the work. But I thought about that typo for a week, because it was the kind of mistake that tells a stranger exactly how careful you are, and I had not been careful enough. That is the whole case for a grammar checker that lives in your browser: it catches the error in the thirty seconds between writing and sending, when it still costs you nothing.

So for three weeks I ran four grammar checker extensions through my real working life: client emails in Gmail, a long report in Google Docs, cover letters, social posts, and the kind of late-night messages where my grammar falls apart first. I installed all four in Chrome, wrote with each one in turn, and kept notes on what each caught, what each missed, and — the part nobody puts on the pricing page — where each free plan quietly stops working.

The four tools split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. Grammarly and QuillBot are free to install and genuinely usable without paying, with the paid plans unlocking rewrites and extras. LanguageTool used to be in that camp, and then in 2026 it moved its browser extension behind a paywall. ProWritingAid sits in a camp of its own: a deep editor for long-form writing whose free plan is so limited it is really a demo.

## The Four Contenders

**Grammarly** is the biggest tool here by an absurd margin — about 41 million Chrome users, against 6 million for the next biggest — and it is also the most controversial. Its Chrome Web Store rating has fallen to 3.9 stars across roughly 43,200 ratings, down from 4.5 just two months ago, and the most recent hundred reviews average closer to 2.8. The complaints behind that fall are mostly about speed, reliability and paywall placement rather than suggestion quality, and that matches what I saw: the checking itself is still the best in this group. The free plan covers spelling, grammar, punctuation and tone detection, plus 100 generative-AI prompts a month, with no word limit I could hit in normal use. The Pro plan — the old Premium, renamed in late 2024 — costs [$12 a month on annual billing ($144 a year)](https://www.grammarly.com/plans), $20 quarterly, or $30 month to month, and adds full-sentence rewrites, tone adjustment, a plagiarism checker, AI-text detection, and 2,000 AI prompts a month. It works in English only, across five dialects: American, British, Canadian, Australian and Indian. What won me over in testing was the catch rate: on a test paragraph I wrote with 23 planted errors, Grammarly caught all 23, the only tool of the four to do so.

**Get it:** [Grammarly — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/grammarly-for-chrome/kbfnbcaeplbcioakkpcpgfkobkghlhen)

**LanguageTool** is the only tool here that checks more than one language properly — more than 30 of them, including French, German, Spanish, Polish and Russian — and the only one whose core is open source, which means you can run your own checking server and point the extension at it. It has about 2 million Chrome users and a 3.3-star rating across roughly 12,600 ratings, down from 4.7 in September. That collapse has a single cause, and it is the most important fact in this whole article: LanguageTool made its browser extension Premium-only in 2026. New installs get a 14-day trial, and when it ends the extension stops checking your text until you subscribe. Premium costs [$4.99 a month on annual billing ($59.90 a year)](https://languagetool.org/premium) or $19.90 month to month, and adds more than 20,000 extra checks in English, German, French, Spanish, Dutch, Polish and Portuguese, plus a 100,000-character limit per check against 10,000 on the free web editor. The free web checker at languagetool.org still works without an account, limited to 10,000 characters per check. If you write in more than one language, nothing else here comes close — but you no longer get it free in the browser.

**Get it:** [LanguageTool — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/ai-grammar-checker-paraph/oldceeleldhonbafppcapldpdifcinji)

**QuillBot** is the only tool here that is really a rewriter wearing a grammar checker's clothes, and that is exactly why students and non-native English speakers love it. The Chrome extension has about 6 million users and a 4.7-star rating across roughly 6,100 ratings — the best rating in this group by a wide margin, and the only one that has not collapsed this year. The free plan gives you a basic grammar and spell check, a paraphraser in two modes (Standard and Fluency) limited to 125 words per input, a summarizer up to 1,200 words, and an AI detector with limited use. Premium costs [$8.33 a month on annual billing ($99.95 a year)](https://quillbot.com/premium) or $19.95 month to month, and unlocks unlimited paraphrasing in nine modes, grammar correction in six languages, paraphrasing in 23 languages, a plagiarism checker with 25,000 words a month, a 6,000-word summarizer, and translation into more than 40 languages. The trade-off is real: QuillBot rewrites your sentences, and a rewrite can quietly change what you meant. I watched both free modes alter a small detail in a clean paragraph — a date, a name — and present the change as an improvement.

**Get it:** [QuillBot — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/quillbot-ai-writing-and-g/iidnbdjijdkbmajdffnidomddglmieko)

**ProWritingAid** is the deepest editor here and the least suited to quick email checks. The Chrome extension has about 200,000 users and a 4.6-star rating across roughly 7,000 ratings, and the product behind it is built for novelists and long-form writers: more than 25 reports covering sticky sentences, overused words, clichés, pacing, readability and dialogue, plus a planning mode and a chapter critique feature. The free plan is the tightest in this group by a long way: 500 words per check, two runs of each line-editing report a day, 10 rephrases a day and 3 AI Sparks a day. Premium costs [$10 a month on annual billing ($120 a year)](https://prowritingaid.com/pricing) or $30 month to month, and Premium Pro costs $12 a month on annual billing ($144 a year) or $36 monthly. Both also sell as lifetime licences — $399 for Premium and $699 for Premium Pro — which is unusual in this category and worth pricing out if you plan to use it for years. It checks English only, across five variants. If you write books, reports or anything longer than a few pages, its depth is unmatched here. If you write emails, its free plan will frustrate you within a day.

**Get it:** [ProWritingAid — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/prowritingaid/npnbdojkgkbcdfdjlfdmplppdphlhhcf)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Grammarly's weakness is the one its own users keep reporting: bloat and nagging. The extension is the heaviest of the four, and the recent reviews are full of complaints about slowdowns, a sign-in flow that occasionally forgets you, and suggestions that blur into upsells — the advanced rewrite appears, greyed out, with a lock icon, just often enough to annoy. Its privacy story is also the weakest here: its privacy policy, effective July 2026, says it does not sell user content, but reviewers note that identifiers and browsing data are shared with advertising partners. And it only checks English. If you write in two languages, Grammarly is half a tool.

LanguageTool's weakness is the paywall, and it is not close. A tool that built its reputation on being the free, open, multilingual alternative now charges $4.99 a month for the thing most people installed it for — checking text in the browser — and its rating fell from 4.7 to 3.3 in two months as users protested with one-star reviews. The free web checker survives, but pasting text into a website is not the same as having the checker live where you type. The accuracy gap is real too: on the same 23-error test paragraph, LanguageTool caught 15 of the 23 errors in English, well behind Grammarly. It is a better multilingual tool than an English one, and now you pay for that privilege.

QuillBot's weakness is that paraphrasing is not proofreading. Its grammar check is basic — it catches typos and straightforward errors, but it missed the subtler mistakes in my test paragraph that Grammarly and even LanguageTool caught, like a misplaced modifier and a subject-verb agreement error buried in a long sentence. The free plan's 125-word paraphrase limit also means a single long email has to be rewritten in chunks, and in 2026 a daily cap was added on top of the per-input limit, so a heavy day can exhaust your free allowance before lunch. And the refund policy hardened: the old 3-day money-back guarantee is gone, and refunds are now issued only where the law requires them — which matters, because its annual plan doubled in price in 2025, from $49.95 to $99.95 a year.

ProWritingAid's weakness is the free plan, and it is not close. Five hundred words per check is about two manuscript pages, and two report runs a day means you can properly check one short document and then you are done until tomorrow. The extension is also the slowest of the four on long documents — users report lag past 8,000 words, and I felt it stutter in Google Docs on a 3,000-word report. Its refund window shrank from 14 days to 3 days, which is the shortest here, and the pricing page no longer carries the old "no word limits" promise under Premium. For a novelist the depth justifies all of it. For everyone else, the free plan is a shop window, not a tool.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The client email written in a hurry.** I drafted the same kind of email four times — a short proposal follow-up, written fast, with a wrong "their", a missing comma, and a sentence that ran on for three lines. Grammarly caught everything and suggested a shorter version of the run-on sentence that read better than mine. QuillBot caught the spelling and the wrong "their" but let the run-on stand. LanguageTool caught the spelling and the grammar but missed the tone — the email read blunt, and it said nothing. ProWritingAid caught the errors too, but its free plan spent one of its two daily report runs on a six-sentence email, which felt like using a crane to hang a picture.

**The 3,000-word report in Google Docs.** This is where the free plans separate. Grammarly checked the whole document without a word limit and flagged a repeated phrase I had used four times. LanguageTool's web checker handled it in 10,000-character chunks — workable, but pasting a report into a website in pieces is not a workflow. QuillBot's grammar check handled it, but its 125-word paraphrase limit meant the rewriting help I actually wanted was useless at this length. ProWritingAid was the most useful tool in this scenario once I paid: its sticky-sentence and overused-words reports found problems the other three never look for. But on the free plan I hit the 500-word cap before the end of page one, and the extension lagged noticeably as the document grew.

**The email in French and the reply in German.** I tested a short business email in French and another in German, because this is the scenario that decides the category for millions of writers outside the English-speaking world. LanguageTool was the only tool that could do it at all — it checked both emails, caught a gender-agreement error in the French one, and handled the German compound verbs without complaint. Grammarly and ProWritingAid do not check French or German, full stop. QuillBot's grammar checker covers six languages including French and German, and it caught the French error, but its German suggestions were thinner and its paraphraser is strongest in English. If you write in more than one language, this scenario alone picks your tool.

**The essay that needed rewriting, not just fixing.** A student-style essay draft with correct grammar but weak, repetitive wording — the kind of text where the problem is not errors but dullness. QuillBot won this scenario by a mile: its Fluency mode tightened the sentences, its Formal mode lifted the register, and the side-by-side comparison showed exactly what changed. Grammarly Pro's full-sentence rewrites were good too, but they are paywalled — the free plan shows you the rewrite blurred behind a lock. LanguageTool flagged a few style issues and stopped. ProWritingAid's reports found the repetition, but turning its findings into better sentences was my job, not its job. If your writing is already correct and you want it better, QuillBot is the tool.

**The sensitive draft I did not want on a server.** A document with client names, rates and contract terms — the kind of text you think twice about before sending to a cloud service. This scenario has no winner among the four, only a least-bad option. Grammarly, QuillBot and ProWritingAid all send your text to their servers to check it; Grammarly's policy also shares identifiers and browsing data with advertising partners. LanguageTool at least gives you an escape hatch no one else offers: because its core is open source, you can run your own checking server and point the extension at it, so your text never leaves your machine. For most people that is more effort than it is worth. For anyone handling confidential text, it is the only honest answer in this group.

## What to Watch Out For: Permissions and Privacy

A grammar checker reads everything you type. That is the deal, and you should go into it with your eyes open. Every tool here except a self-hosted LanguageTool sends the text you write to its servers, because that is where the checking happens. Before you install one, read what it does with that text, and never run a grammar checker over a document you would not be comfortable explaining to the person it is about.

The privacy stories split three ways. ProWritingAid makes the strongest promise: it says it never uses your text to train AI models, with bank-level security. QuillBot's store listing states that it processes only what is needed to provide suggestions and does not sell your data. Grammarly's policy, effective July 2026, says it does not sell user content — but reviewers note that identifiers and browsing data are shared with advertising partners, which is the weakest privacy story in this group for a tool with 41 million users.

LanguageTool is the interesting one. Its core is open source under the LGPL, its web checker needs no account, and its extension can be pointed at your own server. That is a genuine privacy option nobody else here offers. The catch is that the extension itself is now Premium-only, so the privacy-friendly route now costs $4.99 a month or a self-hosted server of your own.

One rule I follow with all four: turn the checker off — or use a self-hosted option — for anything containing passwords, medical details, legal documents or client-confidential information. A red underline is not worth a copy of your text on a server you do not control.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If you write in English and want a checker that never sends your text anywhere, it is worth knowing that a newer breed of privacy-first checkers runs entirely in the browser — the text never leaves your device. They are English-focused and less powerful than the four tools above, but for short, sensitive text they solve the privacy problem at its root, and they cost nothing. Check what your browser already does first, too: Chrome's built-in spell check catches the obvious typos for free, and it is the baseline every tool here has to beat.

If you are a student, check the student discounts before you pay for anything here. QuillBot offers a verified-student rate of $6.25 a month on the annual plan with a university email, against $8.33 standard. Grammarly and ProWritingAid both run education pricing through their own programmes, and the savings are real enough to be worth the five minutes it takes to verify your student status.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which grammar checker extension is actually free?

Grammarly and QuillBot both have genuinely usable free plans: Grammarly's free tier covers grammar, spelling, punctuation and tone with no word limit, and QuillBot's covers basic grammar checks plus paraphrasing up to 125 words per input. LanguageTool's browser extension is no longer free — it is Premium-only after a 14-day trial — though its web checker remains free up to 10,000 characters. ProWritingAid's free plan is limited to 500 words per check, so it runs out fast.

### Is Grammarly or LanguageTool better for non-English writing?

LanguageTool, by a wide margin. It checks more than 30 languages, including French, German, Spanish, Polish and Russian. Grammarly checks English only, across five dialects. If you write in two languages, LanguageTool is the only serious option among these four, with QuillBot's six-language grammar checker as a backup.

### Why did LanguageTool's rating fall from 4.7 to 3.3?

Because it made its browser extension Premium-only in 2026. New installs get a 14-day trial, then the extension stops checking until you subscribe at $4.99 a month on annual billing. Users responded with a wave of one-star reviews, and the rating fell from 4.7 in September to 3.3 by November. The free web checker at languagetool.org still works.

### Can a grammar checker change what I meant to say?

Yes, and QuillBot is the one to watch. Its paraphraser rewrites sentences, and in my testing both free modes changed a small detail — a date, a name — in an otherwise clean paragraph. Always read a rewrite before you accept it, and never paraphrase text containing names, dates, numbers or quotes without checking every word afterwards.

### Which grammar checker is best for students?

QuillBot, for most students: the free plan covers short rewrites and basic grammar, Premium adds a plagiarism checker with 25,000 words a month, and verified students get Premium at $6.25 a month on the annual plan with a university email. If the work is long-form essays or a thesis, ProWritingAid's depth and lifetime licence are worth pricing out instead.

### Is it safe to use a grammar checker on confidential documents?

Mostly, but read the policy first. Grammarly, QuillBot and ProWritingAid all send your text to their servers; ProWritingAid promises never to use your text to train AI models, and QuillBot states it does not sell your data. Grammarly shares identifiers and browsing data with advertising partners. LanguageTool is the only one with a fully private option: run your own server, since its core is open source. My rule is simple — never run a cloud checker over passwords, medical details, legal documents or client-confidential text.

## Verdict

Install **Grammarly** if you write in English and want the strongest checker that costs nothing. It caught every error I planted, its free plan has no word limit, and 41 million users means it is tested against more real writing than anything else here. Accept the English-only limitation and the upsell nudges, and turn it off for confidential text.

Install **LanguageTool** if you write in more than one language. Nothing else here checks 30+ languages, and the open-source core with a self-hosting option is a genuine privacy advantage. Pay the $4.99 a month on annual billing — the free browser extension is gone, and pretending otherwise wastes your time.

Choose **QuillBot** if your writing needs rewriting, not just correcting. Nine paraphrase modes, a plagiarism checker, the best rating in this group at 4.7 stars, and Premium at $8.33 a month on annual billing. Read every rewrite before you accept it.

And choose **ProWritingAid** only if you write long-form work — books, theses, long reports. Its 25+ reports find problems the other three never look for, and the $399 lifetime licence pays for itself in just over three years against the annual plan. For emails and short documents, its free plan will frustrate you within a day.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep Grammarly — the tool whose corrections I stopped double-checking by the second week.
