## I Spent Three Weeks Living Inside Four Password Vaults

I have 214 saved logins. I counted them, because counting them was the first thing that made me feel slightly ill about my own security habits. Of those 214, forty-one shared the same password — a variation on a word I first used in 2011 — and nine were for accounts I could not even remember creating. That is the real state of most people's password security, mine included, and it is the reason I stopped trusting my memory and my browser's built-in saver and started testing dedicated password managers properly.

For three weeks I ran four of them through my actual working life: importing those 214 logins from Chrome, logging in to banking, email, shopping and client portals, changing weak passwords one by one, testing autofill on awkward two-page login forms, saving passkeys, and switching between my laptop and my phone to see which vault actually follows me around. I also watched what each one costs now, because 2026 has been a year of price rises in this category, and the prices quoted in last year's roundups are wrong.

The four tools split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. Bitwarden and Dashlane and LastPass all offer a free plan of some kind. 1Password does not — it is paid only, after a 14-day trial. And the free plans are not equal: one of them stores unlimited passwords on unlimited devices for nothing, one of them caps you at 25 passwords on a single device, and one of them lets you keep unlimited passwords but only on one type of device. Which camp you belong in decides most of this comparison before you read a single feature.

## The Four Contenders

**1Password** is the most polished tool in this group, and it is not close. The Chrome extension has about 7 million users and a 4.4-star rating across roughly 3,900 ratings, and it feels like the product of a company that has thought about every millimetre of the experience: the extension spots login fields other managers miss, including the awkward two-page logins and forms buried inside iframes, and unlocking with a fingerprint or face takes under a second. There is no free plan — after a 14-day trial you pay, and the price went up on March 27, 2026. Individual now costs $3.99 a month on annual billing, or $47.88 a year, up from $35.88; new customers pay $2.99 a month for the first year only. Families costs $5.99 a month, or $71.88 a year, for five people. The current figures are on [1Password's pricing page](https://1password.com/pricing/). What you get for that money is a 128-bit Secret Key that works alongside your master password, a Watchtower dashboard that flags weak, reused and breached passwords, Travel Mode for crossing borders, 1 GB of document storage, built-in two-factor codes, and passkey support. It runs on macOS, Windows, Linux, iOS, Android and ChromeOS, with extensions for Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge and Brave.

**Get it:** [1Password — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/1password-password-manager/aeblfdkhhhdcdjpifhhbdiojplfjncoa)

**Bitwarden** is the only open-source tool here, and the only one whose free plan has no meaningful limit: unlimited passwords on unlimited devices, forever, with no ads and no selling of data. The Chrome extension has about 7 million users, but its rating tells a story — 2.9 stars across roughly 8,100 ratings as of November 2026, down from 4.3 earlier in the year, with the complaints concentrated on the redesigned extension and the January 2026 price rise. That rise was the first in Bitwarden's history: Premium went from $10 a year to $19.80 a year, and Families went from $40 to $47.88 a year for up to six people, as shown on [Bitwarden's pricing page](https://bitwarden.com/pricing/). Premium adds the built-in authenticator for two-factor codes, vault health reports, emergency access, encrypted file attachments and priority support. The security story is the strongest in this group on paper: zero-knowledge encryption with AES-256 and PBKDF2, annual third-party audits, and no public record of a vault breach. You can even self-host the server if you want to. The extension is available in 55 languages, and it was last updated on November 2, 2026.

**Get it:** [Bitwarden — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/bitwarden/nngceckbapebfimnlniiiahkandclblbic)

**Dashlane** is the only tool here that tries to be a whole security suite rather than just a vault. The Chrome extension has about 1 million users and a 4.7-star rating across roughly 18,200 ratings — the highest rating in this group — and it was updated on November 9, 2026. Its free plan is the most restricted: 25 passwords on one device, which is a trial in everything but name. The paid ladder starts with Advanced at $2.75 a month on annual billing for unlimited passwords on unlimited devices, then Premium at $4.99 a month, or $59.88 a year, which adds a built-in VPN powered by Hotspot Shield, secure file storage and phishing alerts, and Friends & Family at $7.49 a month for up to ten people — the current tiers are listed on [Dashlane's pricing page](https://www.dashlane.com/pricing). What sets Dashlane apart is the monitoring: live dark web monitoring, a password health dashboard, and alerts when your credentials appear in a breach. The trade-off is that there is no desktop app any more — you use the web app and the browser extension — and there is no Linux desktop app at all.

**Get it:** [Dashlane — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/dashlane-password-manager/fdjamakpfbbfmyegmbbhhjdbbhbjcllf)

**LastPass** is the biggest name here by users and the hardest to recommend. The Chrome extension has about 8 million users, but a 1.8-star rating across roughly 28,500 ratings — the lowest rating of any major password manager on the store, and a number that reflects years of accumulated anger rather than one bad update. The free plan gives you unlimited passwords, but only on one device type: computers or phones, not both. Premium costs $3 a month, or $36 a year, and unlocks both device types, one-to-many sharing, emergency access and 1 GB of file storage; Families costs $4 a month, or $48 a year, for six people — see [LastPass's pricing page](https://www.lastpass.com/pricing). New accounts start with a 30-day trial of Premium. The autofill is still genuinely good — LastPass has been doing this longer than anyone — and passkey support arrived in August 2025. But the trust problem is real and specific: in late 2022 attackers stole encrypted copies of customer vault backups, and in December 2025 the UK's Information Commissioner's Office fined LastPass £1.2 million over the incident, finding that up to 1.6 million UK users were affected. A US class action over the same breach was settled in 2025 for $24.5 million.

**Get it:** [LastPass — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/lastpass-free-password-ma/hdokiejnpimakedhajhdlcegeplioahd)

## Where Each One Falls Short

1Password's weakness is the price, and the price rise made it worse. At $47.88 a year it costs more than twice what Bitwarden Premium costs, and it is the only tool here with no free plan at all — you cannot even try it properly without starting a trial clock. The first-year discount of $2.99 a month is also a trap for the inattentive: the price jumps to $3.99 in year two, and several review sites still quote the old $2.99 as if it were the standard price. It is also a closed-source product, so unlike Bitwarden you are trusting the company's word about its encryption rather than code anyone can inspect. And there is no way to self-host: your vault lives on 1Password's servers, full stop.

Bitwarden's weakness is the experience, and its own users are saying so. The extension redesign that landed in early 2025 broke flows long-time users relied on, and the store rating has slid from 4.3 to 2.9 across the year — a fall that big does not happen because of one bug. In my testing the autofill missed more fields than 1Password's, particularly on banking sites with non-standard forms, and the interface still feels like a tool built by engineers for engineers: powerful, but with settings buried two menus deep and labels that assume you already know what a "KDF iteration" is. The free plan is generous, but the free plan does not include the built-in authenticator, so your two-factor codes still live in a separate app unless you pay.

Dashlane's weakness is the free plan, and it is not close. Twenty-five passwords on one device is not a free tier, it is a demo: the average person has well over a hundred accounts, so you hit the wall within a day of importing. And once you pay, Dashlane is the most expensive tool here — $59.88 a year for Premium, against $47.88 for 1Password and $19.80 for Bitwarden Premium — for a product that no longer has a desktop app. The VPN is also a rebadged Hotspot Shield rather than Dashlane's own network, and it is only available on the Premium tier, not the cheaper Advanced tier.

LastPass's weakness is trust, and trust is the entire product. A password manager holds the keys to your email, your bank and your work accounts, and LastPass is the one company in this group that has actually lost encrypted copies of its customers' vaults — then, according to the UK regulator, failed to implement sufficiently robust security measures. The 1.8-star store rating is the visible scar of that history, and the free plan's one-device-type rule means most people will hit a paywall within their first week. Its autofill is still good and its price is fair, but every other tool here gives you the same job without asking you to forgive a breach.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The 214-password import.** I imported the same Chrome CSV file into all four vaults. Bitwarden and 1Password both handled it cleanly, with Bitwarden finishing first and sorting everything into folders I could rename in bulk. Dashlane imported the file and then stopped me at 25 passwords on the free plan, which meant the import itself was the moment I had to decide whether to pay. LastPass imported all 214 without complaint, then reminded me the free plan would not sync them to my phone. The lesson is practical: if you are migrating from Chrome's built-in saver, start with Bitwarden or 1Password, because the other two make you pay before you have even finished moving in.

**The awkward bank login.** My bank splits its login across two pages — username first, then password on the next screen — and buries the second form inside an iframe. 1Password filled both pages without hesitation, every time. Dashlane managed it on the second attempt after I taught it the field once. Bitwarden filled the username, then sat on the password page until I clicked the extension icon and chose the login by hand. LastPass handled it about as well as 1Password. If your bank or your work portal has a strange login flow, autofill accuracy is the feature you will notice every single day, and 1Password wins it.

**The phone-and-laptop switch.** I saved a new password on my laptop, then picked up my phone ten minutes later to log in to the same site. Bitwarden and 1Password had it waiting on the phone immediately. Dashlane's free plan did not sync at all — one device means one device. LastPass's free plan did the same, and the fix in both cases is the paid plan. This is the scenario that decides the free tier for most people: a password manager that only lives on one device is a password manager you will stop trusting the first time you need a login on the other one.

**The password health cleanup.** Each tool has a dashboard that grades your vault. 1Password's Watchtower was the clearest: it flagged my forty-one reused passwords, ranked them by risk, and let me work through them in order, weakest first. Dashlane's health dashboard was nearly as good and added breach alerts for two of my email addresses that I had not known were exposed. Bitwarden's health reports are a Premium feature, and on the free plan you get nothing — no reuse report, no breach check, no weak-password list. LastPass's security dashboard exists on the free plan, but its breach alerts felt a step behind Dashlane's. I cleared all forty-one reused passwords in about two weeks, and the tool that made that job easiest was 1Password, with Dashlane second.

**The shared family login.** I shared the household streaming and utility logins with my partner. Bitwarden Families did this best for the money: $47.88 a year for six people, each with their own Premium account and shared collections. 1Password Families was the smoothest to set up — the shared vaults and account recovery are genuinely well designed — but at $71.88 a year for five people it costs half as much again. Dashlane Friends & Family covers ten people for $7.49 a month, the most seats in this group, which makes it the cheapest per person if you actually fill all ten seats. LastPass Families is $48 a year for six, the joint-cheapest with Bitwarden, but sharing was the one place its older interface felt clunkiest.

## What to Watch Out For: Permissions and Privacy

A password manager extension asks for the broadest permissions of any extension you will ever install. All four of these request access to read and change data on the sites you visit, because that is how autofill works — the extension has to see the login form to fill it. That is normal, and it is also the reason you should install only the official extension from the Chrome Web Store, published by the company itself, and never a lookalike with a similar name.

The deeper question is what happens to your vault. 1Password, Bitwarden and Dashlane are all zero-knowledge: your vault is encrypted on your device with a key derived from your master password, and the company cannot read it. Bitwarden goes further by publishing its code, so anyone can verify the claim, and by offering self-hosting for people who do not want their vault on anyone's servers. LastPass also encrypts vaults on the device, but its 2022 incident showed the limit of that promise: attackers stole encrypted copies of the vaults, and while the encryption held, the company's own security around those backups did not — which is why the UK regulator fined it £1.2 million in December 2025.

Three rules I follow with all four. First, use a master password you have never used anywhere else, and write it down on paper and store that paper somewhere safe — if you forget it, no company here can recover your vault for you. Second, turn on two-factor authentication for the vault itself, not just for the sites inside it. Third, never store your master password in the browser or in a note on your phone. The vault is only as strong as the one password that opens it.

One more warning that applies to all four: the price you see quoted in old reviews is probably wrong. 1Password raised its prices in March 2026 and Bitwarden raised its in January 2026, and review sites are still showing the old numbers months later. Check the vendor's own pricing page before you pay — I verified all four the week I wrote this — and watch for first-year discounts that jump in year two.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If the four above do not fit, the two alternatives I would look at first are NordPass and Proton Pass. NordPass comes from the team behind NordVPN, has about 9 million Chrome users and a 4.7-star store rating, and its free plan covers unlimited passwords on one device with a Premium tier that is often discounted for the first year. It is the cleanest option if you already pay for NordVPN and want one account for both. Proton Pass comes from the team behind Proton Mail, has about 1 million Chrome users and a 4.7-star rating, and its free plan is genuinely usable, with a paid tier that bundles in email aliases through SimpleLogin. It is the strongest choice if you already live in Proton's ecosystem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which password manager is actually free?

Bitwarden, by a clear margin. Its free plan stores unlimited passwords on unlimited devices, forever, with no ads. Dashlane's free plan caps you at 25 passwords on one device, and LastPass's free plan gives you unlimited passwords but only on one device type — computers or phones, not both. 1Password has no free plan at all, only a 14-day trial.

### Is Bitwarden really safe if it is free?

Yes. Bitwarden is open source, so its code is public and audited by independent security firms every year, and it uses zero-knowledge encryption — your vault is encrypted on your device and Bitwarden cannot read it. There is no public record of a Bitwarden vault breach. The free plan is funded by the paid plans, not by advertising or selling data.

### Why did Bitwarden's Chrome Web Store rating fall to 2.9?

The extension redesign that landed in early 2025 changed flows long-time users relied on, and the rating slid from 4.3 earlier in 2026 to 2.9 by November. The January 2026 price rise — Premium from $10 to $19.80 a year — added to the frustration. The vault itself and the free plan did not change; the complaints are about the extension's interface and the price, not about security.

### Can I trust LastPass after the 2022 breach?

That depends on what you need to forgive. Attackers stole encrypted copies of customer vault backups in late 2022, and in December 2025 the UK regulator fined LastPass £1.2 million, finding its security measures insufficient and up to 1.6 million UK users affected. The encryption itself held — there is no evidence the stolen vaults were decrypted — but the incident is the reason LastPass sits at 1.8 stars on the store. If trust is your top criterion, Bitwarden or 1Password are safer choices.

### Do password managers work with passkeys?

Yes, all four of these do. 1Password, Bitwarden and Dashlane have supported passkeys for a while, and LastPass added passkey support in August 2025. A passkey replaces the password entirely on sites that support it, and the manager stores it in your vault and fills it the same way it fills a password. I now use passkeys wherever a site offers them.

### What happens if I forget my master password?

You lose access to your vault, in most cases permanently. All four of these use zero-knowledge encryption, which means the company cannot reset your master password for you, because it does not have it. 1Password Families and Bitwarden Families both offer account recovery through a family organiser, which is the one safety net worth setting up. Write your master password down on paper and keep it somewhere safe.

## Verdict

Install **Bitwarden** if you want a password manager that costs nothing and does the job properly. Unlimited passwords on unlimited devices for free, open source, zero-knowledge, no vault breach in its history, and Premium at $19.80 a year is the cheapest paid plan here when you want the authenticator and health reports. Accept that the extension's interface is rougher than the others and that its store rating has fallen for a reason.

Install **1Password** if you want the best password manager and you are willing to pay for it. It had the most accurate autofill in my testing, the clearest Watchtower cleanup, and the most polished experience on every device. The March 2026 price rise to $47.88 a year stings, and there is no free plan, but for the tool that guards your email, your bank and your work, it is the one I would pay for.

Choose **Dashlane** if you want a security suite rather than just a vault: live dark web monitoring, a password health dashboard and a VPN in one subscription, with the highest store rating in this group at 4.7 stars. Skip the free plan — 25 passwords on one device is a demo — and go straight to Premium at $4.99 a month if the monitoring matters to you.

And choose **LastPass** only if you are already invested in it and your vault is already there. Its autofill is still good and Premium at $36 a year is fairly priced, but the 2022 breach, the £1.2 million fine, the 1.8-star rating and the one-device-type free plan all point the same way: the other three give you the same job without asking you to forgive a breach.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep 1Password — the tool whose autofill I stopped double-checking by the second week, and the one I trust with the logins I cannot afford to lose.
