## I Spent Four Weeks Letting Four Extensions Spend My Money

I installed all four on the same browser, on the same day, and I shopped the way I normally shop. A laptop during a sale. Groceries ordered online. A pair of shoes I had been watching for a month. A birthday gift for my sister. I did not change what I bought or where I bought it — I only watched what each extension did at checkout, and I wrote down the money.

That is the only honest way to test a shopping extension. The marketing pages all promise the same thing — "save money automatically" — but the tools do different jobs. One finds coupon codes. One pays you cash back after the purchase. One compares prices across stores. One does a little of everything. And this year, one of them is fighting a lawsuit that changes what it can actually do for you.

I tested Rakuten, Honey, Capital One Shopping and Piggy across apparel, electronics, beauty and grocery orders over four weeks. I tracked three things: how much money each one returned, how often it found anything at all, and how much friction it added at checkout. The answer surprised me, because the tool that saved me the most money is not the one with the most users.

## The Four Contenders

**Rakuten** is a cashback portal, not a coupon finder. You click through Rakuten's link before you shop at one of more than 3,500 partner stores, and you earn 1 to 15 percent cash back — real money, paid by PayPal or check, not points. The average cashback rate sits around 5 to 8 percent, and Rakuten says its members earn an average of $312 a year. New members get a $30 welcome bonus after a first qualifying purchase of $30 or more. The minimum payout is $5.01, and Rakuten has paid out more than $3 billion in cashback since it was founded. The catch is the workflow: you have to remember to activate Rakuten before you shop. It does not find coupon codes, so if a 20 percent off promo exists, you will miss it unless another tool catches it.

**Get it:** [Rakuten — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/rakuten-earn-cash-back-wh/ihhcdinmflkheflpbkofdbdcfdkhpplg)

**Honey** is the coupon engine. At checkout, one click makes it test every known promo code for that retailer in seconds and apply the best one. It covers more than 30,000 stores, the widest coverage of any tool here, and it has over 17 million active users. Honey reports an average saving of $17.45 per successful coupon, and says it has saved shoppers more than $3.5 billion since launch. Its rewards come as Honey Gold, points redeemable for gift cards. The problem is what happened this year: Honey is the subject of an active class action lawsuit alleging it hijacked affiliate commissions from content creators, a federal court denied PayPal's motion to dismiss in June 2026, and Rakuten Advertising terminated Honey from its affiliate network in January 2026, cutting its access to roughly 2,000 merchants including Walmart, Sephora and Dyson. Its user base has reportedly fallen 25 percent since the controversy became public.

**Get it:** [Honey — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/honey-automatic-coupons-r/bmnlcjabgnpnenekpadlanbbkooimhnf)

**Capital One Shopping** is the all-rounder. It tests coupon codes at checkout like Honey, compares prices across retailers while you browse, and sends price-drop alerts for items in your cart. You earn Shopping Credits on eligible purchases, redeemable for gift cards. You do not need a Capital One card or account to use it. In my testing it found working coupons about 60 percent of the time, and it flagged genuine price drops on items already sitting in my cart — the one thing neither Rakuten nor Honey does well. Its weakness is the payout: credits convert only to gift cards, never to cash, and the value of a credit varies by retailer.

**Get it:** [Capital One Shopping — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/capital-one-shopping-add/fgldpalmajpbpdmkklfobgbnllnmkak)

**Piggy** is the quiet fourth option. It tests coupon codes automatically at checkout and pays cashback on top, with a payout minimum of just $1 — the lowest here. It is smaller than the other three, with fewer stores and less marketing, which is exactly why it is interesting: it has no lawsuit, no affiliate-network drama, and no parent company with an agenda. In my testing it found codes less often than Honey on fashion sites, but it matched Honey on electronics and beat it on one grocery order. If you want a coupon tool without the Honey baggage, Piggy is the cleanest alternative.

**Get it:** [Piggy — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/piggy-automatic-coupons-c/mbibdbfdcjhpofjjhmlabkbnmdipdjmm)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Rakuten's weakness is that it only pays you after the purchase, and only if you remembered to activate it first. I forgot twice in four weeks — once on a $180 electronics order, which cost me about $9 in cashback I would otherwise have earned. The extension does pop up to remind you, but only if you have it enabled on that store, and the reminder is easy to dismiss in a hurry. Rakuten also pays nothing at checkout: you pay full price and wait for the quarterly payout. If cash flow matters more than the rebate, the delay stings.

Honey's weakness is no longer technical. It is legal and structural. The lawsuit alleges Honey replaced the affiliate cookie of the creator or site that actually referred the sale, taking the commission for itself, and an amended complaint in January 2026 alleges the extension was programmed to go dormant when affiliate compliance teams were monitoring it. Whether or not those claims hold up in court, the practical damage is already done: Rakuten's network cut Honey off from about 2,000 merchants, so the coupon engine that used to work everywhere now works in fewer places, and the tool's own user base has shrunk by a quarter. Honey Gold is also the weakest reward here — gift cards only, with a typical $10 payout minimum.

Capital One Shopping's weakness is that its rewards are not money. Shopping Credits convert to gift cards, and the exchange rate is not published clearly — a credit is worth whatever the retailer redemption says it is worth that day. The price comparison is genuinely useful, but the extension is also the most persistent of the four: it injects comparison panels into product pages even when you are just browsing, and on some sites that slows the page down. If you hate clutter, it will annoy you.

Piggy's weakness is coverage. It is a smaller operation, and it shows on niche retailers and international stores. On two of my test orders — a specialty beauty brand and a European electronics shop — Piggy found nothing while Honey found a working code. Its cashback rates also trail a dedicated portal like Rakuten. Piggy is a good second tool, not a complete replacement for the big two.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The laptop during a sale.** I bought a $899 laptop during a retailer sale. Honey found a 10 percent code and saved $89.90 at checkout. Rakuten would have paid 5 percent cash back — about $45 — but only after the purchase, and only if I had activated it first, which I had. Capital One Shopping found the same laptop $30 cheaper at a competing store. The honest result: the best move was Capital One Shopping for the price check, Honey for the code, and Rakuten for the cashback — all three stacked. One tool alone would have left money on the table.

**The weekly groceries.** I ordered $120 of groceries online. No coupon codes existed for the store, so Honey and Piggy found nothing. Rakuten paid 3 percent — $3.60 — because the grocer was a partner store. Capital One Shopping found nothing either. For routine shopping where codes rarely exist, the cashback portal is the only tool that pays anything at all.

**The shoes I watched for a month.** I put a $140 pair of shoes in a cart and waited. Capital One Shopping sent a price-drop alert when they fell to $98, and I bought them. Honey's Droplist did the same thing a day later. Rakuten would have paid cashback on the full $140 if I had bought immediately — which is exactly the wrong move when a price drop is coming. Price alerts beat cashback when you can afford to wait.

**The birthday gift.** I needed a gift from a small beauty brand. Honey found a 15 percent code. Piggy found nothing. Rakuten did not cover the store. Capital One Shopping found a 10 percent code. The lesson: on niche and specialty retailers, Honey's 30,000-store database still wins, even after losing 2,000 merchants. Coverage matters most exactly where you expect it least.

**The flight booking.** I booked a $420 flight. None of the four tools did anything useful — no codes, no cashback, no comparison. Travel is the blind spot of this whole category. If you book travel often, none of these extensions earns its keep, and you are better off with a travel-specific tool. I kept all four installed anyway, because they cost nothing when they do nothing.

## The Privacy Question Nobody Can Dodge

Every shopping extension watches what you buy. To find a coupon, Honey has to see the checkout page. To pay cashback, Rakuten has to know which store you visited and what you spent. To compare prices, Capital One Shopping has to read the product pages you browse. That is the deal, and it is not hidden — but the scale of it is easy to miss.

The Honey lawsuit is the clearest warning this category has ever produced. The allegation is not that Honey stole from shoppers. It is that Honey replaced the affiliate tracking of the people who referred the sale — the YouTuber, the blogger, the deal site — and took the commission for itself. If the allegation is true, every "free" coupon Honey found was paid for by someone else's referral fee. A federal court let the case proceed in June 2026, and Rakuten's network has already cut Honey off. Whether you keep Honey installed is now a judgment call, not just a savings calculation.

The practical advice is simple. Do not install more shopping extensions than you need — each one sees your browsing, and each one is another company holding your purchase history. Read the permissions before you install: a coupon extension needs to read checkout pages, but it does not need to read your email or your search history. And if you use Rakuten, remember that the cashback is the product: the stores pay Rakuten a commission for sending you there, and Rakuten shares it with you. That is a fair trade. Just know it is a trade.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Rakuten really free?

Yes. Rakuten costs nothing to install or use. It earns a commission from the retailer when you shop through its links, and shares part of that commission with you as cashback. The $30 welcome bonus requires a first qualifying purchase of $30 or more within 90 days of joining.

### Is Honey safe to use after the lawsuit?

Honey still works, and the lawsuit is about affiliate commissions paid to creators, not about shoppers' money. But the practical impact is real: Rakuten's network cut Honey off from about 2,000 merchants in January 2026, so it finds fewer deals than before, and its user base has reportedly fallen 25 percent. If you keep it, pair it with Rakuten or Capital One Shopping rather than relying on it alone.

### Do I need a Capital One card for Capital One Shopping?

No. Capital One Shopping works with just an email account. You do not need a Capital One card, and the extension's rewards are gift cards, not card points.

### Can I use Rakuten and Honey at the same time?

Yes, and that is the recommended stack. Rakuten pays cashback after the purchase, Honey reduces the price before it. They do not conflict — Rakuten earns its commission while Honey applies the coupon. Just remember to activate Rakuten before checkout, or the cashback does not trigger.

### Why did Honey stop working on some stores?

In January 2026, Rakuten Advertising terminated Honey from its affiliate network, cutting access to roughly 2,000 merchants including Walmart, Sephora, Lego, Dyson and Uniqlo. On those stores Honey can no longer earn or apply its affiliate-linked offers, so it may find nothing where it used to find codes.

### Which coupon extension saves the most money?

For most shoppers, Rakuten returns the most money over a year — about $312 on average, paid as real cash. Honey can save more on a single purchase when it finds a big code, but its rewards are gift cards and its coverage has shrunk. The highest total comes from stacking all three: Rakuten plus Capital One Shopping plus one coupon tester.

## Verdict

Install **Rakuten** first. It is the only tool here that pays real money, it covers 3,500+ stores, and the $30 welcome bonus means it pays you for trying it. The activate-before-you-shop workflow is a habit you have to build, but it is the highest-value habit in this category.

Add **Capital One Shopping** second. It is the only tool that tells you whether the price is good in the first place, and its price-drop alerts saved me more on one purchase than a year of coupons. The gift-card-only rewards are a real limitation, but as a second tool it costs nothing and catches what Rakuten misses.

Keep **Honey** only as a coupon tester, and only alongside one of the other two. Its 30,000-store coverage still wins on niche retailers, but the lawsuit, the lost merchants and the gift-card rewards mean it is no longer the tool to build your savings around.

And if you want a coupon tool with no drama at all, install **Piggy**. It will not save you the most money, but it will never make you wonder who is paying for the free coupon.
