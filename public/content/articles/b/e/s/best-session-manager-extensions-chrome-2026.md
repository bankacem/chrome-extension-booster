## I Spent Three Weeks Losing Tabs on Purpose

I spent three weeks trying to break my browser. Not out of malice — out of self-defence. I keep too many tabs open. Research for one article, a half-finished comparison for another, documentation I swear I will read, a shopping cart I will never check out of, and three YouTube videos paused at the exact moment I got distracted. On a bad day I have sixty tabs open across three windows, and Chrome has crashed on me more than once with all of them inside it.

So I did what I always do when a workflow hurts: I installed four tools that claim to fix it, used each one as my only session manager for several days, and kept score. The four are Session Buddy, Tab Session Manager, Workona and Toby — the four names that come up in every roundup of this category, and the four with the largest installed bases on the Chrome Web Store. I saved sessions, closed windows, killed Chrome mid-task, restarted my laptop, switched projects three times a day, and tried to restore everything exactly as it was. I also counted what each one costs, what it syncs, and what it does with the list of every page I had open — because a session manager knows more about your browsing than almost any other extension you will ever install.

One thing I learned fast: "session manager" means three different things depending on who you ask. For Session Buddy and Tab Session Manager, it means snapshots — save the exact set of open tabs and windows now, restore them later. For Workona, it means workspaces — live project spaces your tabs live inside, saved automatically as you work. For Toby, it means collections — a visual board of saved tab sets you reopen like a bookmark folder with ambition. They all call themselves session managers. They solve overlapping but different problems, and picking the wrong one feels like buying a filing cabinet when you needed a fireproof safe.

## The Four Contenders

**Session Buddy** is the crash-recovery champion, and the most installed tool in this comparison by a wide margin. About 1 million users run it, and it holds a 4.7-star rating on the Chrome Web Store across roughly 25,100 ratings — the highest rating in this category. The idea is simple and it is executed well: Session Buddy keeps a timeline of your browser sessions, takes automatic backups on a schedule you choose, and lets you save any window or the whole browser as a named, searchable session. When Chrome crashes, you open Session Buddy, find the session from ten minutes ago, and restore it window by window or tab by tab. It also manages bookmarks alongside tabs, and it can import and export in more than fifteen formats, including plain text, JSON, CSV, Markdown and HTML. Version 4.1.2 shipped in April 2026, and everything is free with no account required — the developer says the only future paid feature will be an opt-in cloud storage option, and all features today cost nothing.

**Get it:** [Session Buddy — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/session-buddy-tab-bookmar/edacconmaakjimmfgnblocblbcdcpbko)

**Tab Session Manager** is the free, open-source purist of the four. Built by developer Sienori, it runs on Manifest V3, works in Chrome, Firefox and Edge, and does one job with unusual configurability: it saves the state of your windows and tabs, automatically, on an interval you set, and restores them on demand. You can name sessions, tag them, organise them into workspaces, and export them to JSON for backup. Version 7.3.0 shipped in February 2026, and version 7.4.0 followed in July 2026, so it is actively maintained — a point that matters in a category where abandoned extensions are common. There is no paid tier at all. Cloud sync is available as an option for moving sessions between installs, and the developer's own listing states that it does not collect or use your data. Its installed base is far smaller than Session Buddy's — roughly 50,000-plus users by recent roundups — but its users are loyal, and long-time users report years of stable operation even with more than a thousand tabs saved.

**Get it:** [Tab Session Manager — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/tab-session-manager/iaiomicjabeggjcfkbimgmglanimpnae)

**Workona** is not really a session saver. It is a workspace platform that happens to save your sessions as a side effect of how it works, and that difference is the whole pitch. You create named workspaces — one per project, client or thread of work — and each workspace holds its own tabs, notes, saved resources and task list. Switch workspaces and only that project's tabs are visible; the rest hibernate in the background. Every tab in a space is saved automatically, so closing a space and reopening it later restores the session, and signing in syncs your workspaces across computers. Workona has about 200,000 users and a rating around 4.2 stars on its current listing. It is SOC 2 Type II compliant, uses 256-bit AES encryption, and integrates with Google Drive, Asana and Slack. The free plan covers five workspaces; the Pro plan starts at about $6 a month on annual billing, or $7 month to month, and adds unlimited workspaces, team features and 90-day session backups.

**Get it:** [Workona Tab Manager — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/ailcmbgekjpnablpdkmaaccecekgdhlh)

**Toby** is the visual one. It replaces your new tab page with a Trello-style board of saved tab collections, organised into spaces, and you drag tabs onto it like cards. Save a whole window as a session in one click and Toby closes those tabs for you; reopen the collection later, on any device you are signed in on, and the whole set comes back. It even preserves Chrome tab groups, colour included, when you save and restore them. Version 2.0, a ground-up rebuild, shipped in October 2026 with a side-panel view, rebuilt drag and drop, a notes editor and opt-in Smart features that can name collections and sort tabs for you. Toby's rating sits around 4.2 stars, and its listing makes a privacy promise I liked: it only sees the titles, addresses and icons of the tabs you choose to save, never the content of the pages you visit. The catch is the free Starter plan, which caps saved tabs at 60 per workspace, and the fact that Toby needs an account from day one. The paid team plan starts at about $4.50 a user a month.

**Get it:** [Toby: Tab Manager & Session Saver — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/hddnkoipeenegfoeaoibdmnaalmgkpip)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Session Buddy's weakness is that it has no sync. Everything is stored locally, and the developer is explicit about it: there is no cross-device sync today, and the opt-in cloud storage that would add it is still in the future. If you work on a laptop and a desktop, your sessions live on one machine only, and you have to export and import to move them. The second weakness is the one long-time users keep reporting: updates are infrequent, and several recent reviews describe sessions lost or corrupted after Chrome updates. A backup feature that lags up to 24 hours behind your latest changes is not a backup you should trust blindly, and I learned to export important collections separately. Session Buddy also does not preserve Chrome tab group structure the way some rivals do — it saves the tabs, but the grouping can be lost on restore.

Tab Session Manager's weakness is its interface and its learning curve. It is the most configurable tool here, and that is a double-edged sword: auto-save intervals, tags, workspaces, cloud sync settings, tab group handling — there is a lot to set up before it feels invisible, and casual users will bounce off the settings page. The second weakness is restore reliability with tab groups. Long-time users report that tab-group handling sometimes fails on restore and needs manual reopening, which is exactly the kind of fiddling a session manager is supposed to eliminate. And with a much smaller user base than Session Buddy, there are fewer recent reviews to learn from when something goes wrong.

Workona's weakness is weight and lock-in. It is a platform, not a utility: it replaces your new tab page, wants an account, stores your workspace data on Workona's servers, and asks you to reorganise your whole browser around its model. That is a big commitment for a tool whose core job — bringing your tabs back — a free extension does in one click. The free plan's five-workspace cap also fills up fast; anyone juggling more than three projects hits the paywall quickly. And Workona's own listing was last updated in January 2025, which is a long silence for a tool you would build your daily workflow on. If Workona ever shut down or changed its pricing, your workspaces go with it — there is no local-first fallback.

Toby's weakness is that it is a bookmark organiser wearing a session manager's clothes. It does not manage your currently open tabs in real time, and it does not auto-save: if you forget to hit Save Session, the tabs you opened after your last save are simply not in the collection. That manual step is the opposite of what crash recovery needs — a crash never waits for you to save first. Toby also replaces your new tab page entirely, which conflicts with any other new-tab extension you run, and the 60-tab cap on the free Starter plan means a serious research pile hits the limit fast. It is beautiful, and for curating a reference library it is genuinely pleasant to use, but it is the weakest crash-recovery tool of the four.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The Monday-morning crash.** I opened Chrome on a Monday with three windows and 47 tabs from the previous week, and Chrome crashed on launch — the classic update-and-die. Session Buddy had a timeline entry from nine minutes before the crash, and restoring it brought back all three windows, tab by tab. Tab Session Manager's auto-save did the same from its own interval snapshot, though one tab group came back ungrouped and needed manual sorting. Workona restored my spaces, but only the tabs I had placed inside a workspace; the stray window I had never assigned to a space was gone. Toby had nothing at all — I had not saved a session since Friday, so the weekend's tabs were simply lost. For pure crash recovery, the auto-save tools won and the manual-save tool lost.

**The three-project switch.** I run three projects in parallel most days: ExtensionTo articles, a research pile for a second site, and personal admin. With Workona, switching projects meant one click on a workspace name, and the other projects' tabs vanished from view without being closed. That felt genuinely different from the snapshot tools — I never had to decide what to save, because everything was already saved. With Session Buddy and Tab Session Manager, the same switch meant saving the current window as a named session, closing it, then restoring the next project's session. It worked, and it took about thirty seconds, but it was a ritual I had to remember. Toby sat in the middle: I saved each project as a collection, which was pleasant to look at, but reopening a collection opened every tab at once, which hammered my memory until I learned to open tabs one at a time.

**The 60-tab research pile.** I built a research pile for this article itself: sixty tabs of reviews, store listings and pricing pages across two windows. Session Buddy swallowed it without complaint, and its full-text search across saved sessions found a half-remembered review in seconds. Tab Session Manager handled it too, and its JSON export gave me a backup file I could keep outside the browser. Toby hit its free-plan wall at 60 saved tabs per workspace and made me split the pile across two workspaces, which defeated the point of having one research set. Workona handled the pile fine inside a workspace, but I could feel the weight: the workspace dashboard plus sixty suspended tabs made Chrome's new tab page noticeably slower to open.

**The laptop-to-desktop handoff.** I started work on my laptop and wanted to continue on my desktop the same evening. Workona and Toby did this properly: I signed in on the desktop and my spaces and collections were there. Tab Session Manager's optional cloud sync moved my sessions across too, once I had enabled it. Session Buddy could not do it at all — I exported a JSON file, moved it over, and imported it, which worked but is not something you want to do every day. If you genuinely work on two machines, the local-only tool is the wrong tool, however good it is on one machine.

**The accidental close.** I closed the wrong window — the one with twelve tabs for an article I was halfway through writing — and Chrome's own "reopen closed window" did not bring it back because I had already opened and closed another window after it. Session Buddy's history showed the closed window exactly as it was an hour earlier, and I restored the twelve tabs in two clicks. Tab Session Manager's auto-save had the same window. Toby had the tabs only because I had saved that project as a collection that morning; the three tabs I had opened since were gone. This is the scenario that made me keep an auto-save tool installed permanently: the one time you need it, it pays for every day you forgot it was there.

## What Your Sessions Reveal — and Where They Go

A session manager is one of the most privileged extensions you can install. Every tab you save is a list of the pages you visit: your bank, your webmail, your medical portal, the job application you have not told anyone about, the gift you are researching for your partner. A session is not just URLs. It is a map of your life, timestamped. So the question that matters most about these four tools is not what they save — it is where the save goes, and who else can read it.

Session Buddy is the most private tool here by design. Everything stays on your device by default, there is no account, no tracking, and the developer says it does not sell your data or run ads. The trade-off is the one I already described: local-only means no sync, and your sessions die with your hard drive unless you export them. That is a fair trade for most people, and it is the reason I trust Session Buddy with sensitive sessions.

Tab Session Manager is nearly as private. It is open source, its developer states that it does not collect or use your data, and by default everything is local. The optional cloud sync is the exception: once you turn it on, your session data — your URLs and titles — leaves your machine for the sync provider. That is a reasonable feature, but turn it on deliberately, not by accident, and never sync a session that contains tabs you would not want stored on someone else's server.

Workona and Toby are account-based by design, and that changes the deal. Your workspaces and collections live on their servers, encrypted in transit and at rest — Workona cites SOC 2 Type II compliance and 256-bit AES encryption, and Toby says it never sells your data and only sees the titles, addresses and icons of tabs you choose to save. Both promises are credible, and both still mean your browsing structure sits on a third party's infrastructure. I used both happily for project tabs and research. I did not put banking sessions, medical portals or anything with personal documents into either of them, and I would not.

Two practical rules came out of my testing. First, export your sessions regularly, whatever tool you use — a JSON or Markdown export is your escape hatch if the extension breaks, the company changes direction, or your browser profile corrupts. Session Buddy and Tab Session Manager make this easy; do it weekly. Second, close sensitive tabs before you save a session you intend to sync. A session you save to the cloud is a session that exists in two places, and the second place is not yours.

## What Else Is Out There

The four tools above are not the only options, and two alternatives are worth knowing about. [OneTab](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/onetab/chphlpgjalnkjbhppphlopegllbchp) is the simplest tab tool on the Chrome Web Store, with more than 2 million users and a 4.4-star rating. It does exactly one thing: one click collapses every open tab into a single list, freeing your memory, and you restore tabs one at a time or all at once. It is not a session manager — it has no named sessions, no auto-save and no sync — but if your real problem is that Chrome is eating 8 GB of RAM right now, OneTab solves it faster than any tool in this comparison.

Chrome itself is the other alternative. The browser's built-in tab groups, plus its own session restore after a crash, cover the lightest version of this job for free, with no extension at all. What Chrome cannot do is name a session, keep several of them side by side, or restore a window you closed three days ago. That gap is exactly what the tools above exist to fill.

## Key Takeaways

- Session Buddy is the best session manager for crash recovery and for a single computer: about 1 million users, a 4.7-star rating, free with no account, but no cross-device sync.
- Tab Session Manager is the best free, open-source session manager: auto-save on your schedule, tags and workspaces, optional cloud sync, and it works in Firefox and Edge too — but the settings take time to learn.
- Workona is the best session manager for people juggling several projects at once: live workspaces with automatic saving and sync, but the free plan caps at five workspaces and the listing has not been updated since January 2025.
- Toby is the best session manager for visual thinkers who curate research collections: beautiful boards, tab groups preserved with colour, and sync across devices — but it only saves when you remember to save, and the free plan caps at 60 tabs per workspace.
- Auto-save beats manual save for crash recovery every time. A tool that only saves when you click is a tool that has nothing saved on the day you need it most.
- Your sessions are a map of your browsing. Keep sensitive tabs out of cloud-synced sessions, and export your sessions regularly whatever tool you use.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Session Buddy free?

Yes. All of Session Buddy's features are free and it needs no account. The developer says the only future paid feature will be an opt-in cloud storage option; everything available today costs nothing. The trade-off is that there is no cross-device sync yet, so your sessions live on one machine unless you export them.

### Does Tab Session Manager sync between Chrome and Firefox?

It can. Tab Session Manager is built by the same developer for Chrome, Firefox and Edge, and its optional cloud sync moves sessions between installs. The Firefox version is a separate install, and tab group saving is Chrome-only, because Firefox does not have native tab groups. By default everything is stored locally.

### Which session manager is best for multiple projects?

Workona, if you want live workspaces where each project has its own tabs, notes and resources, saved automatically and synced across computers. Tab Session Manager is the free alternative: named sessions with tags and workspaces do the same job, but switching projects is a manual save-and-restore ritual instead of a single click.

### Can a session manager recover tabs after Chrome crashes?

Yes, if it auto-saves. Session Buddy keeps a timeline of sessions with scheduled automatic backups, and Tab Session Manager auto-saves on an interval you set, so both can restore the windows and tabs you had open before the crash. Toby cannot, unless you saved the session manually before the crash — which is exactly what a crash prevents you from doing.

### Is it safe to sync my sessions to the cloud?

Mostly, with one rule: do not sync sessions that contain sensitive tabs. A synced session stores your URLs and page titles on someone else's server. Workona is SOC 2 Type II compliant with 256-bit AES encryption, and Toby states it never sells your data, but neither changes the fact that the data leaves your machine. Keep banking, medical and personal-document tabs out of synced sessions, and export a local backup regularly.

### What is the difference between a session manager and OneTab?

OneTab collapses your open tabs into a single list to free memory — it is a one-click clear-out, not an archive. A session manager saves named, restorable snapshots of your windows and tabs, with auto-save, search and organisation. Use OneTab when Chrome is slow right now; use a session manager when you want to close a project on Friday and reopen it exactly as it was on Monday.

## Verdict

Install **Tab Session Manager** if you want the best all-round session manager and you are willing to spend ten minutes learning its settings. It is free, open source, actively maintained, auto-saves on your schedule, syncs across devices when you ask it to, and works in Firefox and Edge as well as Chrome. It is the only tool here that is free, private by default, and genuinely cross-browser, and its July 2026 update shows a developer who is still shipping.

Install **Session Buddy** if you use one computer, you want the simplest and most proven crash recovery, and you never want to think about accounts or subscriptions. A million users and a 4.7-star rating are not an accident. Just remember its two rules: export your important sessions yourself, because there is no cloud backup, and do not expect your sessions to follow you to a second machine.

Choose **Workona** if you juggle five or more projects and you want your browser organised the way your work is organised — one workspace per project, everything saved automatically. Check the [current pricing](https://workona.com/pricing/) before you commit, since the free plan's five-workspace cap fills up fast, and keep your own exports in case the platform ever changes direction.

And if you think in pictures rather than lists, and your sessions are really research collections you curate rather than windows you need to resurrect after a crash, **Toby** is the one to try. Just save early, save often, and do not trust it as your crash-recovery plan.
