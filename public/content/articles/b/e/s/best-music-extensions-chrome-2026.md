## I Spent Two Weeks Turning My Browser Into a Stereo

It started with a laptop that refused to be loud. I was listening to a playlist on the Spotify web player, volume slider maxed out in the browser and on the system, and the music still sat there, thin and apologetic, like it was coming from the next room. My headphones were fine. My phone played the same songs loud enough to annoy the neighbours. The browser was the weak link, and that annoyed me enough to spend two weeks fixing it properly.

I tested four extensions that dominate the Chrome Web Store for browser music and audio: Volume Master, Airs Audio, Equalizer for Chrome Browser and Advanced Audio Equalizer. I ran each one as my daily audio tool for several days, across the same test material — a bass-heavy hip-hop playlist, an acoustic live session, a quiet classical recording, a handful of podcasts, and a late-night film — on the Spotify web player, YouTube and YouTube Music. I listened on laptop speakers, on cheap earbuds and on decent over-ear headphones, and I kept notes on what each tool did to the sound, how much effort it took to use, and what it asked for in return.

One thing I learned fast: there are two different jobs here, and most people confuse them. Making music louder is not the same as making music sound better. A volume booster takes whatever audio the tab is playing and pushes it past 100%, which fixes quiet recordings and weak laptop speakers. An equalizer reshapes the sound itself — more bass, clearer vocals, less harsh treble — which fixes music that sounds flat or muddy. The best tools in this comparison do both. The worst do one and pretend it is the other. Keep that distinction in mind for everything that follows, because it is the whole story of this category.

## The Four Contenders

**Volume Master** is the simplest tool here, and by far the most installed. Around 7 million people run it, and it holds a 4.8-star rating on the Chrome Web Store across roughly 49,700 reviews. That is not a close race: it has more users than the other three contenders combined. Open the popup and you get a slider for the current tab, from 0% to 600%, plus a list of every tab currently playing audio so you can jump to the noisy one with a single click. There is a voice boost and a bass boost tucked in, and keyboard shortcuts let you jump straight to 0–600% by pressing keys 0 to 6. It is completely free, with no ads, no account and no paid tier. The developer, Peta Sittek, states in the listing that the extension collects no user data and processes audio entirely inside the browser. For the one job most people actually want — my browser is too quiet, make it louder — nothing else here is as fast. Click, drag, done.

**Get it:** [Volume Master — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/volume-master/jghecgabfgfdldnmbfkhmffcabddioke)

**Airs Audio** is the phoenix of this comparison. It is the successor to Ears: Bass Boost, EQ Any Audio, the beloved equalizer that was removed from the Chrome Web Store in late August 2026 after years of service and a long struggle with Chrome's Manifest V3 changes. Ears had a devoted following — people used it for the better part of a decade — and Airs Audio is the same idea rebuilt from scratch by a new developer for modern Chrome. Open it on any tab with sound and the current tab is equalized immediately; you drag interactive nodes on a curve to shape bass, mids and treble exactly how you want, and a built-in spectrum visualizer shows the live audio levels and your EQ changes in real time. It works as a bass booster, a treble reducer, a vocal enhancer or a full equalizer, on any site including Spotify, YouTube, Twitch and Instagram. It is free to install with an in-app Airs Pro upgrade that unlocks extra themes — one reviewer mentioned paying $4.99 — and it carries the Chrome Web Store's Featured badge. Because it is a newer listing, it does not yet have the enormous user counts of the old Ears, but the reviews from former Ears users are strikingly warm: people who lost their favourite equalizer overnight are finding their way back to the same workflow here.

**Get it:** [Airs Audio - Bass Booster & Equalizer — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/airs-audio-bass-booster-e/ibhmgglejliilciffebcbnklceoblcbe)

**Equalizer for Chrome Browser** is the preset-lover's equalizer. It gives you a 10-band graphic equalizer with plus or minus 12 dB of adjustment per band, and 21 ready-made presets covering the genres that matter — Bass Booster, Hip-Hop, Electronic, Vocal Amplifier and more — plus preamp gain, bass and volume boosting, surround sound effects, and the ability to edit and save your own custom configurations. Around 500,000 users run it, and it holds a 4.7-star rating across roughly 9,200 reviews. What I liked most in testing was the speed of it: pick a preset and the sound changes instantly, no dragging curves around, no learning curve. It also keeps CPU usage low, which matters more than it sounds — an equalizer that stutters your music while it processes it has failed at its only job. It is free, and for someone who wants to tap "Hip-Hop" and get on with their day, it is the most direct route from flat browser audio to something with shape.

**Get it:** [Equalizer for Chrome Browser — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/equalizer-for-chrome-brow/abikfbojmghmfjdjlbagiamkinbmbaic)

**Advanced Audio Equalizer** is the effects rack of the group. Where the others give you volume and EQ, this one adds reverb, chorus, 3D audio, a compressor and background noise controls, all with presets, plus volume boosting up to 800% — the highest ceiling of any tool in this comparison. Your settings can also sync across devices, so the curve you tuned on your desktop follows you to your laptop. In testing, the compressor was the surprise star: it evens out the difference between whisper-quiet dialogue and sudden loud passages, which makes it genuinely useful for films and podcasts, not just music. The 3D audio effect is fun on headphones, widening the soundstage in a way that makes live recordings feel bigger. It is free to install from the Chrome Web Store. The trade-off is complexity — this is the one tool here where the popup can feel like a small mixing desk, and if all you wanted was a louder tab, that is more panel than you need.

**Get it:** [Advanced Audio Equalizer — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/advanced-audio-equalizer/ibefjagfpcdefiapiapnhleogifimklb)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Volume Master's weakness is that it is a volume tool first and everything else second. The bass boost and voice boost exist, but they are simple toggles, not an equalizer — you cannot shape the sound, you can only push it. If your music sounds muddy, Volume Master will give you louder muddy music. And there is a second, quieter problem: the extension has not been updated in a long time. Its store listing was checked in late October 2026 with the last update well over a year behind it, and independent extension trackers flag it as abandoned. It still works perfectly on current Chrome, and seven million users is a strong vote of confidence, but an unmaintained extension is a risk in a browser that keeps changing its rules — Ears was killed by exactly that kind of change. Finally, at 600% the distortion is real. Past about 250–300% on my laptop speakers, the sound started to crackle and flatten; the slider goes to 600%, but your speakers do not want it to.

Airs Audio's weakness is youth. It is a rebuild, not a continuation, and it shows in two ways. First, it has a much smaller user base and far fewer reviews than the tools it competes with, so you are trusting a newer developer with a newer codebase rather than a decade of public track record. Second, the free version gates its themes behind Airs Pro, and at least one paying user reported the upgrade not working for them — a small thing, but it is the only paid complaint I found across all four tools. There is also the honest question of longevity: the developer rebuilt Ears because Google's platform changes killed it, and those platform changes have not stopped. Airs Audio is the best-sounding equalizer I tested, but it is also the one I would most want to see still maintained a year from now.

Equalizer for Chrome Browser's weakness is the opposite of Airs Audio's: it is easy to use and hard to fine-tune. The presets are excellent, but the 10-band sliders are small and fiddly in the popup, and there is no spectrum visualizer to show you what you are actually doing to the sound — you are adjusting blind and listening for the result. It also has no per-tab volume list like Volume Master's, so if you have three tabs playing audio you cannot manage them from one place. And like Volume Master, it is a browser-only tool: none of your EQ settings follow you to your phone, your music app or your system audio. What you tune here stays in Chrome.

Advanced Audio Equalizer's weakness is that it tries to do everything, and the interface shows it. Reverb, chorus, 3D audio, compressor, noise controls, EQ, volume boost — the popup is crowded, and the first time I opened it I spent five minutes just figuring out which panel did what. The effects are also easy to overdo: reverb on a podcast makes the host sound like they are broadcasting from a bathroom, and the 3D effect on already-wide electronic music turns into mush. This is the tool with the most to learn and the most ways to make your audio worse before it gets better. It is also the only one of the four where I could not verify a large, long-standing user base from the store listing, so you are taking the feature list on trust more than on crowd wisdom.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The quiet laptop.** My starting problem, and the one that began this whole test. Spotify web player, volume maxed everywhere, music still too quiet on laptop speakers. Volume Master fixed it in ten seconds: I dragged the slider to 180% and the playlist finally sounded like music instead of a rumour. Airs Audio and the equalizers could not help here on their own — shaping a quiet signal does not make it louder. If your problem is volume, start with a booster, not an EQ. This is the single most common use case in this category, and it is why Volume Master has seven million users.

**The bass-heavy commute playlist.** I queued a hip-hop playlist on YouTube Music and tested the equalizers head to head. Airs Audio won this round clearly: I dragged the low end up, pulled the harsh upper mids down slightly, and watched the spectrum visualizer confirm what my ears were hearing. The bass had weight without swallowing the vocals. Equalizer for Chrome's Hip-Hop preset got me 90% of the same result in one tap, which is the better deal if you do not enjoy dragging nodes around. Volume Master's bass boost, by comparison, just made everything boomier — louder low end, but no control over which low end.

**The mumbling podcast host.** A true-crime podcast where the host recorded in what sounded like a closet, voice buried under room tone. Equalizer for Chrome's Vocal Amplifier preset lifted the voice out of the mud in one click, and Airs Audio's vocal-enhancer approach did the same with a little more fiddling. Advanced Audio Equalizer's compressor was the dark horse here: it evened out the host's habit of whispering one sentence and nearly shouting the next, so I stopped riding the volume keys. Volume Master was useless for this — louder mumbling is still mumbling.

**The late-night film.** A quiet drama on a streaming site, dialogue barely audible, then a sudden loud scene that woke the house. Advanced Audio Equalizer won this one outright. The compressor narrowed the gap between the whispers and the shouts, and the volume boost to about 150% brought the dialogue up to a comfortable level without the loud scenes becoming an event. I also tried the 3D audio effect on a concert film, and on headphones it genuinely widened the stage. This is the scenario that justifies Advanced Audio Equalizer's crowded interface: when the job is not just music, the extra tools earn their place.

**The YouTube rabbit hole with wild volume swings.** Three tabs open: a music video mastered loud, a quiet acoustic cover, and a vlog recorded at half volume. Volume Master's per-tab list was the only interface that handled this gracefully — I could see all three tabs, jump to the quiet one, boost it, and leave the loud one alone, all without touching the system volume. The equalizers all work per tab too, but none of them show you the full picture of what is playing where. If your listening habit is ten tabs deep, per-tab management matters more than any preset.

## What to Watch Out For: Privacy, Copycats and Your Ears

Every extension in this article needs serious permissions to do its job. To change the audio of any tab, an extension must be able to read and modify data on the websites you visit, and to capture audio it needs tab-capture access. That is normal for this category and it is stated plainly on each listing — Volume Master, for example, lists tabCapture, activeTab, tabs and storage — but it means you should only install these from the official Chrome Web Store, from the developer you actually checked, and never from a random download site.

The copycat problem is real and specific. Volume Master's popularity has attracted imitators with similar names — listings like "Volume Pro", "Sound Master Plus" or "Master Your Sound" — and some copycat audio extensions have been flagged by security researchers for data collection or affiliate-link injection. Before you install, check three things: the developer name (Peta Sittek for Volume Master), the user count, and the rating. If any of those do not match what this article describes, you are looking at the wrong listing. The same applies to the "Sound Booster" name, which has at least four different listings by different developers on the store — a name alone is not an identity.

The Ears story is the cautionary tale for the whole category. A much-loved equalizer with years of happy users was removed from the store in August 2026 after Google's Manifest V3 changes broke it, and its users woke up to a dead extension. Airs Audio exists because someone rebuilt the idea. The lesson is not to avoid new tools — it is to avoid depending on one tool. If your whole listening setup lives inside a single extension, keep a second one installed as a backup, and prefer tools whose developers are still shipping updates. Volume Master's long silence on updates is the one thing that genuinely worries me about an otherwise excellent tool.

Then there is the safety warning that belongs in every article like this one and usually gets skipped: boosting audio past 100% can damage your hearing and your speakers. The Web Audio API amplifies digitally before the sound reaches your hardware, and at 300% or 600% a sudden loud passage can be physically painful through headphones. I kept my boosts modest — 150–200% for quiet laptop speakers, never above 250% — and I would suggest you do the same. Start low, raise it in small steps, and take the headphones off before you drag a slider to its maximum. Your ears do not have an undo button.

And one more boundary worth stating plainly: none of the four contenders here are downloaders or rippers. They change how audio sounds while it plays in your browser; they do not save it, convert it or strip it out of the site it came from. If an extension promises to download the music you are listening to, that is a different category with different legal and security problems, and it is not what this article is about.

## What Else Is Out There

The four tools above are not the only options. [Sound Booster for Chrome](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/sound-booster-for-chrome/bhcielkibmaibfcjjmolgpapmlpooaph?hl=en-US) is the strongest alternative I found: up to 600% boost, equalizer presets for bass and movies, a fullscreen mode that keeps the boost active while you watch, an active-tab mode that mutes every tab except the one you are watching, and customizable keyboard shortcuts — all free, with audio processed locally and no personal data collected. It is a genuine rival to Volume Master on features, though with a much smaller user base, which is why it sits here as an alternative rather than a contender.

The other name you will keep seeing is Volume Booster, the extension that pushes audio up to 1000% and claims over two million weekly users. I did not make it a contender because its extreme ceiling is a liability dressed as a feature — at 1000% the distortion is severe, and the extra headroom buys you nothing usable over 600%. If you install it, treat it as a 200% tool that happens to go further, and never let it near your headphones at full tilt.

## Key Takeaways

- Volume Master is the best music extension for most people: free, 4.8 stars, about 7 million users, 600% boost and per-tab control, but it is a volume tool, not an equalizer, and it has not been updated in over a year.
- Airs Audio is the best equalizer I tested: a full visual EQ curve, live spectrum visualizer, and the rightful successor to the much-loved Ears, but it is a newer listing with a smaller user base and its themes sit behind a paid upgrade.
- Equalizer for Chrome Browser is the fastest route to better sound: 21 presets, 10-band EQ and low CPU use, but the sliders are fiddly and there is no visualizer or per-tab list.
- Advanced Audio Equalizer is the most capable tool here: 800% boost, reverb, chorus, 3D audio and a genuinely useful compressor, but the interface is crowded and it is easy to make your audio worse before it gets better.
- Louder is not better. A booster fixes quiet speakers; only an equalizer fixes flat or muddy sound. Know which problem you have before you install anything.
- Never push a boost past about 250% on headphones, and always check the developer name before installing — copycat audio extensions are a real problem on the store.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Volume Master free?

Yes. Volume Master is completely free, with no ads, no account and no paid tier. It boosts any tab up to 600%, controls volume per tab, and the developer states it collects no user data. Its only cost is that it has not been updated in over a year, so keep an eye on whether it keeps working as Chrome changes.

### Can a Chrome extension really make my music sound better, or just louder?

Both, but different tools do different halves. A volume booster like Volume Master only makes audio louder — it cannot improve the quality of the sound itself. An equalizer like Airs Audio or Equalizer for Chrome reshapes the frequencies, which is what actually makes music sound better: more bass, clearer vocals, less harsh treble. If your music sounds flat, you need an EQ. If it sounds fine but too quiet, you need a booster.

### What happened to Ears: Bass Boost, EQ Any Audio?

Ears was removed from the Chrome Web Store in August 2026 after years of service, largely because Chrome's Manifest V3 changes broke how it worked. Its developer community mourned it — it had been a favourite for the better part of a decade — and Airs Audio is a from-scratch rebuild of the same idea for modern Chrome, by a new developer. If you loved Ears, Airs Audio is the closest thing to it that exists today.

### Is it safe to boost volume to 600%?

Technically the extension allows it, but you should not do it. Past about 250–300% on real speakers and headphones, digital amplification introduces distortion and crackling, and a sudden loud passage at 600% through headphones can be physically painful and can damage your hearing. I kept my boosts at 150–200% for quiet laptop speakers and never went above 250%. Start low and raise it in small steps.

### Do these extensions work on Spotify, YouTube and YouTube Music?

Yes. All four contenders work on any tab playing audio in Chrome, including the Spotify web player, YouTube, YouTube Music, Twitch, SoundCloud and Instagram. They process the tab's audio stream, so they are not tied to any one service. They do not work outside the browser — your phone app, your desktop music player and your system audio are untouched.

### Do music extensions collect my listening data?

It depends on the extension. Volume Master states in its listing that it collects no user data and processes audio entirely inside your browser. Airs Audio and Sound Booster for Chrome make similar local-processing claims. But every audio extension needs broad permissions to capture tab audio, so always read the privacy disclosure on the store listing before installing, and avoid any audio extension that does not disclose its data practices at all.

## Verdict

Install **Volume Master** if you want the best music extension for most people. It is free, it has seven million users and a 4.8-star rating behind it, and it solves the problem most people actually have — a browser that is too quiet — in ten seconds with one slider. The per-tab list is the feature you will use every day once you have it. Just remember its two limits: it makes audio louder, not better, and it has not been updated in over a year, so pair it with a backup.

Install **Airs Audio** if you care about how music sounds, not just how loud it is. The visual EQ curve and live spectrum visualizer make it the most precise and most enjoyable equalizer I tested, and it is the rightful heir to Ears for everyone who lost their favourite EQ in August 2026. It is my pick for anyone who listens on headphones and wants their playlists to actually sound shaped.

Choose **Equalizer for Chrome Browser** if you want better sound with zero learning curve — tap the Hip-Hop preset and you are done. And choose **Advanced Audio Equalizer** if your listening is not just music: its compressor and effects make it the best tool here for films, podcasts and late-night watching.

My own setup, after two weeks of testing, is Volume Master plus Airs Audio running side by side: one to make quiet tabs loud, one to make good music sound great. That pair covers every scenario in this article, costs nothing, and takes less than a minute to install. If you install only one, make it Volume Master — but if you love music, the second one is where the magic is.
