## Quick answer

The best spreadsheet tool for a small business depends on the work, not on a universal ranking. **Google Sheets** is often a practical starting point when several people need to edit in a browser. **Microsoft Excel** is a strong choice for advanced analysis, existing Microsoft workflows, and desktop-heavy work. **Airtable** can be a better fit when one set of records must be viewed as a grid, calendar, gallery, kanban board, or another structured layout.[1] [2] [3]

Before choosing, define the task, the shape of the data, who needs access, whether offline work matters, which integrations are essential, and what the total cost will be for the actual team. A free plan or familiar interface is not useful if it creates manual work or blocks the workflow later.

## The five questions to answer first

A short needs assessment prevents most spreadsheet-tool mistakes:

1. **What job must the file support?** Budgeting, inventory, project tracking, sales reporting, and customer records have different requirements.
2. **How many people work on it?** A solo owner, a two-person team, and a distributed finance team may need different sharing and permission controls.
3. **How structured is the data?** A flexible grid may be enough for a simple list, while linked records and repeatable views can help with operational data.
4. **Will people work without a connection?** Check how offline access is configured instead of assuming that a cloud product is either always online or fully offline.
5. **Which integrations are non-negotiable?** Confirm that the tool connects to the accounting, CRM, storage, reporting, or automation systems the business already uses.

These questions are more reliable than choosing the product with the longest feature list. A small business usually benefits from fewer workarounds and clearer ownership of the data.

## Excel, Google Sheets, Airtable, or another tool?

The following comparison is a starting point, not a promise that one product is best for every company.

| Tool | Best fit | Important strength | Watch before choosing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Microsoft Excel | Analysis-heavy work and Microsoft-centered teams | Deep formula, modeling, and desktop capabilities | Advanced features and collaboration depend on plan, platform, and file location. |
| Google Sheets | Browser-first teamwork and lightweight reporting | Simple sharing and simultaneous editing | Offline access requires configuration; complex workbooks may need careful governance. |
| Airtable | Operational records that need different views | Grid plus calendar, gallery, kanban, timeline, list, and gantt views | It is a database-style workflow tool, so formula and spreadsheet compatibility should be tested. |
| LibreOffice Calc | Local, open-source desktop work | Offline editing without a subscription | Collaboration and integrations may require more setup than cloud-first options. |
| A dedicated accounting or CRM system | Regulated or repeatable business processes | Purpose-built records, workflows, and permissions | It may cost more and be less flexible for ad hoc analysis. |

Microsoft describes Excel as offering free online collaborative spreadsheets and worksheet sharing, while Google documents browser-based offline setup for Sheets. Airtable’s documentation describes multiple views over the same table, which is useful when a small-business team needs operational layouts rather than only cells and formulas.[1] [2] [3]

## Microsoft Excel: choose it for analysis and compatibility

Excel is a sensible choice when the business already works in Microsoft 365, depends on advanced formulas or models, or needs a familiar desktop application. It can also be useful when files must move through established finance, reporting, or operations processes.

The main question is not whether Excel has enough features. It is whether the team can keep formulas understandable, control versions, and share the workbook in the way the business requires. Online collaboration exists, but the exact experience depends on the account, platform, storage location, and workbook features.

Excel is usually a stronger fit when:

- the team already relies on Microsoft 365;
- analysis requires advanced formulas, models, or desktop workflows;
- offline-first work is common; or
- existing templates and external processes are built around `.xlsx` files.

It may be less suitable when the primary requirement is frictionless browser collaboration with people who do not use the same Microsoft environment.

## Google Sheets: choose it for browser collaboration

Google Sheets is often a practical starting point for small teams that need to share a file quickly and work in a browser. Google’s documentation also explains how to configure offline access for recently opened documents and individual files, subject to browser, extension, storage, and account conditions.[2]

That does not make Sheets a universal replacement for Excel. A team should test its important formulas, imports, exports, permissions, and reporting needs with a representative workbook. Establish naming rules, ownership, sharing boundaries, and a backup process before a critical file becomes a business system by accident.

Google Sheets is usually a strong fit when:

- several people need to edit or review a file from different locations;
- the workflow already uses Google Drive and other Google Workspace products;
- the business wants a browser-first starting point; or
- offline access can be configured for the team’s actual devices.

For AI-assisted formula work, keep that as a separate use case. The [AI formula generator guide](/blog/best-ai-formula-generator-for-google-sheets-1) can support that narrower question without turning this tool-selection article into an AI product review.

## Airtable: choose it for structured records and multiple views

Airtable is useful when the data behaves more like a set of operational records than a single analysis sheet. Its official documentation lists grid, form, calendar, gallery, kanban, timeline, list, and gantt views, and explains that one table can have multiple views.[3]

That model can help a small business maintain one source of records while giving different people a layout suited to their work. For example, an inventory team may need a grid, a marketing team may need a calendar, and a manager may need a filtered overview. The team should still verify formulas, exports, automation, permissions, and plan limits against its actual workflow.

Airtable is a candidate when:

- the business needs different views over the same records;
- filtering, grouping, and operational status matter as much as calculations;
- forms or structured intake reduce manual data entry; or
- a process should be more repeatable than a free-form shared spreadsheet.

It is not automatically the best choice for complex financial modeling or work that must remain compatible with traditional spreadsheet files.

## When a spreadsheet is no longer the right tool

Spreadsheets are flexible, but flexibility can hide process problems. Consider a database-style application or dedicated business system when the team repeatedly needs:

- linked records such as customers, orders, products, and invoices;
- controlled workflows with clear stages and owners;
- role-based permissions and an audit trail;
- repeatable forms and validation rules; or
- reporting that must remain consistent while many people edit the underlying data.

This does not mean a spreadsheet has failed. It means the business has moved from a calculation problem to a data-management problem. A hybrid approach can work: keep analysis in a spreadsheet while a dedicated system owns the source records.

## Offline access: verify the setup, not just the marketing page

Offline support is a capability with prerequisites. Google’s official help says that computer users need an initial internet connection, a supported browser, the Google Docs Offline extension, and sufficient device storage before working offline with Sheets.[2] Microsoft Excel may also be used through desktop and online experiences, but the appropriate choice depends on the business’s accounts, devices, and workflow.

Ask these questions before promising offline work:

| Check | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Which files are available offline? | A team may not have every file cached or synchronized. |
| Which device and app are being used? | Browser, desktop, and mobile experiences can differ. |
| What happens after conflicting edits? | Offline changes may need synchronization and review. |
| Is local data protected? | A downloaded or cached file needs device-level protection. |
| Can the team recover an older version? | Recovery reduces the cost of accidental edits or sync problems. |

## Cost and integration checks for a small business

Avoid comparing only the advertised starting price. Calculate the likely cost for the number of editors, storage needs, administration, required add-ons, automation, support, and migration. Plans and included features can change by region and account type, so confirm current details on the provider’s pricing page before committing.

Integration is equally important. A tool that exports cleanly to accounting or reporting systems may save more time than a product with an impressive but unused feature list. Test one real workflow from input to report rather than judging integrations from a logo directory.

A practical pilot should include:

1. one representative dataset with realistic column names and data volume;
2. one collaboration task with the people who will actually edit it;
3. one export or integration into the next business system;
4. one offline or recovery test if those requirements matter; and
5. one permission review before sensitive data is imported.

## Browser extensions and spreadsheet workflows

Chrome extensions can support a browser-based workflow, but they should remain secondary to choosing the right spreadsheet platform. An extension cannot fix a poor data model, missing permissions, or a tool that does not support the team’s required workflow.

Use extensions narrowly and review their permissions before granting access to business data. For example, a formula-oriented extension may be relevant to a specific Sheets task, while a screenshot tool may help document a report. These are separate workflow decisions, not reasons to choose one spreadsheet platform over another.

## A simple selection scorecard

Score each candidate from 1 to 5 against the business’s real requirements. Weight the criteria that affect risk or daily work rather than giving every feature equal importance.

| Criterion | Weight | What a high score means |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Fits the main workflow | 5 | The tool matches the business’s most frequent job. |
| Team collaboration | 4 | People can share, edit, review, and control access clearly. |
| Data structure | 4 | Tables, records, formulas, or views match the information model. |
| Offline and recovery | 3 | The team can work and recover data under its actual conditions. |
| Integrations | 3 | Important handoffs work without repetitive copying. |
| Administration and privacy | 4 | Ownership, permissions, and data handling are understandable. |
| Total cost | 3 | The complete cost is acceptable for the team and usage. |

Multiply each score by its weight, then compare the total with notes from the pilot. A scorecard does not remove judgment, but it makes the decision easier to explain and revisit.

## FAQ

### What is the best spreadsheet tool for a small business?

There is no universal winner. Google Sheets is a strong starting point for teams that value browser-based collaboration, Excel is a strong fit for advanced desktop analysis and Microsoft workflows, and Airtable is useful when records need multiple structured views. Choose by task, data model, teamwork, offline needs, and total cost.

### Is Excel or Google Sheets better for a small business?

Choose Google Sheets when simultaneous browser collaboration and simple sharing are central. Choose Excel when advanced desktop analysis, existing Microsoft workflows, or offline-first work matter more. Both can be appropriate when the business has different needs across teams.

### Can a small business use Google Sheets offline?

Yes, Google documents offline access for Sheets when it is configured in a compatible browser or app. On a computer, Google says offline setup requires an initial connection, a supported browser, the Google Docs Offline extension, and available storage.[2]

### When should a small business choose Airtable instead of a spreadsheet?

Airtable is worth considering when the team needs one set of records displayed through different views such as grid, calendar, gallery, kanban, timeline, list, or gantt. It is a useful option for structured workflows, not automatically a replacement for every formula-heavy spreadsheet.[3]

### Should a small business choose a spreadsheet or a database?

Use a spreadsheet when calculations, flexible tables, and quick analysis are the priority. Consider a database-style tool when records, relationships, permissions, repeatable workflows, and multiple views are more important than a free-form grid.

## Verdict

For most small businesses, the right choice is the tool that matches the daily workflow and remains understandable as the team grows. Start with a representative pilot, compare collaboration and data structure, verify offline and recovery requirements, test one important integration, and calculate the full cost for the actual users.

Choose Google Sheets for a browser-first collaborative workflow, Excel for analysis-heavy or Microsoft-centered work, and Airtable when structured records need multiple operational views. Revisit the decision when the business’s data-management needs become more complex than a grid can safely support.
