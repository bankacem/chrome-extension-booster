## I Spent Two Weeks Reading the Internet in Languages I Do Not Speak

I read German engineering forums, French news sites, Japanese product manuals and a Spanish academic paper last month, and I do not speak any of those languages well enough to do it without help. That is the honest use case for a translate extension: not a holiday phrasebook, but the daily work of reading the web in languages you half-know. I installed four of the most-used translation extensions in Chrome, used them for two weeks on real pages — news articles, documentation, PDFs, YouTube subtitles, shopping sites — and kept notes on what each one actually did, where each one failed, and what each one costs when the free tier runs out.

The first thing I learned is that the category has changed more in the last year than in the five years before it. Google removed the last Manifest V2 extensions from the Chrome Web Store on August 31, 2026, so everything installable today is a Manifest V3 extension, and several old favourites quietly stopped being maintained years ago. The second thing I learned is that the official Google Translate extension is not the tool most people think it is any more: it lost full-page translation, and its store rating has fallen to around 3.0 stars. The extension that translates the most naturally is not the one with the most users, and the one with the most users is not the one I would install first.

## The Four Contenders

**Google Translate** is the official extension from Google, and it is the biggest in this group by an absurd margin: roughly 38 million users, against about 4 million for the next contender. It is completely free, with no paid tier, no sign-up and no limits, and it was updated on October 15, 2026 to version 2.0.18. Highlight any text on a page and a small popup translates it instantly; double-click a word and you get a dictionary entry with parts of speech. It covers more than 100 languages through Google's engine, and it needs no account at all. But two things about it surprise people who install it expecting the Google Translate they know from the website. First, full-page translation was removed from the extension — translating a whole page is now a feature of Chrome itself, not of the extension, which only handles selections. Second, the store rating has fallen to about 3.0 stars across roughly 44,900 ratings, with the complaints concentrated on reliability and on pages that simply fail to translate. The output is also frequently literal: it gets the meaning across, but it reads like a machine wrote it, because a machine did. One more trap: there is a lookalike listing called "Google Translate Extension for Chrome" that states in its own text that it is not affiliated with Google. Only the official listing is Google's.

**Get it:** [Google Translate — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/google-translate/aapbdbdomjkkjkaonfhkkikfgjllcleb)

**DeepL: translate and write with AI** is the quality pick, and the gap is visible on the first page you translate. The extension has about 4 million users and a 4.5-star rating across roughly 12,700 ratings, the highest rating in this group, and it was last updated on March 23, 2026. Where Google Translate gives you a word-for-word rendering, DeepL gives you a sentence that sounds like a person wrote it — especially in European language pairs, where it is consistently the most natural output I tested. The free tier covers 50,000 characters a month, which sounds generous until you translate two long articles and hit the wall. The paid tiers were renamed in 2026: Individual costs around $8.74 a month on annual billing ($10.49 monthly), Team around $28.74 per user a month, and Business around $57.49 per user a month. Paying unlocks unlimited full-page translation, automatic page translation, glossary and terminology control, and DeepL Write. It works inside Gmail, Google Docs and Slides, YouTube, WhatsApp, LinkedIn and X, which is where I ended up using it most — translating an email thread in place rather than copying it out. The weaknesses are real: it covers only 30+ languages, so Arabic and many Asian languages are weaker or missing, and the free tier's terms prohibit submitting confidential or personal data, which matters if you translate work documents.

**Get it:** [DeepL — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/deepl-translate-and-write/cofdbpoegempjloogbagkncekinflcnj)

**Mate Translate** is the feature-rich one, and the one whose recent reviews worried me most. It has about 400,000 users, and its rating is disputed between trackers: some show 3.4 stars across about 6,600 ratings, others show a lifetime average around 4.3 stars — but both agree that the average of the last 100 reviews is only about 2.3 stars, which is a recent collapse, not a long-term verdict. It was last updated on June 23, 2026. The feature list is the longest here: one-click full-page translation, PDF translation, Netflix-subtitle translation, pronunciation with phonetic transcription and transliteration, a dictionary and a curated phrasebook with cross-device sync, and 103 languages on the listing itself (the vendor's marketing claims more, up to 248 across all platforms — the numbers conflict, so take the listing's figure). The free tier covers selection lookups; Mate Plus, available as a subscription or a one-time lifetime licence with a 7-day trial, unlocks sync, dark mode and the extras. There is no public price list on the vendor's site — third-party reports cite around $5.99 a month or $49.99 a year, which I could not confirm, so check the in-app purchase screen before paying. The weaknesses are practical: users report full-page translation spinning forever, the extension conflicts with Cloudflare human-verification checks and can lock you out of protected sites until you disable it, and people report trouble restoring lifetime licences after switching machines.

**Get it:** [Mate Translate — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/mate-translate-%E2%80%93-translat/ihmgiclibbndffejedjimfjmfoabpcke)

**ImTranslator: Translator, Dictionary, TTS** is the free workhorse, and the only tool here with no paid plan at all. It has about 900,000 users and a 4.4-star rating across roughly 5,300 ratings, and it was last updated on August 3, 2026. It is really four tools in one: a popup translator, a pop-up bubble, an inline translator that replaces text on the page, and one-click full-page translation — plus a dictionary, text-to-speech and a translation history. Its best trick is that you can switch between three translation engines — Google, Microsoft Bing and Translator — so when one engine mangles a sentence you can try another without leaving the page. The interface is available in 22 languages and there are keyboard shortcuts for everything. The weaknesses: it has no engine of its own, so your text is sent to whichever provider you pick; the interface looks dated and one November 2026 review said it "has not changed in years"; and it shows donation and update popups when the browser launches, which gets old fast. But for the price — nothing, donation-supported — it is hard to argue with.

**Get it:** [ImTranslator — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/imtranslator-translator-d/noaijdpnepcgjemiklgfkcfbkokogabh)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Google Translate's weakness is that it does less than people assume and worse than its reputation suggests. The removal of full-page translation from the extension is the big one: if you install it expecting to translate whole articles, you will be disappointed, because that job now belongs to Chrome's built-in translator. The output quality is the second problem — it is accurate enough to understand a page, but it reads like a machine translation, with awkward word order and idioms translated literally. And the 3.0-star rating is not a fluke: it reflects real reliability complaints, pages that fail to translate, and a product that Google has clearly stopped investing in as an extension.

DeepL's weakness is the paywall around the features that make it great. The free tier's 50,000 characters a month sounds like a lot until you translate a 3,000-word report and realise you have used most of it. Full-page translation without limits, automatic page translation and the glossary are all paid features, so the free extension is really a selection translator with a very good engine. The language coverage is the other limit: 30+ languages is fine if you work in European languages, but it is a real gap for Arabic, Hindi, Korean and several others that Google covers easily.

Mate Translate's weakness is reliability, and it is getting worse rather than better. A last-100-reviews average of 2.3 stars is the kind of number that means something broke recently, and the reports agree: full-page translation hanging, conflicts with Cloudflare checks that lock you out of sites, and licence restoration problems. The feature list is the best in this group on paper, but a feature that does not work is worse than a feature that does not exist, because you build a habit around it and then it fails on the page that matters.

ImTranslator's weakness is that it is a front-end, not a translator. It sends your text to Google, Microsoft or Translator's engines, so its quality is their quality, and its privacy is their privacy. The dated interface is a smaller complaint, but the donation popups on browser launch are a daily irritation, and the tool's language count is inconsistent even on its own listing — 170 in one place, 100+ in another — which tells you how much care goes into the details.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The German engineering forum.** I needed to follow a thread about a CNC controller error, full of technical jargon and compound nouns. DeepL handled it best by a clear margin — the sentences came out in natural English, and the technical terms were right. Google Translate got the meaning across but the word order was clumsy enough that I had to re-read sentences. Mate Translate's full-page translation worked on this page, which was a point in its favour, and ImTranslator's Google engine matched Google Translate's own output, as expected. For technical reading, DeepL won.

**The French news article.** A 2,000-word article from a French newspaper, translated in full. Google Translate's extension could not do this at all — no full-page translation — so I used Chrome's built-in translator instead, which is a different tool and a reminder of how the extension has shrunk. DeepL translated the whole page on the free tier until I hit the character limit mid-article, which is exactly the kind of thing that happens once and then you pay. Mate Translate did the whole page in one click, and ImTranslator did too. For long articles, the free full-page tools are Mate and ImTranslator; DeepL is better but capped.

**The Japanese product manual.** A PDF manual for a camera, translated from Japanese. Mate Translate was the only one of the four that translates PDFs directly in the browser — that alone decided this scenario. DeepL does not cover Japanese as well as its European pairs, and Google's PDF handling means downloading and uploading to the Translate website. If you read PDFs in other languages, Mate is the only real option in this group, reliability complaints notwithstanding.

**The Spanish academic paper.** A dense paper with long sentences and academic vocabulary. DeepL was clearly the best here: it preserved the sentence structure and the academic register, while Google's version flattened everything into simpler, flatter English. ImTranslator with the Bing engine produced the worst result of the four, with two sentences that changed the meaning. I switched ImTranslator to the Google engine and it improved immediately, which is the practical value of having three engines to choose from.

**The YouTube subtitle test.** I watched a German tutorial with auto-generated subtitles and translated a comment thread underneath. DeepL works inside YouTube directly, translating comments and the interface in place, which felt seamless. Google Translate handled selected text fine. Mate Translate's Netflix-subtitle feature is its party trick, but I do not watch Netflix in the browser often enough for it to matter. For YouTube, DeepL and Google are the practical choices.

## What to Watch Out For: Privacy and the Manifest V3 Clean-Out

A translation extension sees everything you ask it to translate, which means it can see a lot: your emails, your documents, your search results. DeepL's free tier explicitly prohibits submitting confidential or personal data, and that is a rule worth respecting — do not paste a contract or a medical letter into a free translator. ImTranslator sends your text to whichever engine you select, so your privacy is Google's or Microsoft's privacy policy, not ImTranslator's. Mate Translate syncs your phrasebook across devices, which means your translation history lives on its servers. Google Translate needs no account, which is a genuine privacy advantage: nothing you translate is tied to an identity.

The other thing to watch is the state of the category after the Manifest V3 clean-out. Google removed the last Manifest V2 extensions from the store on August 31, 2026, and several once-popular translation extensions have not been updated since 2022 — S3.Translator, for example, still installs, but its last version is from 2022 and its user numbers are contradictory across trackers, which is a sign nobody is maintaining it. Stick to extensions updated in 2026. And check the publisher before you install: there are lookalikes with similar names, and the only official Google Translate extension is the one published by Google, with the listing ID ending in `aapbdbdomjkkjkaonfhkkikfgjllcleb`.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If the four above do not fit, the two alternatives I would look at first are Immersive Translate and S3.Translator. Immersive Translate shows bilingual side-by-side translations of web pages, PDFs, EPUBs and video subtitles, has about 3 million Chrome users and a 4.6-star rating, and was named one of Chrome's Best Extensions of 2024. It is free with a daily request limit, and Pro costs around $14.99 a month or $119.88 a year. It is the best option if you are learning a language and want to see the original and the translation together. S3.Translator is free, multi-engine and has keyboard shortcuts for everything, but it looks unmaintained since 2022 and its numbers are contradictory across trackers, so I would only install it if you specifically want its shortcut-driven workflow and you accept the risk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which translate extension is the most accurate?

DeepL, for European languages. Its output reads like a person wrote it, where Google Translate reads like a machine. For Asian languages and for languages DeepL does not cover, Google Translate is the fallback, and ImTranslator lets you switch between Google, Bing and Translator engines to compare.

### Can I translate a whole web page with the Google Translate extension?

Not any more. Full-page translation was removed from the extension, and it now only translates selected text. Whole-page translation is a built-in Chrome feature — right-click the page and choose Translate. If you want full-page translation inside an extension, use Mate Translate or ImTranslator, or pay for DeepL.

### Is DeepL free?

Partly. The free tier gives you 50,000 characters a month, which covers short selections and a couple of articles. Full-page translation without limits, automatic page translation and the glossary are paid: Individual costs around $8.74 a month on annual billing.

### Does Mate Translate still work in 2026?

It works, but its recent reviews are poor — about 2.3 stars across the last 100 reviews, with reports of full-page translation hanging and conflicts with Cloudflare verification checks. Its feature list is the longest in this group, but I would install it knowing the reliability complaints, not in spite of them.

### Which translate extension is best for PDFs?

Mate Translate is the only one of these four that translates PDFs directly in the browser. The others need you to download the PDF and upload it to a translation website, or copy the text out by hand.

### Is it safe to translate private documents with these extensions?

Be careful. DeepL's free tier prohibits confidential or personal data. ImTranslator sends your text to Google, Microsoft or Translator, so your privacy is their policy. Google Translate needs no account, which is the best privacy setup here, but the text still goes to Google's servers. For sensitive documents, use a paid plan with a data-processing agreement, or translate locally.

## Verdict

Install **DeepL** if you care about translation quality and you mostly read European languages. It is the only tool here whose output I stopped double-checking, and the in-place translation inside Gmail and Docs is genuinely useful. Accept the 50,000-character free cap, and pay for Individual if you translate long documents regularly.

Install **ImTranslator** if you want the best free translator. Four tools in one, three engines to switch between, full-page translation, no paid plan and no account. Accept the dated interface and the donation popups.

Install **Google Translate** if you want the simplest option and you only translate short selections. It is free, needs no account and covers 100+ languages, but do not expect it to translate whole pages or to read naturally.

Install **Mate Translate** only if you specifically need PDF or Netflix-subtitle translation, and go in with your eyes open about the reliability complaints. Its feature list is the best on paper; its recent reviews are the worst in this group.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep DeepL — the tool whose translations I trusted enough to stop checking, even with the character cap.
