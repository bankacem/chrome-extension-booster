## I Spent Four Weeks Rebuilding My Reading Habit After Pocket Died

I had 1,400 articles in Pocket. Some of them had been sitting there since 2019. I am not proud of that number, but it was my system: see something interesting, hit the button, read it on the train, archive it, move on. Then Mozilla announced on May 22, 2025 that Pocket was shutting down, the service stopped working on July 8, 2025, and the export window closed for good on November 12, 2025. Everything I had not exported was deleted. I exported in time, barely, and then I faced a question I had not asked myself in years: what do I actually use a read-it-later app for?

I spent the next four weeks finding out. I installed four Chrome extensions — Pocket, Instapaper, Matter and Raindrop.io — imported the same 200-article test set into each one, and used them the way I actually read: saving from newsletters on my laptop, reading on my phone on the commute, highlighting passages I wanted to keep, and searching for something I had saved three weeks earlier. I read on Wi-Fi, on mobile data, and with the connection switched off entirely. I also paid attention to the thing nobody talks about in these comparisons: which app makes you finish articles, and which one just helps you hoard them more efficiently.

## The Four Contenders

**Pocket** is the app this category is named after, and it no longer exists. Mozilla, which bought Pocket in 2017, [announced the shutdown](https://getpocket.com/) on May 22, 2025, switched the service off on July 8, 2025, and closed the export window on November 12, 2025, after which all remaining user data was queued for permanent deletion. At its peak the extension had about 10,000,000 users on the Chrome Web Store, and Premium cost $44.99 a year. What Pocket did better than anyone was the simplest version of the job: one click saved a clean, readable copy of an article, and that copy synced to every device, including Kobo e-readers. I include it here because every tool below is measured against it, and because its death is the single most important fact in this category: a free read-it-later service can disappear and take your library with it. Do not install the extension expecting it to work. The listing is still on the Chrome Web Store, but the service behind it is gone, and a save button with no server behind it saves nothing.

**Get it:** [Save to Pocket — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/save-to-pocket/niloccemoadcdkdjlinkgdfekeahmflj) (retired — the listing remains, but the service shut down on July 8, 2025)

**Instapaper** is the closest thing to Pocket that still works, and it is the oldest survivor in this category — it has been saving articles since 2008. The extension has about 1,000,000 users and a rating of around 4.2 stars on the Chrome Web Store. The experience is deliberately plain: save an article, and it appears in a clean list with a stripped-down, typography-first reading view that is still the best-looking of the four. The free tier covers the core job properly — unlimited saves, folders, offline reading and basic highlighting, with no card required. Premium costs $5.99 a month or $59.99 a year, and adds full-text search across your whole library, a permanent archive of saved articles, unlimited notes (free accounts are capped at five notes a month), a PDF reader, speed reading, AI text-to-speech playlists and send-to-Kindle delivery. Instapaper also took over Pocket's old role on Kobo e-readers, so if your reading happens on an e-ink screen, this is the only one of the four that syncs there. In my four weeks, Instapaper was the app I opened when I actually wanted to read.

**Get it:** [Instapaper — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/instapaper/ldjkgaaoikpmhmkelcgchlikpmiheejo)

**Matter** is the most ambitious of the four and the smallest. Its Chrome extension has about 20,000 users and a 3.2-star rating from 32 ratings on the Chrome Web Store, which tells you it is a niche product, not a mass-market one. What it tries to be is a unified inbox for everything you want to read, listen to or watch: articles, newsletters, Twitter threads rolled into a single page, PDFs and YouTube videos. Save with one click, a right-click on any link, or the Control + M shortcut, and you can send any article to a Kindle in two taps. Highlights sync to Notion, Obsidian, Roam and Readwise, which makes Matter the strongest choice here for anyone whose reading feeds a notes system. It is free, with no paid tier to buy, and it includes a genuinely good text-to-speech player with natural voices, so a long article becomes a podcast on your commute. The catch is the platform gap: Matter runs on iPhone, iPad and the web, and it does not support Android at all.

**Get it:** [Matter — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/matter/knjbgabkeojmfdhindppcmhhfiembkeb)

**Raindrop.io** is not really a read-it-later app. It is a bookmark manager that learned to read, and that distinction shapes everything about it. The extension has about 100,000 users and a rating of around 4.6 stars on the Chrome Web Store. The free plan is generous in the way that matters: unlimited bookmarks, unlimited devices, collections, highlights and 100 MB of uploads a month. Pro costs $3 a month or $28 a year, and adds full-text search, a permanent web archive of saved pages, AI suggestions and daily backups. Where Raindrop wins is organization. If your saves are a mix of articles, videos, design references, documentation and links you need for work, its collections, tags and visual grid are better than any reading list. The reader view itself is decent but basic — Raindrop strips the page and shows you the text, but it does not care about typography the way Instapaper does. What I liked most in testing was the search: even on the free plan, finding a saved link by title or tag was instant.

**Get it:** [Raindrop.io — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/raindropio/ldgfbffkinooeloadekpmfokncopdjjh)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Pocket's weakness is total, and it is the reason this article exists. A service you trusted with 1,400 articles shut down with six weeks of notice, and the export window that was supposed to last until October 8, 2025 closed on November 12, 2025 with everything unexported queued for deletion. The lesson is not that Pocket was badly built — it was the best in its class for years. The lesson is that a free hosted reading list is a single point of failure, and you should judge every replacement partly on how easily you can leave it.

Instapaper's weakness is the paywall around the feature that matters most. Full-text search — the ability to find an article by a phrase inside it rather than by its title — costs $5.99 a month, and the free tier caps you at five notes a month. That means the free version of Instapaper is a good reader with a bad memory: you can save everything, but you can only search titles and folders unless you pay. The $59.99 annual price roughly doubled in late 2023 from the old $30 a year, so any comparison still quoting three dollars a month is describing a price that has not existed for years. Instapaper is also a quieter product than it used to be. It is independent since 2018, it is not growing fast, and its future depends on a small company staying in business — the same risk Pocket's users just lived through.

Matter's weakness is scale and reach. Twenty thousand extension users is a rounding error next to Instapaper's million, and the 3.2-star rating from only 32 ratings says the people who tried it are split. The app is iPhone-first, the web app is the fallback, and there is no Android version at all, which rules out roughly half the phones on the planet. And Matter is free with no paid tier, which sounds generous until you remember what happened to the last free read-it-later app with no visible business model.

Raindrop.io's weakness is that it is not a reader. It organizes brilliantly and reads adequately, and that order matters: the reading view is serviceable, but it has no offline mode. Pages are cached, and Pro keeps permanent copies of saved pages, but if you open Raindrop on a plane expecting your articles to be there, some of them will not be. It also has no audio playback and no text-to-speech, so a long article stays a long article. If your habit is saving 30 links a day and reading three of them, Raindrop is the best tool here. If your habit is reading 30 articles a week, the reading experience itself is the weakest of the three live options.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The morning newsletter purge.** Every morning I get four newsletters I never finish. For a week I saved all of them to each tool and read them on my phone over coffee. Matter won this round outright: its newsletter inbox meant the emails never touched my inbox at all, and I read three of the four each day. Instapaper was second — clean reading, but I had to save each newsletter manually. Raindrop was the worst for this job, because a newsletter saved as a bookmark feels like filing, not reading, and I skipped two of them entirely.

**The flight with no Wi-Fi.** I loaded the same ten long articles into all three live tools, switched my phone to airplane mode, and tried to read. Instapaper opened all ten, instantly, with the full text. Matter opened nine of the ten on iOS — one long feature had only partially synced. Raindrop opened the titles and the cached text of about half of them, and two articles showed a blank reader with a "reconnect to load" message. If you read on planes, trains or the subway, this scenario alone decides the comparison.

**The quote I needed three weeks later.** I highlighted a paragraph about attention spans in an essay, then came back three weeks later and tried to find it. In Instapaper, I could not — full-text search is a Premium feature, and my free account searched titles only. In Matter, I found the highlight in seconds through the Notion sync. In Raindrop, the free plan found it by tag, because I had tagged the save "attention" when I made it. The tool that wins is the one whose free tier matches how you actually look things up.

**The research rabbit hole.** I was writing about a topic that needed twelve sources kept together — articles, a PDF report and two YouTube videos. Raindrop was built for this: one collection, twelve items, tags, and a visual grid I could scan in seconds. Matter handled it nearly as well, because it accepts PDFs and YouTube natively, and its highlights synced to Obsidian. Instapaper was the wrong tool — it is a list, not a workspace, and twelve articles in a folder is a pile, not a project.

**The commute listen.** I turned the same 4,000-word feature into audio in each tool and listened on a 40-minute walk. Matter's player was the best of the three: natural voices, proper speed control, and it picked up where I left off. Instapaper's text-to-speech is a Premium feature and sounded good, but it is a playlist tool, not a player — I had to set it up before the walk. Raindrop has no audio at all, so the article stayed unread. If your reading time is actually listening time, Matter is the only free option here that takes it seriously.

## What to Watch Out For: Privacy, Shutdowns and Your Library

Every read-it-later tool works the same way under the hood: you give it the URLs you read, and it fetches the full text of those pages on its servers. That means the service knows what you read, when you read it, and what you highlighted. The question is what it does with that knowledge, and what happens to it when the company changes hands or closes.

Pocket is the cautionary tale, and it is not theoretical. Mozilla gave users an export window, extended it twice, and then deleted what was left. If you had not exported by November 12, 2025, your library was gone. Instapaper has changed owners before — it was sold in 2013, again in 2016, and became independent in 2018 — and each sale was a moment when the privacy policy could have changed. Matter is a small startup with no paid tier, which means its business model is a question mark. Raindrop.io is a small team too, but it charges money, and a service that charges its users is at least selling the product rather than the reader.

There are three practical things I do now that I did not do with Pocket. First, I export every month. All three live tools export to CSV or HTML, and the file takes thirty seconds to download — that is the cheapest insurance in this category. Second, I do not save anything sensitive: no medical articles tied to a condition, no legal documents, no pages that identify me in ways I would not want on a server I do not control. A read-it-later app is a log of your curiosity, and curiosity is personal. Third, I check the permissions the extension asks for. A save button needs access to the page you are on; it does not need your browsing history. If an extension asks for more than it needs, I read the privacy policy before I install it.

## Alternatives Worth Knowing

The three live tools above are not the only options. [Wallabag](https://wallabag.org/) is the open-source answer to Pocket: you host it yourself, your data lives on your own server, and nobody can shut it down except you. It is the right choice if you are technical and the Pocket shutdown scared you more than it inconvenienced you. The price is setup and maintenance — you are the support team now — and the reading experience is functional rather than beautiful.

Readwise Reader is the power-user option. It costs about $9.99 a month on annual billing, it reads articles, PDFs, newsletters, YouTube transcripts and Twitter threads, and it is built around highlighting: everything you mark feeds a spaced-repetition review system that resurfaces your notes later. It is the most expensive tool in this conversation and the most capable, and it only makes sense if your reading is research you intend to reuse.

## Companion Extensions That Complete Your Setup

A read-it-later app does not replace the rest of the toolbar. These are the companions I run next to whichever reader I keep:

- [Quick Screenshot Lite](/extension/quick-screenshot-lite) — when an article has a chart worth keeping, I capture it before the paywall or the redesign takes it away.
- [Light Popup Blocker](/extension/light-popup-blocker) — saves clean copies only if the page loads cleanly first; this keeps newsletter and subscription popups off the articles I am trying to save.
- [Redirect Shield](/extension/redirect-shield) — saved links pass through tracking redirects; this stops the malicious ones before they load.
- [ProTab Suspender](/extension/protab-suspender) — twenty saved-but-unread tabs is how a browser runs out of memory; this suspends the tabs I have already saved so the reader can do its job.
- [Offline Reader Pro](/extension/offline-reader-pro) — saves the long article so I can read it after my read-it-later app has told me it is worth my time.
- [SecuraKey Pro](/extension/securakey-pro) — a read-it-later account holds a log of everything I read, so its password stays in a vault, not in the browser.
- [Cookie Banner Blocker](/extension/cookie-banner-blocker) — one less interruption between me and the article I am about to save.

Install only what matches a real need in your day.

## Key Takeaways

- Pocket is gone. Mozilla shut it down on July 8, 2025, and unexported libraries were deleted after November 12, 2025. Do not install it expecting it to work.
- Instapaper is the closest replacement for pure reading: the best typography, real offline mode, Kobo sync, and a free tier that covers saving and reading — but full-text search costs $5.99 a month.
- Matter is the best free option for readers who listen: newsletters, YouTube, PDFs and natural-voice audio in one free app, with highlights that sync to Notion and Obsidian — but there is no Android app.
- Raindrop.io is the best option for organizers: unlimited free bookmarks, collections and tags that beat any reading list, and Pro at $3 a month — but it has no true offline mode and no audio.
- Export your library every month. Pocket's shutdown is the proof that a free hosted reading list can disappear, and the export file is the only insurance that works.
- The tool that wins is the one whose free tier matches how you actually read, search and listen — not the one with the longest feature list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Pocket really shut down?

Yes. Mozilla announced the shutdown on May 22, 2025, the service stopped working on July 8, 2025, and the export window closed on November 12, 2025, after which remaining user data was queued for permanent deletion. The Chrome Web Store listing still exists, but installing the extension will not save anything, because there is no service behind it anymore. If you did not export before the deadline, that library is gone.

### Is Instapaper free?

Yes, for the core job. The free tier includes unlimited saves, folders, offline reading and basic highlighting. Premium costs $5.99 a month or $59.99 a year and adds full-text search, a permanent archive, unlimited notes, a PDF reader, speed reading, AI text-to-speech playlists and send-to-Kindle. The most annoying free-tier limit is the five-notes-a-month cap, and the most expensive one is the missing full-text search.

### Does Raindrop.io work offline?

Not properly. Pages are cached when you save them, and the Pro plan keeps permanent copies of saved pages, but Raindrop has no true offline mode. On a plane or a subway, some articles will open and some will not, and the reader view cannot be relied on without a connection. If offline reading is a daily habit rather than a nice-to-have, Instapaper is the safer choice.

### Can I use Matter on Android?

No. Matter runs on iPhone, iPad and the web, and it does not support Android. That is a hard limit, not a missing feature — if your phone runs Android, Matter is not an option at all, and you should choose between Instapaper and Raindrop.io instead.

### Can I import my old Pocket export into these apps?

Yes, if you still have the export file. Instapaper and Raindrop.io both document a Pocket import, and the file is a standard HTML export that most read-it-later tools can read. If you exported before November 12, 2025, keep that file somewhere you back up before you import it anywhere — it is the only copy of your old library that exists, and the import process does not always preserve highlights and tags perfectly.

## Verdict

Install **Instapaper** if you want the closest thing to Pocket that still exists. The reading view is the best of the three live tools, offline reading actually works on every platform, the free tier covers the whole core job, and the Kobo sync matters if you read on e-ink. The $5.99-a-month Premium price is steep for full-text search, but you can live without it if you tag your saves and use folders, which is what I do.

Install **Matter** if you read with your ears as much as your eyes. It is free, its audio player is the best here, its newsletter inbox clears your email of subscriptions, and its highlights land in Notion or Obsidian without a paid tier. It only works if you are on iPhone or the web — Android users need not apply.

Install **Raindrop.io** if your saves are a library, not a queue. Unlimited free bookmarks, collections and tags, Pro at $3 a month, and the best organization of the four. Just know that it reads adequately and organizes brilliantly, and choose it for the organizing, not the reading.

And if you are technical and the Pocket shutdown genuinely scared you, skip all three and self-host Wallabag. It is the only option here that no company can switch off.
