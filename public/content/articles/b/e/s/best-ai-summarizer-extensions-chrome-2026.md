## I Spent Three Weeks Summarising Everything Before I Read It

I read too much. That is not a boast; it is a scheduling problem. Every day my browser fills with articles I should read, papers I need to understand, and videos someone swore were worth an hour. I cannot read them all, and the ones I skim I do not really understand, so I started summarising everything first — and then I started testing the tools that do the summarising, because a bad summary is worse than no summary. It tells you the article said something it did not say, and you walk away confident and wrong.

For three weeks I ran three AI summarizers through the same stack: long news features, dense research papers, forty-minute YouTube videos, and PDF reports. I compared each summary against the original, checked the facts it claimed, and noted where it invented a detail that was never there. The three tools are built for three different readers: Perplexity is for the person who wants an answer, SciSpace is for the researcher drowning in papers, and Glasp is for the learner who watches and reads and wants to keep what they found.

## The Three Contenders

**Perplexity** is the official extension from the AI search company, and it changed while I was testing it. The current listing, relaunched on October 31, 2026, is now called Perplexity Computer: a browser agent that can drive your browser and carry out multi-step tasks, on top of the side-panel summaries and answers the earlier version offered. The extension itself is free; the Pro plan costs $17 a month on annual billing, down from $20, and the free tier now includes 3 Pro searches a day, down from 5. Perplexity's strength is that it is a search engine first: it answers with sources, and every claim in a summary carries a citation you can click. When I asked it to summarise a news article, it told me what the article said and linked the sentence it was based on, which is the single most useful habit in this whole category.

**Get it:** [Perplexity — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/perplexity/pninphncmkphpjgfffammoenglghcjii)

**SciSpace** is the only tool here built for one job and one reader: research papers. Its tagline is "do hours of research in minutes", and it works across a corpus of more than 270 million papers. Open a paper or a PDF and SciSpace explains it in plain language, summarises each section, answers questions about the methods and the results, and extracts the key findings with citations. The Premium plan costs $12 a month on annual billing and includes 1,200 credits. In my testing it was the only tool that could read a methods section and tell me, honestly, whether the study's sample size supported its conclusion. If your reading list is papers, nothing else here comes close.

**Get it:** [SciSpace — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/scispace-do-hours-of-rese/cipccbpjpemcnijhjcdjmkjhmhniiick)

**Glasp** is the learner's tool, and the most popular summarizer in this group: more than 2 million users and a 4.3-star rating on the Chrome Web Store. Its extension, YouTube Summary with ChatGPT & Claude, summarises YouTube videos, web articles and PDFs, powered by ChatGPT, Claude, Mistral AI and Gemini, with full transcripts and timestamps you can click to verify any claim. It is free with in-app purchases, and the free tier includes 20 Chat credits a month. Glasp's real idea is social learning: your highlights and notes live on a public profile by default, so other learners can follow what you are reading. That is either the best feature here or the worst one, depending on how you feel about your reading list being public.

**Get it:** [YouTube Summary with ChatGPT & Claude by Glasp — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/youtube-summary-with-chat/nmmicjeknamkfloonkhhcjmomieiodli)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Perplexity's weakness is that it is moving. The October 2026 relaunch turned a clean side-panel summarizer into a browser agent, and the new "Computer" capability is powerful but also new, which means the extension I tested in week one was not quite the extension I tested in week three. The free tier also shrank while I watched: Pro searches per day fell from 5 to 3, and the Pro price, while down from $20 to $17 a month, is still the most expensive paid plan in this group for a summarizer. And Perplexity is a search tool first: ask it to summarise a paper's methods section and it gives you a good answer, but it does not understand research the way SciSpace does.

SciSpace's weakness is narrowness. It is superb on papers and ordinary on everything else. Point it at a news article or a YouTube video and it works, but it is clearly outside its comfort zone, and its summaries of general web content are plainer than Perplexity's or Glasp's. The credit system is also the least transparent here: 1,200 credits a month on Premium sounds precise, but different actions cost different numbers of credits, and I never found a clear table of what each one costs. If your reading is mostly papers, none of this matters. If it is mostly everything else, SciSpace is the wrong tool.

Glasp's weakness is the one the others do not have: your highlights are public by default. I highlighted a passage in a medical article during testing and only later realised it was visible on my Glasp profile to anyone who looked. You can change the setting, but the default is public, and a tool that publishes your reading by default should say so louder than it does. The other weakness is dependence: Glasp's summaries are powered by ChatGPT, Claude, Mistral and Gemini, which means the quality of your summary depends on which model answers, and the free tier's 20 Chat credits a month run out quickly if you summarise long videos.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The forty-minute video.** I gave all three the same forty-minute talk and asked for the five key points. Glasp won, and it was not close: it returned a timestamped summary where every point linked to the exact moment in the video, and I verified three of the five against the transcript in under a minute. Perplexity gave a good summary but no timestamps. SciSpace gave a serviceable summary and clearly wished I had given it a paper instead.

**The dense research paper.** I fed all three a paper with a methods section I did not understand. SciSpace won outright: it explained the method in plain language, flagged that the sample size was small for the claim being made, and answered my follow-up questions about the results with citations to specific sections. Perplexity gave a decent plain-language summary but missed the sample-size problem entirely. Glasp summarised the abstract well and the rest thinly.

**The long news feature.** I gave all three a 5,000-word feature and asked what it actually concluded. Perplexity won: its summary carried clickable citations for every claim, and one of its citations caught a sentence I had misread. Glasp's summary was accurate but shorter than I wanted. SciSpace's was correct and dull, and it read like it was summarising a paper, because that is what it is built to do.

**The PDF report.** A 60-page industry report, the kind nobody reads and everybody cites. SciSpace handled the PDF best, extracting the key findings table and explaining the methodology. Perplexity was nearly as good and faster. Glasp summarised the first ten pages well and got thinner as the document went on, which is a pattern I noticed twice: Glasp is strongest on short and medium content.

**The "explain it to me" test.** I asked each tool to explain a technical topic to me as if I knew nothing about it, using the same article as the source. Perplexity won for clarity and for citing its sources. SciSpace won for depth, when the source was a paper. Glasp won for the experience: its transcript-plus-summary view let me read the summary, click a timestamp, and watch the exact moment the speaker said it, which is the closest any of these tools comes to actually teaching you.

## What to Watch Out For: Permissions and Privacy

A summarizer reads what you read. That is the job, and it means the tool sees the article, the paper, the video transcript — and sends it to an AI company's servers. Do not summarise anything confidential: a contract, a medical report, an unreleased document. Read those yourself.

Glasp deserves a specific warning, because its default is different from the others. Your highlights and notes are public by default. Check your Glasp profile after your first session, change the visibility setting if you want your reading private, and assume that anything you highlighted before you changed it was visible. It is a good feature for public learning and a bad surprise for private reading.

The other thing to watch is hallucination, which is the polite word for a summary inventing a fact. In my testing, all three invented at least one detail across three weeks — a number that was not in the original, a claim the author never made. The defence is built into two of these tools: Perplexity's clickable citations and Glasp's timestamps let you verify a claim in seconds. Use them. A summary you do not verify is a rumour with good formatting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which AI summarizer is best for YouTube videos?

Glasp, by a clear margin. Its extension returns a timestamped summary with the full transcript, and every point links to the exact moment in the video so you can verify it. Perplexity also summarises videos, but without timestamps.

### Can SciSpace summarise regular articles?

Yes, but it is not its strength. SciSpace is built for research papers, and its summaries of general web content are plainer than Perplexity's or Glasp's. If your reading is mostly papers, it is the best tool here; if it is mostly news and blogs, pick Perplexity or Glasp.

### Is Perplexity's extension free?

Yes, the extension is free, and the free tier includes 3 Pro searches a day. The Pro plan costs $17 a month on annual billing and unlocks unlimited Pro searches and the browser-agent features.

### Are my Glasp highlights really public?

Yes, by default. Glasp is built around social learning, so your highlights and notes live on a public profile unless you change the visibility setting. Check your profile after your first session and change it if you want your reading private.

### Do AI summarizers make things up?

Sometimes, yes. In my testing all three invented at least one detail across three weeks — a number that was not in the original, or a claim the author never made. Verify important claims against the source, using Perplexity's citations or Glasp's timestamps.

### Which summarizer is best for students?

Glasp for everyday reading and video lectures, because it is free and its timestamped summaries make revision fast. SciSpace if your reading is research papers, because nothing else here understands a methods section.

## Verdict

Install **Perplexity** if you want the best general summarizer. It answers with sources you can click, it summarises articles, PDFs and videos, and the free extension covers everyday use. The Pro plan at $17 a month is worth it if you research daily.

Install **SciSpace** if your reading list is research papers. Nothing else here explains a methods section, checks whether the evidence supports the conclusion, or searches a 270-million-paper corpus. At $12 a month, Premium is the cheapest paid plan in this group.

Install **Glasp** if you learn from video. More than 2 million users, the best YouTube summaries I tested, free with 20 Chat credits a month — and change the public-by-default highlight setting the moment you install it.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep Perplexity — the tool whose citations caught me misreading an article, which is exactly what a summarizer is for.
