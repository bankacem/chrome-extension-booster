## I Spent Three Weeks Automating the Boring Parts of My Browser

I copy things from one tab to another for a living. Not all day, but enough of the day: a lead from LinkedIn into a spreadsheet, a price from a supplier's site into an email, the same five clicks on the same dashboard every morning. It is the kind of work that takes ninety seconds and feels like ten minutes, and it is exactly the work browser automation is supposed to kill.

For three weeks I ran two web automation extensions through my real repetitive work, each for several days at a time, on the same jobs: scraping a list of contacts from a page into a sheet, monitoring a page for a change, filling the same form with different data, and turning a messy page into a clean summary I could send to a client. I counted how many of those jobs finished without me touching the keyboard, how long each one took to set up, and where the free plan stopped me.

The two tools come at the same problem from opposite directions. Bardeen is a workflow builder: you describe the job, it builds a playbook, and it runs the steps for you. Harpa AI is a copilot that lives on the page: you talk to it, it reads the page you are on, and it does the thing right there in front of you. One is a machine you program; the other is a colleague you ask.

## The Two Contenders

**Bardeen** is the older and more established of the two, with about 200,000 users and a 4.4-star rating across roughly 451 ratings on the Chrome Web Store. You describe a task in plain language — "take the leads from this LinkedIn search and add them to this Google Sheet" — and Bardeen builds a playbook from pre-built actions, then runs it in your browser while you watch. It connects to the apps you already use, and its library of ready-made playbooks is the deepest in this category. The free plan gives you 100 credits a month, the Basic plan costs $10 a month, and the Premium plan costs $50 a month. One honest change to note: Bardeen has moved decisively toward sales prospecting in the last year. It still automates general browser work, but its newest features, its templates and its marketing are all aimed at sales teams finding and enriching leads.

**Get it:** [Bardeen — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/bardeen-ai-automate-brows/ihhkmalpkhkoedlmcnilbbhhbhnicjga)

**Harpa AI** is the newer challenger, with about 400,000 users and a 4.7-star rating across roughly 3,200 ratings — twice Bardeen's user base and a noticeably higher rating. It is a page-aware AI copilot: open any page, ask Harpa a question about it, and it answers using the page's actual content. Beyond chat, it runs commands on the page — summarise, extract, translate, rewrite — and it can monitor pages for changes and run automations on a schedule. The free tier is a demo in the honest sense: 10 AI messages a day and 100 command runs, enough to test the idea but not to run a workflow on. Paid plans start from roughly $12 to $18 a month on annual billing, priced in tokens and "Megatokens" in a system I found genuinely confusing — I had to read the pricing page twice and I still could not tell you exactly what a month of heavy use costs.

**Get it:** [Harpa AI — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/harpa-ai-web-automation-w/eanggfilgoajaocelnaflolkadkeghjp)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Bardeen's weakness is the drift. It started as a general browser automator and it is becoming a sales tool, and you feel it in the template library: the newest playbooks are almost all about prospecting, enrichment and outreach, while the general-purpose automations have not had the same attention. The credit system is also tight on the free plan — 100 credits a month sounds like a lot until you learn that a single playbook run can cost several credits, and a scraping job across a hundred rows can eat the month's allowance in an afternoon. And Bardeen's automations run in your browser, which means your browser has to be open, the tab has to be there, and a website redesign can break a playbook that worked yesterday.

Harpa's weakness is the pricing. The free tier is genuinely useful for testing, but the paid pricing in tokens and Megatokens is the most confusing I have seen in this whole series of comparisons. I could not work out, from the pricing page alone, what my three weeks of testing would have cost on a paid plan — and a price you cannot work out is a price you cannot budget for. Harpa is also a copilot first and an automator second: its page-aware chat is excellent, but its scheduled automations and multi-step workflows are thinner than Bardeen's playbooks, and setting up a complex job takes more fiddling than Bardeen's one-click templates.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The lead list.** I took a search-results page of fifty contacts and needed names, roles and companies in a spreadsheet. Bardeen won: its playbook scraped the page, cleaned the data and wrote it to Google Sheets in one run, and I watched it work through the rows. Harpa did the same job, but it took two attempts — the first run missed the company field on half the rows — and I had to guide it with a follow-up prompt. For a repeatable scraping job, Bardeen's structured playbook beat Harpa's conversation.

**The morning dashboard.** Every morning I open the same dashboard, copy the same three numbers, and paste them into a message for a client. Harpa won this one: I set a command that reads the page and writes the summary, and running it became one click on the page itself. Bardeen could do it too, but building the playbook took fifteen minutes of setup for a ninety-second job, and the playbook broke once when the dashboard changed a label.

**The page that changed.** I needed to know the moment a supplier changed a price on their site. Both tools monitor pages, and both caught the change — Harpa sent the alert slightly faster, and its alert included a short summary of what changed, while Bardeen's alert just told me something had changed and I had to open the page to see what. Small difference, real time saved.

**The form that never ends.** I had to fill the same web form forty times with different data from a spreadsheet. Bardeen won outright: its playbook looped through the rows and filled the form while I made coffee, finishing all forty in one run. Harpa can fill forms, but it is not built for loops, and doing forty by conversation would have taken longer than doing them by hand.

**The messy page, made sendable.** A client sent me a long, jargon-heavy page and asked what it meant for them. Harpa won: I asked it to summarise the page for a non-technical client, it did, and the summary was good enough to send with light editing. Bardeen has AI summarisation too, but it is not the point of the tool, and the summary it produced was thinner and needed more of my time.

## What to Watch Out For: Permissions and Privacy

A web automation extension is the most powerful thing you can install in a browser, and the most dangerous. To do its job it needs to read pages, click buttons, fill forms and copy data — which means it can see your emails, your passwords as you type them, your bank balance, and anything else on the page. Both tools here ask for broad permissions, and both deserve the same rule: never run an automation on a page with sensitive information unless you understand exactly what the tool will do with what it reads.

The second thing to watch is that automations break. Websites change their layout without telling you, a button moves, a label is renamed, and the playbook that worked on Monday fails silently on Tuesday — or worse, fills the wrong field. I check every automated run the first few times, and I still spot-check the important ones. An automation you never verify is a mistake you scheduled.

The third thing is the terms of service of the site you are automating. Scraping a public directory is one thing; automating actions on a site whose terms forbid it — LinkedIn is the famous example — can get your account restricted. Bardeen's sales-prospecting templates sit squarely in this grey area. Read the terms of the site you are automating, not just the terms of the tool.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If your automations mostly move data between web apps rather than clicking around in the browser, the tool you already know is probably the better one. The official **Zapier Chrome extension** lets you trigger Zaps from any page — push a lead, a link or a screenshot into a workflow without opening Zapier itself — and Zapier's catalogue of thousands of app connections is deeper than either tool here. It is a complement to Bardeen and Harpa, not a replacement: Zapier connects apps, these two drive the browser.

And if your work is mostly in Microsoft's world, the official **Power Automate browser extension** records browser flows and runs them on a schedule, free with Windows and Microsoft 365. It is less friendly than either tool here and far more powerful for repetitive desktop-plus-browser jobs, which is why IT departments like it and everyone else finds it intimidating.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Bardeen really free?

Bardeen's free plan gives you 100 credits a month. A single playbook run can cost several credits, and a scraping job across a hundred rows can use the month's allowance in one afternoon. For light, occasional use it is genuinely free; for daily automation you will be on the $10-a-month Basic plan quickly.

### What is the difference between Bardeen and Harpa AI?

Bardeen is a workflow builder: you describe a job, it builds a playbook, and it runs the steps for you — best for repeatable, multi-step jobs like scraping to a sheet. Harpa is a copilot on the page: you talk to it, it reads the page you are on, and it acts there — best for asking questions about a page and running quick commands.

### Why is Harpa's pricing confusing?

Harpa prices paid plans in tokens and Megatokens, and the pricing page does not make it obvious what a month of heavy use costs. Paid plans start from roughly $12 to $18 a month on annual billing, but I could not work out an exact monthly cost from the pricing page alone — test the free tier first and watch your usage.

### Can these tools automate LinkedIn?

Technically, yes — Bardeen even has templates for it. But LinkedIn's terms restrict automated access, and accounts have been restricted for it. If you automate on LinkedIn or any site with similar terms, you are accepting that risk, and you should read the site's terms first.

### Do automation extensions work when my browser is closed?

No. Both Bardeen and Harpa run inside your browser, so the browser has to be open and the tab has to be there. If you need automations that run while your computer is off, you need a server-side tool like Zapier, not a browser extension.

### Which automation extension is best for sales teams?

Bardeen, because that is where it is heading: its newest playbooks, templates and features are built around prospecting, enrichment and outreach, and its CRM connections are the deepest here. Harpa is the better choice if you want a general-purpose copilot rather than a sales machine.

## Verdict

Install **Bardeen** if you have a repeatable job you want done the same way every time. Scraping a list into a sheet, filling the same form forty times, moving data between apps — its playbooks are the deepest in this category, the pricing is clear, and the $10-a-month Basic plan is honest value. Just know it is becoming a sales tool, and its free 100 credits go fast.

Install **Harpa AI** if you want an AI that lives on the page and answers questions about what you are looking at. Four hundred thousand users and a 4.7-star rating are not an accident, and its page-aware chat is the best in this category. Test the free tier first, and do not pay until you understand the token pricing.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep Bardeen — because the job I automate most is the same job every time, and a playbook that runs it while I make coffee is worth more than a copilot I have to talk to.
