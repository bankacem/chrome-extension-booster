## I Spent Two Weeks Timing My Work With Four Trackers Running

I have a problem that will sound familiar to anyone who bills by the hour or works from home: I sit down to work, and three hours later I cannot tell you where the time went. Some of it was real work. Some of it was email that felt like work. Some of it was a tab I opened to check one thing and never closed. A time tracker is supposed to answer that question honestly, and the difference between a good one and a bad one is whether the answer is automatic or another chore you have to remember.

For two weeks I ran four of them through my actual working days: starting timers on client work, letting automatic tracking run in the background, checking idle detection when I walked away for coffee, exporting the reports at the end of the week, and watching what each one costs — because in 2026, three of these four changed their free plan or their price, and one of them disappeared from the Chrome Web Store entirely and came back as a different listing.

The four tools split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. Toggl Track, Clockify and Harvest are manual timers: you press start, you press stop, and the record is exactly what you said it was. RescueTime is automatic: it watches everything and categorises it for you, and you never press anything. Which camp you belong in decides most of this comparison before you read a single feature — and the honest answer is that freelancers who bill clients need the manual camp, while people who want to know where their day went need the automatic one.

## The Four Contenders

**Toggl Track** is the biggest name in this group, with 300,000 Chrome users and a 3.1-star rating across roughly 1,500 ratings, and it was updated on November 10, 2026 to version 4.11.26. It is the timer-button tool: a start/stop button embedded in more than 120 web tools — Trello, Asana, Todoist, Jira, Notion, Google Docs, GitHub — so you start timing without opening another tab. It has a Pomodoro timer with break reminders, idle detection, tracking reminders for when you forgot to start, and automatic start/stop when Chrome opens and closes. Projects, tags and billable rates live on every time entry, and the web app adds reports and profitability analysis. It is open source under the Apache 2.0 licence, and it takes no screenshots and records no keystrokes, which matters if you track time on a work machine. The free plan covers up to 3 users — it was 5 until 2026, when Toggl cut it — and paid plans start at Starter, $9 per user per month billed annually ($12 month-to-month), with Premium at $16 annually ($24 monthly). The weakness is the rating: the store shows 3.1 stars now, down from 4.35 in a February 2026 snapshot, and recent reviews complain most about mobile and desktop sync failures and reports that feel outdated. There is also no invoice generation, which is a real gap if you bill from your tracked time.

**Get it:** [Toggl Track — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/toggl-track-productivity-time-tracker/oejgccbfbmkkpaidnkphaiaecficdnfn)

**Clockify** is the free-tier pick, and the one with the worst recent reviews. It has 300,000 Chrome users and a 2.8-star rating across only 225 ratings, and it was last updated on October 27, 2026 to version 2.13.0. It is a one-click timer that drops a start button into more than 50 web apps — Trello, Jira, Gmail, Google Calendar, GitHub, Asana, Todoist — with a Pomodoro timer, idle detection, reminders, auto start/stop, a keyboard shortcut (Ctrl+Shift+U), a default project, a calendar view and a timesheet that syncs across web, desktop and mobile. It is open source, and the free plan covers up to 5 users — but that number changed in April 2026, when Clockify capped a free plan that used to be unlimited. Paid plans start at Basic, $3.99 per user per month billed annually, which is the cheapest paid tier in this whole comparison. The weakness is reliability: the most common complaint in recent reviews is that the extension stops loading, fails to connect, and needs repeated reinstalls, and the recent-review average on Chrome-Stats is 1.70 out of 5. It is also time-tracking only — time-off, invoicing and scheduling all need paid web plans — and the Pro plan adds activity monitoring with screenshots and GPS tracking, which is a different product from the extension and worth knowing about before you upgrade.

**Get it:** [Clockify — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/clockify-time-tracker/pmjeegjhjdlccodhacdgbgfagbpmccpe)

**RescueTime** is the automatic tracker, and the one with a story you need to hear before you install it. Its current Chrome listing has 1,000 users and a 4.3-star rating across only 3 ratings, and it was updated on October 12, 2026 to version 3.4.3. That is because the old extension was removed from the Chrome Web Store on August 27, 2026, and RescueTime relaunched it as a brand-new listing in the autumn — the 1,000 users and 3 ratings are a fresh start, not an established track record. What it does is genuinely different from the other three: it tracks everything automatically, with no start or stop button, categorises websites from Very Productive to Very Distracting, detects idle time, and builds your reports from what you actually did. Focus Sessions that block distracting sites are a paid feature, as are goals and alerts, and timesheets with billable rates need the Solo+ plan. The free Lite plan costs nothing but keeps only two weeks of report history and one goal, with no Focus Sessions, alerts, offline time or iOS app. Solo is $7 a month billed annually ($9 month-to-month) and Solo+ is $12 annually ($15 monthly). The weaknesses: the relaunch is unproven, and the old listing's reviews repeatedly reported incompatibility with newer Chrome versions, inaccurate tracking with gaps and misreported hours, and persistent login problems — whether the rewrite fixed any of that is unknown, because there are only 3 ratings so far. There is also no Pomodoro timer and no tracking reminders, and the desktop app is still required for the full product on Windows and macOS.

**Get it:** [RescueTime — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/rescuetime-for-chrome-and/ejbhmcfcmklnigimmkkcolgigcnehien)

**Harvest** is the invoicing pick, and the one that changed owners. It has 30,000 Chrome users and a 2.8-star rating across 161 ratings, and it was last updated on November 6, 2026 to version 4.1.40. It is a one-click timer in the Chrome toolbar — the icon turns orange while a timer is running — with timers embedded in Asana, Basecamp, Trello, ClickUp, GitHub, Jira, Notion and Monday, manual time entry, expense tracking, and the feature none of the others has: it turns tracked time into invoices and collects payment online through Stripe and PayPal. Reports cover project budgets, staff and profitability. The free plan is $0 forever but covers exactly 1 seat and 2 projects. Teams is $9 per seat per month billed annually ($11 month-to-month) and Enterprise is $14 annually ($17.50 monthly), and paid plans now warn that extra invoices, projects, clients and tasks may be billed according to use on top of the seat price — a 2026 change that followed Bending Spoons acquiring Harvest in July 2025. The weaknesses are integration-shaped: multiple reviews from May to August 2026 report the Basecamp integration broken, with Basecamp 5 not supported, and one reviewer on November 6, 2026 reported the Asana integration disappearing entirely. There is no pause button, you cannot edit notes from the extension itself, and there are no reminders, idle detection or Pomodoro timer. It is closed source.

**Get it:** [Harvest — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/harvest-time-tracker/fbpiglieekigmkeebmeohkelfpjjlaia)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Toggl Track falls short on reliability and on the free plan. The sync failures between mobile and desktop are the single most repeated complaint in 2026 reviews, and the reports — the thing you actually read at the end of the week — are described as weak and outdated. The free plan cut from 5 users to 3 in 2026 means a tiny team of four now pays, and profitability reports and timesheet approvals sit behind the Premium plan. If you bill from your time, the missing invoice generation is the gap you will feel first.

Clockify falls short on the extension itself. The timer and the web app are fine; the Chrome extension is the part people complain about, with loading failures and repeated reinstalls dominating recent reviews and a recent-review average of 1.70 out of 5. The free plan is still the most generous here at 5 users, but the April 2026 cap means it is no longer the unlimited free tier it was sold as for years. And the cheapest paid plan is cheap for a reason: time-off, invoicing and scheduling all cost extra.

RescueTime falls short on proof. Everything about the new listing is a promise rather than a record: 1,000 users, 3 ratings, one month old. The old extension's problems — unreliable tracking, login issues, Chrome incompatibility — may be fixed in the rewrite or may not be, and there is no evidence either way yet. The free Lite plan is also thin in a way that matters: two weeks of history is not enough to see a monthly pattern, and Focus Sessions, the feature that makes RescueTime more than a report, are paid.

Harvest falls short on integrations and on price clarity. A time tracker that loses its Asana and Basecamp integrations is a time tracker that stops being where your work happens, and both broke in 2026. The usage-based charges on top of the seat price make the real monthly cost harder to predict than any other tool here, and the free plan's 1 seat and 2 projects means it is a trial in everything but name. The missing pause button and the inability to edit notes from the extension are small things that annoy you daily.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The client invoice.** I tracked a week of work for one client and turned it into an invoice. Harvest did it end to end: timer in the toolbar, entries tagged to the project, invoice generated from the tracked hours, payment collected through Stripe. Toggl Track and Clockify both produced clean time reports, but neither generates an invoice, so the report had to go into a separate invoicing tool. RescueTime produced the most honest record of the week — it caught the half hour I spent in a tab I had forgotten about — but its report is about productivity, not billable hours, and turning it into an invoice would have meant re-entering everything. If you bill clients, Harvest is the only tool here that finishes the job.

**The forgotten timer.** I started a timer on a task, got pulled into a meeting, and came back two hours later. Toggl Track's idle detection caught it: it asked whether I had been away and offered to discard the idle time. Clockify did the same. Harvest has no idle detection and no pause button, so the timer just kept running and I had to edit the entry on the website afterwards — the extension itself cannot edit notes. RescueTime never had the problem, because there is no timer to forget: it recorded the meeting as what it was and moved on. This is the scenario that makes automatic tracking tempting, and the scenario that shows Harvest's missing features most clearly.

**The small team.** I set up a four-person team on each free plan to see what "free" actually means in 2026. Clockify covered all four — its free plan allows 5 users, capped in April 2026 from unlimited. Toggl Track did not: its free plan was cut from 5 users to 3 in 2026, so the fourth person meant paying Starter at $9 a month. Harvest's free plan covers 1 seat and 2 projects, so a team of four was never a possibility. RescueTime's Lite plan is free for one person with two weeks of history. The honest summary: Clockify is the only free plan in this group that still fits a small team, and even that changed this year.

**The distraction audit.** I wanted to know where my afternoons actually went, so I let RescueTime run for a week without touching it. The answer was uncomfortable and specific: 47 minutes a day on one news site, 20 minutes on a shopping tab I kept reopening, and a recurring 15-minute hole after lunch that was a messaging app. Toggl Track, Clockify and Harvest could not answer this question at all, because they only record what you tell them to record — and nobody starts a timer on their own distraction. RescueTime's Focus Sessions then blocked the news site during work hours, which is the feature that turns the audit into a fix. If your problem is knowing where the day went rather than billing it, RescueTime is the only tool in this group that does the job.

**The end-of-week report.** I exported each tool's weekly report and tried to answer one question: how many hours did I spend on each project, and was it profitable? Toggl Track's report was the clearest for project hours, but profitability analysis needs Premium. Clockify's report was solid and its PDF export worked on the free plan, though only for a one-month range. Harvest's report was the most useful for a freelancer, because it ties hours to project budgets and invoices in the same place. RescueTime's report was the most interesting and the least useful for billing: it told me I was 62% productive on Tuesday and which sites dragged the number down, which is fascinating and does not help you invoice anyone.

## What to Watch Out For: Vanishing Extensions, Shrinking Free Plans and New Owners

The first thing to watch is that extensions can vanish. RescueTime's old Chrome extension was removed from the Chrome Web Store on August 27, 2026, and the replacement listing is barely a month old, with 1,000 users and 3 ratings. Timely did something similar in June 2026: it discontinued its standalone Memory for Chrome extension entirely, and browser activity is now captured only by its Mac and Windows desktop apps. Any 2026 roundup still listing Timely's Memory for Chrome as a live extension is out of date, and anyone installing RescueTime in late 2026 should know they are an early adopter of a rewrite, not a user of an established tool.

The second thing is that free plans shrank in 2026, and the marketing did not always keep up. Clockify's free plan was unlimited users until April 2026, when it was capped at 5. Toggl Track's free plan was 5 users until 2026, when it was cut to 3 — and Toggl also raised its paid prices, with Starter at $9 and Premium at $16 per user per month on annual billing. If you read an older comparison that calls either of these "free forever, unlimited", that comparison is describing a product that no longer exists. Check the current pricing page before you commit a team to a free tier.

The third thing is ownership. Bending Spoons acquired Harvest in July 2025, and the 2026 price list reflects it: Teams from $9 per seat per month annually, Enterprise from $14, and paid plans now warn that extra invoices, projects, clients and tasks may be billed according to use on top of the seat price. Harvest is still a good invoicing tool, but its real monthly cost is now harder to predict than any other tool in this comparison, and the Basecamp and Asana integrations that justified the price both broke in 2026.

One more warning that applies to all four: a time tracker sees a lot. Clockify's store disclosure lists personally identifiable information, authentication information and website content, and its Pro plan adds activity monitoring with screenshots and GPS tracking — the extension itself does not take screenshots, but the platform can on paid plans, which is a distinction your team should understand before you upgrade. RescueTime records every app and website you visit and sends that activity to its servers to build your reports, and its privacy policy deletes free-account data older than 15 months on a rolling basis. Toggl Track is the cleanest here: no screenshots, no keystrokes, data not sold, SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 certified. Whatever you pick, install only the official extension from the Chrome Web Store and check the publisher — a time tracker with broad permissions is a bad thing to get wrong.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If the four above do not fit, the two alternatives I would look at first are TrackingTime and TimeCamp. TrackingTime puts timer buttons inside more than 60 web apps — Trello, Asana, Notion, Linear, Microsoft Planner — with automatic timesheets, calendar views, invoicing and attendance, and it is the calendar-first tracker in this group. It has 30,000 Chrome users and a 4.2-star rating, and it was updated on October 31, 2026. Its free plan exists, and paid plans start at Starter, $4.50 per user per month billed annually. Its weakness is friction: reviewers say it takes extra clicks for quick tracking, and some integrations do not support the workflows people expect. TimeCamp is the budget pick: a one-click timer in more than 70 web apps with keyword-based automatic tracking, Pomodoro, invoicing and profitability reports, and the only tool in this whole roundup whose free plan covers unlimited users. It has 6,000 Chrome users and a 3.7-star rating, and it was updated on October 8, 2026. Paid plans start at Starter, $3.99 per user per month annually, and its Chrome plugin is open source. Its weakness is that the extension itself is basic — most of the power lives in the web and desktop apps — and its optional employee-surveillance features, including screenshots on the Ultimate plan, can feel intrusive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which time tracker extension is best for freelancers?

Harvest, if you invoice from your hours — it is the only one here that turns tracked time into an invoice and collects payment. Toggl Track is the better pure timer, with more integrations and a cleaner privacy record, but you will need a separate invoicing tool. If you only need to know where the day went, RescueTime answers a question the others cannot ask.

### Is Toggl Track really free?

For up to 3 users, yes — but that number was 5 until 2026, when Toggl cut it. A four-person team pays Starter at $9 per user per month on annual billing. Profitability reports and timesheet approvals need Premium at $16. Check the current pricing page before you commit a team.

### Why does Clockify have such a low rating?

The 2.8-star store rating, with a recent-review average of 1.70, reflects the Chrome extension specifically: reviewers report it stops loading, fails to connect, and needs repeated reinstalls. The web app and the product as a whole rate much higher — the 4.8 stars on Clockify's own site is Capterra for the whole product, not the extension. If you mostly use the web app, the extension's rating matters less.

### What happened to RescueTime's Chrome extension?

The old listing was removed from the Chrome Web Store on August 27, 2026. RescueTime relaunched it as a new listing in October 2026 (version 3.4.3), which is why it shows only 1,000 users and 3 ratings. Do not use the old listing links — they lead to a dead page — and treat the new listing as an early-stage rewrite with no meaningful review history yet.

### Does any of these track time automatically?

Only RescueTime. Toggl Track, Clockify and Harvest are manual timers: you press start and stop. RescueTime watches everything and categorises it, which is why it can tell you where your afternoon went — and also why it sees more of your activity than a manual timer ever will. If automatic tracking feels invasive, pick a manual timer and accept that you will sometimes forget to start it.

### Do time tracker extensions spy on you?

It depends on the tool and the plan. Toggl Track takes no screenshots and records no keystrokes. Clockify's extension does not take screenshots, but its Pro plan adds activity monitoring with screenshots and GPS tracking. RescueTime records every app and website you visit by design — that is the product — and deletes free-account data older than 15 months. Read the store's privacy disclosure before installing any of them, and never install a time tracker from anywhere but the official Chrome Web Store listing.

## Verdict

Install **Toggl Track** if you want the best manual timer: the most integrations, the cleanest privacy record, Pomodoro and idle detection, and an open-source extension. Accept the 3-user free limit and the sync complaints, and accept that you will invoice somewhere else.

Install **Clockify** if you want the most generous free plan and the cheapest paid tier: 5 users free, Basic at $3.99 a month, and an open-source extension. Accept that the extension itself is the weakest part of the product, and keep the web app open as a backup.

Install **RescueTime** if your problem is knowing where the day went rather than billing it: automatic tracking, productivity scores and Focus Sessions do a job nothing else here does. Accept that you are installing a one-month-old relaunch with 1,000 users and 3 ratings, and that the free plan keeps only two weeks of history.

Install **Harvest** if you bill clients by the hour and want the invoice to come from the same tool as the timer: Stripe and PayPal collection, expenses and project budgets in one place. Accept the broken Basecamp and Asana integrations, the usage-based charges on paid plans, and a free plan that covers 1 seat and 2 projects.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep Toggl Track — the timer I actually remembered to press, in the tools I already use, with a privacy record I could defend to a client. But if I billed by the hour, I would keep Harvest next to it, because the invoice is the part of the job that pays.
