## I Spent Three Weeks Living Inside Four Calendar Extensions

I missed a meeting on a Tuesday, and that is how this test started. Not a big meeting. A thirty-minute call with a client who had already rescheduled once, and I was eight minutes late because my calendar tab was buried under fourteen other tabs and the browser notification never fired. I apologised, we moved on, and then I did what I always do when a small failure annoys me enough: I turned it into a three-week experiment.

For three weeks I ran four calendar extensions side by side in Chrome, each one as my daily driver for several days, on the same Google Calendar and the same messy real schedule: client calls, recurring standups, a dentist appointment I kept moving, and a personal calendar full of school runs and gym sessions. I wanted to answer a simple question: which of these tools actually stops you missing meetings, and which ones just put a prettier button on the same problem?

One thing I learned before installing anything: Google has never published an official Calendar extension for Chrome. Every listing with "Google Calendar" in the name is made by a third party, including the ones that look official — and every tool here asks for access to your calendar.

The four tools I tested cover four different jobs. Checker Plus and Button are toolbar popups that show your schedule without opening a tab. Calendly does not show your calendar at all — it lets other people book time on it. And Reclaim AI rearranges your calendar for you, defending focus time and slotting tasks into the gaps.

## The Four Contenders

**Checker Plus for Google Calendar** is the veteran of this category, and it shows in the numbers: about 300,000 users and a 4.4-star rating on the Chrome Web Store across roughly 2,100 ratings. Click the toolbar icon and you get your full agenda in a popup, with the ability to create events, RSVP to invitations and snooze reminders without ever opening Google Calendar itself. Its signature feature is notifications: persistent desktop alerts, and even voice reminders that read out your next meeting. The core is free, funded by voluntary contributions, and about 95% of the features cost nothing; a one-time contribution of any amount — usually from about $15 — unlocks the rest.

**Get it:** [Checker Plus for Google Calendar — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/checker-plus-for-google-c/hkhggnncdpfibdhinjiegagmopldibha)

**Button for Google Calendar** is the minimalist of the four, and the lightest by a wide margin at about 300KB. Around 100,000 users run it, with a 4.1-star rating from roughly 200 ratings. It does one thing: it puts your next events in a small popup and lets you join the meeting in one click, whether it is Zoom, Google Meet or Microsoft Teams. You click, you see what is next, you join. The trade-off for being free is advertising — the extension shows ads inside the popup, which is how the developer pays for it. If you want the fastest possible answer to "what is my next meeting and how do I join it," this is the tool that answers it in the fewest clicks.

**Get it:** [Button for Google Calendar — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/button-for-google-calenda/lfjnmopldodmmdhddmeacgjnjeakjpki)

**Calendly** is the only tool here that is not really about viewing your calendar. It is about letting other people put things on it without the back-and-forth email chain. The Chrome extension has about 700,000 users and a 3.8-star rating, and it drops scheduling links and time-slot suggestions directly into Gmail and LinkedIn. The free plan gives you one event type with unlimited meetings, which is enough for a freelancer or anyone who just needs a booking page. Paid plans start at about $10 a user a month and add more event types, group scheduling and CRM integrations. The extension itself is useless without a Calendly account, and that is the point: it is a remote control for a scheduling service, not a calendar viewer.

**Get it:** [Calendly — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/calendly-browser-extensio/cbhilkcodigmigfbnphipnnmamjfkipp)

**Reclaim AI** is the smartest tool here and the hardest to explain in one sentence. It watches your Google Calendar and automatically schedules the work you keep meaning to do: exercise, deep work, lunch, and one-on-ones with your team. When a meeting lands on top of a focus block, Reclaim moves the block instead of deleting it. It has about 600,000 users, a 4.7-star rating on G2 across roughly 2,600 reviews, and it has been owned by Dropbox since August 2024. The free Lite plan covers one calendar sync, three habits and a one-week scheduling range, with no time limit and no card required. Paid plans start at about $10 a seat a month on annual billing and unlock unlimited habits and an eight-week range. You sign up on the web and add the extension from there.

**Get it:** [Reclaim AI](https://reclaim.ai/)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Checker Plus's weakness is density. It has more settings than any calendar tool I have used, and the popup packs in so much — agenda, notifications, snoozing, voice reminders, quick-add — that a new user can spend twenty minutes configuring it before it does anything useful. On a small laptop screen the popup feels crowded, and a few of the best features, like voice notifications, sit behind the contribution wall.

Button for Google Calendar's weakness is the ads. A calendar popup is the last place you want advertising, and the extension shows it. Worse, the tool is deliberately thin: it cannot create events, cannot snooze a reminder, and cannot show you anything beyond the next few events. The moment your question is anything more than "what is next," you are opening Google Calendar anyway, which is the tab this extension was supposed to save you from opening.

Calendly's weakness is permissions and price creep. The extension asks for access to every website you visit, because it needs to inject scheduling buttons into Gmail, LinkedIn and Google Calendar. That is a broad permission for a calendar tool, and it is the most common complaint in its reviews, alongside reports of calendar sync hiccups. The free plan is also genuinely limited: one event type means one kind of meeting, so the moment you need a 15-minute intro call and a 60-minute consultation, you are paying.

Reclaim AI's weakness is trust and control. It needs deep access to your calendar to do its job, and it moves your events around on its own, which feels wrong the first time it happens. If you are the kind of person who wants every block exactly where you put it, Reclaim will fight you. The free plan is also tight: three habits and a one-week scheduling range is enough to test the idea, but not enough to run a real routine on.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The back-to-back morning.** I had four meetings stacked from 09:00 to 12:30 with ten-minute gaps, and I ran the morning with each tool in turn across four weeks. Checker Plus won outright: the desktop notification fired five minutes before each call, the voice reminder read the meeting name aloud, and the popup let me join from the notification itself. Button for Google Calendar was nearly as good at joining — one click, straight into the call — but it never warned me, so I had to remember to look.

**The "when are you free?" email.** A client asked for a call next week, and I answered from Gmail. With Calendly, I clicked the extension in the compose window, picked three times, and the email carried live booking links — the client booked within the hour, no reply needed. With the other three tools, I had to open my calendar, check the week, and type the times out by hand. This is Calendly's home turf, and it is not close: if you answer availability questions more than twice a week, the extension pays for itself in an afternoon. For more on handling those emails, my guide to the [best Chrome extensions for meeting notes](/blog/best-chrome-extensions-for-meeting-notes) covers what to do once the call is actually booked.

**The disappearing gym session.** I set a habit in Reclaim AI: gym, three times a week, 45 minutes, before 10:00. In week one, two of the three sessions were eaten by meetings that landed on top of them, and Reclaim quietly moved them to the next free morning instead of letting them vanish. By week three, it had found a pattern — Tuesday and Thursday mornings were reliably free — and my gym blocks stopped moving. If your calendar is a list of intentions that meetings keep destroying, this is the scenario that sells Reclaim.

**The flight across time zones.** I flew from London to New York mid-test, and my calendar turned into a mess of times that looked right and were wrong. None of these four tools fixes time zones on its own, but Checker Plus's popup showed the next event with a countdown in local time, which made the damage visible fastest. Button showed the same event with no countdown, so I had to do the arithmetic myself.

**The shared family calendar.** I run a family calendar with my partner for school runs, appointments and the weekly shop, and I tested which tool made that least painful. The honest answer is none of them: all four are built around one person's work calendar, and none of them has a good story for a shared household schedule. Checker Plus handled it best, because its popup shows multiple calendars at a glance and lets me add an event to the family calendar without opening the full page. If your calendar is half work and half family, pick the viewer, not the scheduler.

## What to Watch Out For: Permissions and Privacy

Every calendar extension is a privacy decision, because a calendar is a diary. It knows who you meet, when, where, and how often. Before you install any of these, read what it asks for.

Checker Plus connects through Google's official Calendar API using OAuth, which means it never sees your Google password, and the developer states that calendar data is not stored on outside servers. That is the best architecture in this group. Button for Google Calendar also uses OAuth, but it is ad-supported, and an ad-supported extension has a business reason to know more about you than a donation-funded one does. Read its privacy disclosure before you install it, and decide whether a free popup is worth the ads.

Calendly's permission is the widest here: it asks for access to every website you visit, so it can inject scheduling buttons into Gmail, LinkedIn and Google Calendar. The company says it does not move your browsing data elsewhere, and its extension has a good reputation, but the permission itself is real.

Reclaim AI is SOC 2 Type II certified and connects through OAuth, and it only accesses the calendar metadata it needs to manage your availability. The trade is different from the others: you are not just letting a tool read your calendar, you are letting it change your calendar. That is a bigger grant of trust, and you should only give it to a tool whose company you expect to still exist next year. Reclaim being owned by Dropbox helps here. The cautionary tale is Clockwise, the AI calendar optimiser acquired by Salesforce and shut down entirely on March 27, 2026, deleting all user data.

One rule I follow with all four: never put anything in a calendar you would not want read aloud in a meeting.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

The four tools above are not the only options, and two others came up constantly in my research. [Motion](https://www.usemotion.com/) is Reclaim's closest rival: an AI scheduler that plans your day around tasks, deadlines and meetings, and automatically rebuilds the plan when things slip. It is more expensive — paid plans start at about $19 a user a month, with only a short trial instead of a permanent free tier — and it is built for people whose day is task-driven rather than meeting-driven.

GCalPlus is the quiet free option in this category. Made by GCalTools, it adds power-user features directly inside Google Calendar itself: custom colours, event insights, bulk editing and a cleaner interface, without a popup or a separate app. It offers no notifications, scheduling links or auto-planning, but if your complaint is that Google Calendar's interface is hard to read, GCalPlus fixes the interface itself. It is the only alternative here that makes the calendar better rather than building around it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there an official Google Calendar extension for Chrome?

No. Google has never published an official Calendar extension for Chrome, and it does not maintain or endorse any listing with "Google Calendar" in the name. Checker Plus and Button both connect through Google's official Calendar API using OAuth, but neither is made by Google.

### Which calendar extension is best for not missing meetings?

Checker Plus for Google Calendar, by a clear margin. It is the only tool in this comparison with persistent desktop notifications, and its voice reminders read out your next meeting before it starts. Button will get you into the meeting in one click, but it will not warn you it is coming.

### Is Checker Plus for Google Calendar free?

Yes, for about 95% of its features. The core — agenda popup, notifications, event creation, snoozing — costs nothing, and the developer funds it through voluntary contributions. A one-time contribution, usually from about $15, unlocks the rest, including voice notifications. There is no monthly subscription.

### Can I use Calendly for free?

Yes. Calendly's free plan gives you one event type with unlimited meetings, which is enough if you only offer one kind of booking — a 30-minute intro call, for example. A second event type, group scheduling or CRM integrations need a paid plan, from about $10 a user a month.

### Does Reclaim AI work with Outlook?

Yes, but with a caveat. Reclaim added Outlook and Microsoft 365 support, but it is newer and less mature than the Google Calendar integration, which is where the product started. If your work calendar lives in Outlook, test the free Lite plan first before you pay.

### What happened to Clockwise?

Clockwise, the AI calendar optimiser, was acquired by Salesforce and shut down completely on March 27, 2026. The product stopped working, new sign-ups closed, and user data was deleted. It is the cautionary tale of this category: pick a tool whose company you expect to still exist next year. Many older roundups still list Clockwise — ignore them.

## Verdict

Install **Checker Plus for Google Calendar** if you want the best all-round calendar extension. It is the only tool here that actively stops you missing meetings, it creates events and snoozes reminders from the popup, and it is effectively free.

Install **Button for Google Calendar** if you want the lightest possible tool and your only real question is "what is next and how do I join it." One click, straight into the call, at about 300KB.

Choose **Calendly** if other people book meetings with you. Scheduling links inside Gmail, a free plan that covers one event type, and paid plans from about $10 a user a month. It does nothing for your own schedule, but for anyone who answers "when are you free?" more than twice a week, it is the fastest payback in this article.

And choose **Reclaim AI** if your problem is not seeing your calendar but protecting it. Automatic habit scheduling, focus-time defence, and a tool that moves your gym session instead of letting a meeting eat it. The free Lite plan is enough to test the idea, and at about $10 a seat a month it is the cheapest AI scheduler still standing after Clockwise disappeared.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep Checker Plus — the tool that would have saved me from the missed meeting that started this test.
