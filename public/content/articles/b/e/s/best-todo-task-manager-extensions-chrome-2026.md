## I Spent Three Weeks Living Inside Four Task Lists

I have 47 open tasks right now. I counted them, because counting them was the first thing that made me feel slightly ill about my own organisation. Of those 47, eleven are overdue, six have no due date at all, and three are for projects I finished last month and forgot to close. That is the real state of most people's task management, mine included, and it is the reason I stopped trusting my memory and my notebook and started testing dedicated to-do extensions properly.

For three weeks I ran four of them through my actual working life: capturing tasks from web pages without leaving the tab, setting reminders for deadlines, sharing a grocery list with my partner, planning a week of work, and switching between my laptop and my phone to see which list actually follows me around. I also watched what each one costs now, because 2026 has been a year of price rises in this category, and the prices quoted in last year's roundups are wrong.

The four tools split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. Todoist, TickTick and Any.do are real task managers with projects, priorities, reminders and collaboration. Google Keep is a notes app with checklists that a lot of people use as a to-do list — and the difference shows up the first time you need a recurring task or a deadline. Which camp you belong in decides most of this comparison before you read a single feature.

## The Four Contenders

**Todoist** is the most popular task manager in this group, and it is not close. The Chrome extension has about 600,000 users and a 4.66-star rating across roughly 51,000 ratings, and it was updated on October 30, 2026. It is the tool I have used the longest, and the reason is the capture speed: highlight any text on a page, right-click, add to Todoist, and the page title and link are attached automatically. Natural-language entry works as advertised — type "call the bank tomorrow at 10am #errands" and it parses the date, the time and the project without you touching a picker. There are boards, filters, labels, priorities, subtasks and a calendar view, plus integrations with more than 60 apps including Google Calendar and Dropbox. The free Beginner plan gives you 5 personal projects, smart Quick Add, task reminders and flexible list and board views. Pro now costs $5 a month on annual billing ($60 a year) or $7 a month billed monthly, and Business costs $8 per user a month on annual billing ($96 a year) or $10 monthly — the current figures are on [Todoist's pricing page](https://todoist.com/pricing/). Note the rise: Pro used to be $4 a month, and the $5 price is new in 2026. Pro unlocks 300 personal projects, reminders, calendar layout, durations and AI features. It runs everywhere: web, Windows, macOS, iOS, Android and every major browser.

**Get it:** [Todoist — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/todoist-for-chrome-planne/jldhpllghnbhlbpcmnajkpdmadaolakh)

**TickTick** is the all-in-one, and the only tool here that bundles a to-do list, a calendar, a Pomodoro timer and habit tracking into a single app. The Chrome extension has about 100,000 users and a rating around 4.2 stars across roughly 700 ratings, and it was last updated on May 26, 2026. The app itself is the star: it has been recommended by The Verge, MakeUseOf and Wirecutter, and its iOS app averages 4.86 stars across more than 46,000 ratings. The free tier covers tasks, lists, reminders and basic calendar views. Premium costs $49.99 a year as of October 30, 2026 — up from $35.99, a rise of nearly 40% that older reviews do not mention — and it unlocks the full calendar, custom filters, habit tracking, the Eisenhower Matrix and the Pomodoro timer. The extension itself is a companion to the app: it lets you add tasks from any page and see your list in a popup, but the heavy lifting happens in the desktop and mobile apps. The weaknesses are practical: you must create an account before you can do anything, several reviewers report the extension failing to log in or losing its connection, and recent app reviews complain about lag — a 500 to 1,000 millisecond delay between tapping a task complete and the app reacting, which is the kind of thing that makes you tap twice and un-complete it.

**Get it:** [TickTick — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/ticktick-todo-task-list/diankknpkndanachmlckaikddgcehkod)

**Any.do** is the prettiest list here, and the one whose recent reviews worried me most. The Chrome extension has about 100,000 users and a 4.03-star rating across roughly 2,930 ratings, but the average of the last 100 reviews is only 3.38 stars — the recent mood has slipped. It puts your to-do list, calendar and a daily planner in one place, syncs across phone, desktop and web, and has WhatsApp reminders, which no other tool here offers. The free Personal plan covers tasks, lists, reminders, the calendar and the daily planner with sync across devices. Premium costs $4.99 a month on annual billing ($59.88 a year) or $7.99 billed monthly, and it unlocks recurring tasks, WhatsApp reminders, AI features, colour tags and location reminders; Teams costs the same per member, and Family is $8.33 a month for four members — the current tiers are on [Any.do's pricing page](https://www.any.do/pricing). The weaknesses: the extension takes over your new tab page and gives you no option to turn that off, which is the single most common complaint; recurring tasks are paywalled, which is a basic feature everywhere else; video ads arrived alongside the monetisation push; and users report task order appearing differently across devices and login failures in Brave.

**Get it:** [Any.do — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/any-do/kdadialhpiikehpdeejjeiikopddkjem)

**Google Keep** is the odd one out: not a task manager at all, but a notes app that 8 million people have installed as a Chrome extension, and the one with the worst rating in this group by a wide margin. The extension has about 8,000,000 users and a 2.5-star rating across roughly 8,000 ratings, and it is completely free with a Google account. It does exactly one thing: save a page, a selection or an image into a note, in a single click, with the source link attached. Notes can have checklists, reminders, labels and colours, and they sync to the Keep app on Android and iOS — the Android app has more than a billion downloads. But it is not a task manager: there are no projects, no priorities, no recurring tasks, no collaboration beyond sharing a note, and no way to organise notes from inside the extension itself. The 2.5-star rating is the visible scar of that mismatch: people install it expecting a to-do list and get a clipper. It is still the fastest capture tool here, and if your lists are short and simple, it may be all you need — but the moment you need a deadline that repeats every Monday, you have outgrown it.

**Get it:** [Google Keep — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/google-keep-chrome-extension/lpcaedmchfhocbbapmcbpinfpgnhloca)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Todoist's weakness is the price rise and the popup. Pro went from $4 to $5 a month on annual billing in 2026, which is a 25% rise for the same features, and the free plan is capped at 5 personal projects, which is tight for anyone with work and home and a side project. The extension popup is also the slowest of the four to load in my testing — the most common complaint in recent reviews is speed and memory — and sync can lag, so a task added on the phone sometimes takes a minute to appear in the extension.

TickTick's weakness is that the extension is the weakest part of the product. The app is excellent and the extension is a thin companion: it adds tasks and shows your list, but the calendar, the Pomodoro timer and the habits live in the app, not in the browser. The 40% Premium price rise to $49.99 a year is the second problem — it happened in October 2026 and most reviews still quote $35.99. And the sign-in requirement means you cannot even try it without an account, which is friction the others do not have.

Any.do's weakness is the new-tab takeover and the paywalling of basics. Taking over the new tab without an off switch is the kind of decision that makes people uninstall a tool they otherwise like, and it is the top complaint in the reviews. Recurring tasks behind a paywall is the other one: a task that repeats every week is not a premium feature, it is the definition of a to-do list. The recent-review average of 3.38 stars against a 4.03 lifetime average tells the story — long-time users are unhappy with the direction.

Google Keep's weakness is that it is not a task manager. No recurring tasks, no priorities, no projects, no due-date views, and the extension cannot even edit a note properly — notes created in the extension have not saved automatically since December 2022, and you have to open keep.google.com to work on them. The 2.5-star rating is honest. It is a clipper with checklists, and judging it as a task manager is unfair — but that is how most people use it, and that is why they are disappointed.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The 47-task import.** I moved my existing lists into all four tools. Todoist handled it best: natural-language entry let me type tasks in plain English and it parsed the dates, and the board view made the mess visible. TickTick imported cleanly and the calendar view immediately showed me which days were overloaded. Any.do imported fine but the recurring tasks I rely on — the weekly report, the monthly bills — were paywalled, so the import was the moment I had to decide whether to pay. Google Keep could not import a list at all; I would have had to retype everything as checklist notes. The lesson is practical: if you are migrating from another list, start with Todoist or TickTick, because the other two make you pay or retype before you have even finished moving in.

**The deadline with a reminder.** I set a task due tomorrow at 10am with a reminder. All four reminded me, but differently. Todoist's reminder arrived on time on both laptop and phone. TickTick's reminder was the most configurable — I could set it for 30 minutes before, at the time, and again at the deadline. Any.do's reminder worked, but only the basic kind; the location reminder I wanted was Premium. Google Keep's reminder worked for a single note, but there is no way to set a reminder that repeats. If reminders are the feature you live by, TickTick's are the most flexible and Todoist's are the most reliable.

**The shared grocery list.** I shared a household list with my partner. Todoist's sharing was the smoothest: I added her to the project, she got her own account, and tasks we each completed synced instantly. TickTick shared well too, with comments on tasks. Any.do's sharing needed Premium for the collaboration features I wanted. Google Keep's sharing worked — you share a note — but there is no way to assign a task to a person, so we ended up writing names in the task text. For shared lists, Todoist won.

**The weekly planning session.** Every Sunday I plan the week: what is due, what is overdue, what can wait. TickTick was the best here, because the calendar view shows tasks and events together, and I could drag a task to a different day. Todoist's calendar view is a Pro feature, so on the free plan I was looking at a list, not a week. Any.do's daily planner was genuinely nice — it walks you through tomorrow's tasks one by one — but it is a planner, not a calendar. Google Keep has no planning view at all. If you plan your week visually, TickTick's calendar is the reason to choose it.

**The capture from a web page.** I found an article I wanted to read later and a product I wanted to buy, and captured both without leaving the tab. Todoist was fastest: right-click, add, done, with the link attached. TickTick's capture worked but opened a larger dialog. Any.do captured fine. Google Keep was the fastest of all for saving the page itself — one click, no dialog — but it saved a note, not a task, so the article sat in my notes with no due date and no reminder, and I forgot about it for a week. Capture speed is not the same as task management, and this scenario proved it.

## What to Watch Out For: Permissions, Price Rises and the Microsoft Gap

A to-do extension asks for broad permissions, and you should know why. Todoist, TickTick and Any.do all request access to read the pages you visit, because that is how capture works — the extension has to see the page to save it. That is normal. What is worth checking is the privacy policy: TickTick's extension reads a login cookie to know whether you are signed in, and Any.do's sync means your task list lives on its servers. Install only the official extension from the Chrome Web Store, published by the company itself.

The second thing to watch is the price you see quoted in old reviews. Todoist Pro rose from $4 to $5 a month in 2026, and TickTick Premium rose from $35.99 to $49.99 a year in October 2026 — a 40% jump that most reviews still do not mention. Check the vendor's own pricing page before you pay; I verified all four the week I wrote this.

The third thing is the Microsoft gap. There is no official Microsoft To Do extension for Chrome. The listings that exist — MS-ToDo, MS-ToDo-online, Add to Microsoft To Do — are all third-party, made by independent developers, and none of them is Microsoft. If you live in Microsoft To Do, do not install one of these expecting official support; use the web app, or switch to a tool with a real extension. It is the most surprising hole in this category, and it is worth knowing before you go looking.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If the four above do not fit, the two alternatives I would look at first are Notion Web Clipper and ClickUp. Notion Web Clipper has more than a million Chrome users and turns any page into a Notion page, which makes it the best capture tool if your task system lives in Notion — but Notion is a workspace, not a to-do list, and it is overkill if you just want to remember to buy milk. ClickUp is a full project-management platform with a Chrome extension that adds tasks from any page, and its free tier is generous, but it is built for teams and its interface is heavier than any tool here. Both are worth a look if your needs are bigger than a personal list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which to-do extension is actually free?

Google Keep is fully free, but it is a notes app, not a task manager. Among real task managers, TickTick's free tier is the most generous — tasks, lists, reminders and a basic calendar. Todoist's free Beginner plan gives you 5 projects, and Any.do's free Personal plan covers tasks, lists and the calendar, but recurring tasks are paid.

### Is Todoist worth $5 a month?

If you live in your task list, yes. The natural-language entry, the capture speed and the 60+ integrations are the best in this group, and $60 a year is fair for a tool you open twenty times a day. If your lists are simple, the free plan's 5 projects may be enough, and TickTick's free tier covers more.

### Why did TickTick's price go up so much?

Premium went from $35.99 to $49.99 a year in October 2026, a rise of nearly 40%. The vendor has not made a fuss about it, and most reviews still quote the old price. At $49.99 it is still cheaper than Todoist Pro at $60 a year, and it includes the calendar, Pomodoro and habits that Todoist charges extra for or does not have.

### Can I use Google Keep as a to-do list?

You can, and 8 million people have installed the extension, but it is a poor task manager: no recurring tasks, no priorities, no projects, and the extension cannot edit notes properly. It is excellent for capturing pages and quick checklists. If your lists have deadlines, use a real task manager.

### Is there an official Microsoft To Do extension for Chrome?

No. There is no official Microsoft To Do extension. The listings called MS-ToDo and Add to Microsoft To Do are third-party tools by independent developers. They work, but they are not Microsoft, and they are not supported by Microsoft.

### Do these extensions work on my phone too?

Todoist, TickTick and Any.do all have iOS and Android apps that sync with the extension. Google Keep syncs to its own mobile apps. The extension itself is Chrome-only, so your phone experience is the app, not the extension — test the app before you commit, because that is where you will check your list most often.

## Verdict

Install **Todoist** if you want the best task manager and you are willing to pay for it. It had the fastest capture in my testing, the best rating in this group, and natural-language entry that actually saves time. The $5-a-month Pro price stings after the rise from $4, but for the tool that runs your day, it is the one I would pay for.

Install **TickTick** if you want everything in one place: tasks, calendar, Pomodoro and habits, with the best weekly-planning view I tested. Accept that the extension is a companion to the app, and that Premium is now $49.99 a year.

Install **Any.do** if you want the prettiest planner and you use WhatsApp reminders. Skip it if the new-tab takeover bothers you — there is no off switch — and do not pay for Premium just for recurring tasks.

Install **Google Keep** if you want a free clipper for saving pages into notes. Do not install it as a task manager: it has no recurring tasks, no priorities and no projects, and its 2.5-star rating is the honest verdict on that mismatch.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep Todoist — the tool whose capture I stopped thinking about by the second week, and the one that held my 47 tasks without making me feel worse about them.
