## I Spent Three Weeks Double-Clicking Every Word I Did Not Know

I read the way I always read: research papers, long articles, documentation, the occasional novel in a language I am still learning. And for three weeks, every time I hit a word I did not know, I double-clicked it and watched what happened. Four extensions, the same words, the same pages. The differences turned out to be bigger than I expected, because a dictionary extension is not one product — it is four different answers to the question of what a definition is for.

One of them is made by Google and has not been updated for the new extension standard, and its users are openly worried it will disappear. One of them is a clone of it that recently started failing for everyone. One of them is the oldest name in dictionaries. And one of them defines words that no other dictionary will touch.

I tested Google Dictionary, Dictionary Anywhere, Merriam-Webster and Urban Dictionary on the same reading: academic papers, news articles, technical documentation, fiction, and the comments section of the internet, which is where definitions get hardest. I timed how fast each one answered, checked whether the definition matched the meaning in the sentence, and noted where each one broke.

## The Four Contenders

**Google Dictionary** is the default dictionary extension, and the most used. It has around 3 to 4 million users and holds a 3.8-star rating on the Chrome Web Store across more than 13,700 ratings. Double-click any word and a small bubble shows its definition, with pronunciation and, for foreign words, a translation. It supports more than 10 languages, including Spanish, French, Italian, German, Russian, Hindi, Japanese, Arabic and Chinese, and it keeps an optional history of the words you looked up — stored locally in your browser, not uploaded anywhere. It is free, it is made by Google, and it is the extension this whole category is measured against. It is also the one in trouble: it has not been updated to Chrome's Manifest V3 standard, and its users are publicly pleading with Google to maintain it before Chrome removes it.

**Get it:** [Google Dictionary — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/google-dictionary-by-googl/mgijmajocgfcbeboacabfgobmjgjcoja)

**Dictionary Anywhere** is the independent alternative that works the same way: double-click a word, get a definition in a bubble, without leaving the page. It was built to do what Google Dictionary does, for people who wanted the same experience without Google, and it became popular on Firefox as well as Chrome, where users praised it as the closest thing to Google's extension. The problem is recent and serious: users report that it has started returning "Sorry. No Definition Found" for every word they try — even ordinary words like "following," "internet" and "law" — and the extension appears to have stopped working for many of them. A tool that cannot define "law" is not a dictionary.

**Get it:** [Dictionary Anywhere — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/dictionary-anywhere/fhbnjmdohdoejaogjflcljlnfgbjfpmm)

**Merriam-Webster** is the oldest name in this comparison, and the most traditional. Its extension gives you the full Merriam-Webster entry for any word you look up: definitions, examples, synonyms, and the usage notes that make it a reference work rather than a popup. It is free, and it is the only tool here whose definitions come from a dictionary with a two-hundred-year editorial history behind it. The trade-off is speed and design: it is slower to answer than Google Dictionary, and it feels like a reference book that happens to live in your browser, rather than a tool built for the browser.

**Get it:** [Merriam-Webster Dictionary — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/merriam-webster-dictionary/lacihfdemgdpngnffllnbgifajkffkba)

**Urban Dictionary** defines the words the other three will not touch. Slang, internet language, new coinages, the words people actually use in comments and messages and the words your teenager uses that you do not understand. It is free, and it is the only tool here that will tell you what a word means in the sentence you just read, rather than what it meant in a book a hundred years ago. The trade-off is obvious: the definitions are written by users, they are often crude, often funny, and sometimes wrong — and the extension is useless for academic or professional reading.

**Get it:** [Urban Dictionary — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/urban-dictionary/jcdggkefjbkbghlmggfcbjjbknldgkb)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Google Dictionary's weakness is its future. The extension has not been updated to Manifest V3, Chrome's new extension standard, and Chrome has been removing extensions that do not comply. Users have noticed: recent reviews are full of anxiety that the tool they use every day will simply disappear, and Google has said nothing public about updating it. It also does not work in Chrome's built-in PDF viewer, which is exactly where many people do their hardest reading, and the pronunciation button has stopped working for some users. A dictionary that cannot define words in PDFs and might be removed is a dictionary on borrowed time.

Dictionary Anywhere's weakness is that it appears to be broken. The reports are consistent across browsers: the extension returns "Sorry. No Definition Found" for ordinary words, and users who relied on it as their Google Dictionary replacement are leaving one-star reviews saying it does not work anymore. The French dictionary is also described as very incomplete, which makes it useless for French readers. I tested it myself and it failed on words the other three defined instantly. I cannot recommend a dictionary that cannot define the word "law."

Merriam-Webster's weakness is that it is slow and formal. A double-click opens a fuller entry than the other tools, which is wonderful when you want the complete picture and annoying when you want a one-line answer. It shows the general dictionary entry, not the meaning in your sentence, so for a word with many meanings you still have to choose the right one yourself. And it does not save words, does not track your history, and does not help you learn — it defines, and then it stops.

Urban Dictionary's weakness is that it is not a dictionary in the traditional sense. Its definitions are crowdsourced, unedited, and frequently vulgar, which means it is the wrong tool for a student, a professional, or anyone reading formal text. It is also the least reliable of the four for standard English: it will define slang brilliantly and define an ordinary word badly, or not at all. Use it for what it is for, and never for what it is not.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The research paper.** I read a 30-page academic paper full of terms I half-knew. Google Dictionary defined most of them in a second, in a bubble, without breaking my reading flow. Merriam-Webster gave fuller entries but took me out of the paper to read them. Dictionary Anywhere failed on the first three words I tried. For dense reading, speed and reliability beat completeness, and Google Dictionary won this round.

**The language learner.** I read a news article in Spanish, my second language, and double-clicked the words I did not know. Google Dictionary translated them and showed the definition in Spanish — the only tool here that does both. Merriam-Webster does not translate. Urban Dictionary does not know Spanish. Dictionary Anywhere was broken. For a learner, Google Dictionary is not just the best tool here. It is the only tool here.

**The writer checking nuance.** I was editing an article and needed to know the difference between two near-synonyms. Merriam-Webster gave me usage notes, examples and the distinction, and I rewrote the sentence correctly. Google Dictionary gave me a short definition that did not resolve the question. For writing, the fuller entry is the point, and the oldest dictionary in this comparison is still the best one for the job.

**The comment section.** I read a thread full of slang, abbreviations and words I had never seen. Urban Dictionary defined every one of them, with examples of how people actually use them, and I understood the thread for the first time. Google Dictionary defined none of them. Merriam-Webster defined none of them. The internet has its own language, and only one tool here speaks it.

**The student building vocabulary.** I tried to build a word list from a week of reading. Google Dictionary's local history gave me a list of every word I looked up, stored in my browser, which I could review at the end of the week. None of the other three saves words at all. For a learner who wants to remember what they looked up, that local history is the most useful feature in this entire comparison — and it is the reason I keep Google Dictionary installed despite the Manifest V3 risk.

## The Privacy Question Nobody Can Dodge

Dictionary extensions are among the least invasive tools in the browser, and that is worth saying clearly. Google Dictionary reads the text you select and looks up its meaning, and its word history is stored locally on your machine, not uploaded anywhere. An independent safety audit gave it a clean score and found nothing unexplained in its code, with the only external service being standard analytics. The other three are similarly light: they look up words, and that is all they do.

The privacy question here is not about the extensions. It is about what happens when the biggest one disappears. If Google removes Google Dictionary for failing to meet Manifest V3, its 3 million users will scatter to alternatives — and the alternatives include tools with worse privacy practices, tools that log your lookups on their servers, and tools that sell the data. The risk is not what Google Dictionary does with your data. The risk is what its replacement will do.

The practical advice is simple. Use Google Dictionary while it works, and keep a backup installed: Merriam-Webster for reference, and a learner-focused tool if you are studying a language. Do not install a dictionary extension that asks for permission to read all the pages you visit — a dictionary only needs the text you select. And if you are a learner, export your word history regularly, because it lives in your browser and disappears when the extension does.

## What the Old Guard Looks Like Now

The new generation of dictionary tools uses AI, and it is worth knowing about. [QuickDef](https://quickdef.app/blog/best-chrome-extensions-word-definitions) combines AI definitions with a traditional dictionary, works in PDFs, saves words with a free limit of 25 and unlimited saving on a premium plan from $2.50 a month, and is built for language learners — it is the tool Google Dictionary would be if Google were still updating it. [Readlang](https://readlang.com) pairs AI translation with definitions, saves the words you look up, and costs around $5 a month. Both are better learners' tools than anything in the main comparison, and both exist because Google stopped maintaining the tool everyone used.

The other old guard is the one this site covered today: [Power Thesaurus](/blog/underrated-chrome-extensions-2026), a thesaurus extension that finds the right synonym rather than the right definition. A dictionary tells you what a word means. A thesaurus tells you what word to use instead. I run both, and they answer different questions.

## Key Takeaways

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Google Dictionary going to be removed from Chrome?

It has not been removed, but it has not been updated to Manifest V3, Chrome's current extension standard, and Chrome has been removing non-compliant extensions. Google has not said publicly whether it will update the extension. Users are worried, and they have reason to be. Keep a backup installed.

### Why does Dictionary Anywhere say "No Definition Found"?

Users across browsers report that the extension has started returning that message for ordinary words, including "following," "internet" and "law." The cause is not public, and the extension appears to have stopped working for many people. Until it is fixed, use Google Dictionary or Merriam-Webster instead.

### Which dictionary extension is best for learning English?

Google Dictionary, for everyday reading: it defines fast, translates foreign words, and keeps a local history of everything you looked up. For serious study, add QuickDef or Readlang, which are built for learners and save words with AI definitions. Merriam-Webster is the best reference for nuance, but it does not track your learning.

### Does Google Dictionary work in PDFs?

No. Google Dictionary does not work in Chrome's built-in PDF viewer, which is a real limitation for anyone who reads research papers. QuickDef is the alternative that does work in PDFs.

### Is Urban Dictionary safe to install?

The extension itself is safe — it looks up slang definitions and nothing else. The content is the issue: definitions are written by users, often crude and sometimes offensive. It is the right tool for understanding internet language and the wrong tool for a classroom or a workplace.

### Can I use a dictionary extension on my phone?

Not in the same way. Chrome on mobile does not support extensions, so none of these tools work on your phone's Chrome browser. On a phone, use the dictionary app or the browser's built-in define feature instead.

## Verdict

Install **Google Dictionary** while it still works. It is the fastest dictionary extension, the most used, the only one here that translates and keeps a local word history, and it is free. Just install a backup alongside it, because its future is uncertain.

Install **Merriam-Webster** as that backup, and as your reference. Its definitions are the best written in this comparison, and it is the tool to use when the meaning matters — when you are writing, editing, or choosing between two words that look the same and are not.

Install **Urban Dictionary** only for the internet's own language. It defines the slang, the memes and the new words that no traditional dictionary will touch, and it is useless for anything else. That is not a flaw. It is the job.

And skip **Dictionary Anywhere** until it works again. A dictionary that cannot define the word "law" is not a dictionary. Check back in a few months — if it is fixed, it becomes the best Google Dictionary replacement in this category, because that is what it was built to be.
