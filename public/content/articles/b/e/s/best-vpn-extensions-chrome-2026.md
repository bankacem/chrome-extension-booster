## I Spent Three Weeks Routing My Whole Working Life Through Four VPN Extensions

I learned to distrust public Wi-Fi the expensive way. Two years ago I filed an invoice from a coffee shop, and by the time I got home my email had sent password-reset requests I never asked for. Nothing catastrophic happened — I caught it in time — but it changed how I think about the browser. The browser is where I do my banking, my client work, my email, and most of my reading. If Chrome is unprotected, I am unprotected, and a VPN app sitting idle on my desktop does not help if I never open it.

So for three weeks I ran four VPN extensions through my real working life: the coffee shop downstairs, a hotel network on a short trip, my home fibre, a video call with a client, a streaming session after dinner, and the banking site that blocks me every single time it sees a VPN address. I installed each one, signed in, connected, and lived with it for days at a time — not a ten-minute speed test, but the ordinary friction of opening tabs, loading pages, and getting work done.

The first thing I learned is that a Chrome VPN extension is not the same product as the VPN app that shares its name. Every one of these four is, technically, a proxy: it protects the traffic inside Chrome and nothing else. Your email client, your games, your video calls in a desktop app — all of that keeps using your normal connection while the extension is on. That is not a flaw, exactly, but it is the single most misunderstood fact in this category, and it changes what "best" means. A second thing I learned: none of the four extensions has a kill switch. If the connection drops, your browser traffic is exposed and nothing catches it. The desktop apps all have one. The extensions do not.

The four split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. NordVPN, Surfshark and Proton VPN all work as standalone extensions: install, sign in, connect, and the desktop app can stay closed. ExpressVPN opens in Remote Control Mode by default, where it drives the desktop app instead of connecting by itself — close the app's processes and the extension stops working. Only its Proxy Mode works alone, and ExpressVPN itself warns that mode offers limited privacy protection.

## The Four Contenders

**NordVPN** is the biggest tool in this category by a distance that is almost embarrassing for the others: about 15 million users on the Chrome Web Store, with a 4.3 to 4.4-star rating across roughly 9,100 reviews. The listing is titled "VPN for Chrome: NordVPN proxy protection", and NordVPN is admirably honest about what that means — it is a proxy for Chrome, not a whole-device tunnel. What you get in the extension is the full country list, 7,200+ servers in 150 countries, Threat Protection to block scam and phishing sites, a split-tunnelling panel that lets you exclude a domain or an IP address from the VPN, and WebRTC leak blocking. It connects on its own with the desktop app closed, which is what most people expect from a browser extension and not what all of them do. The Basic plan costs [$3.49 a month on the two-year plan](https://nordvpn.com/pricing/) — $94.23 up front for the first 27 months — and covers 10 devices, with a 30-day money-back guarantee. NordVPN is based in Panama, and its no-logs claim has been checked by Deloitte six times, most recently in February 2026. In [independent testing against the d3ward ad-blocking list](https://gizmodo.com/best-vpn/chrome), the extension's Threat Protection stopped 70 of 135 tracker domains, or 52% — respectable, but well behind the desktop version's 76%.

**Get it:** [NordVPN — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/vpn-for-chrome-nordvpn-pr/fjoaledfpmneenckfbpdfhkmimnjocfa)

**ExpressVPN** is the highest-rated extension here and the oddest one to live with. The Chrome Web Store shows about 1 million users and a 4.5-star rating from roughly 2,800 reviews — the best rating of the four. The extension does two jobs. In Remote Control Mode it is a remote for the desktop app: it switches the full VPN on and off, and the whole device is protected. In Proxy Mode it works alone in Chrome, but ExpressVPN's own description of that mode is blunt — limited privacy protection, coverage for this browser only. What it does better than anything else here is block trackers: in the same d3ward test, the extension stopped 118 of 135 domains, or 87%, beating even ExpressVPN's own desktop app at 71%. It also spoofs your browser geolocation, blocks WebRTC leaks, and includes location switching across 105 countries and 170+ locations. The Basic plan costs [$2.99 a month on the two-year plan](https://www.expressvpn.com/pricing) — $83.72 for the first 28 months, with four bonus months included — and covers 10 devices, with a 30-day money-back guarantee. The audit trail is the longest here: KPMG has assessed its no-logs claims around its TrustedServer system, most recently as of 28 February 2025, and Cure53 has audited the extension itself three times, most recently in June 2024. The catch is the renewal: Basic auto-renews at $99.95 a year, close to three times the introductory rate.

**Get it:** [ExpressVPN — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/expressvpn-vpn-proxy-brow/fgddmllnllkalaagkghckoinaemmogpe)

**Surfshark** does the most of any extension here, and it does it with no desktop app present. The Chrome Web Store shows about 1 million users and a 4.0 to 4.1-star rating from roughly 3,600 to 3,700 reviews — the lowest rating of the four, and the one I spent the most time trying to explain. Alongside the VPN there is CleanWeb ad and tracker blocking, cookie pop-up blocking, malicious-site blocking, and breach warnings. A bypass list turns the VPN, the ad blocker, or both off for sites you name. On the d3ward test it blocked 107 of 135 domains, or 79% — the same figure CleanWeb scores on the desktop, making Surfshark the only one of the four where the extension and the app block the same amount. The network is 4,500+ servers in 100 countries, and one subscription covers unlimited devices — the only unlimited-devices plan in this group. The Starter plan costs [$2.49 a month on the two-year plan](https://surfshark.com/pricing), with a 7-day free trial and a 30-day money-back guarantee, and its no-logs policy has been independently audited. Two things count against it. The extension is 96.9 MB, roughly nine times the size of NordVPN's or ExpressVPN's, and that low store rating is real: recent reviews complain about connections that drop and logins that do not stick.

**Get it:** [Surfshark — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/surfshark-chrome-vpn-exte/ailoabdmgclmfmhdagmlohpjlbpffblp)

**Proton VPN** is the only one of the four with a genuinely free plan, and that fact alone rearranges the whole comparison. The Chrome Web Store shows about 2 million users and a rating in the 4.3 to 4.7-star range across roughly 4,200 to 4,600 reviews. The free plan needs only a Proton account — no credit card — and gives you unlimited data with no cap at all, which no other free VPN in the store matches. The catch is that Proton picks the server for you, from 10 countries, at medium speed, and you get one connection. Everything else is strong. Secure Core routes your connection through a second server in Switzerland before it exits. Split tunnelling lets you name sites that skip the VPN. The extension is open source, the company is based in Switzerland under some of the strongest privacy laws in the world, and its no-logs policy has been independently audited. Paid Plus plans start at [$2.99 a month on the longest deal](https://protonvpn.com/pricing) and open up the full network: 20,000+ servers in 140+ countries, 10 devices, streaming support, and the fastest speeds. The shortfall is clear: the extension has no ad blocker at all. NetShield exists on the desktop, where it blocks 93% of the same test domains, and it does not come to Chrome — the extension stopped just 10 of 135 domains in testing, roughly what an unprotected browser does.

**Get it:** [Proton VPN — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/proton-vpn-fast-secure/jplgfhpmjnbigmhklmmbgecoobifkmpa)

## Where Each One Falls Short

NordVPN's weakness is the gap between its extension and its app. The extension blocks barely half the trackers the desktop version does, its ad and tracker blocker is a desktop-only feature on the cheaper plans, and it is the only one of the four that asks for permission to manage your downloads. The renewal price is the other sting: Basic costs $3.49 a month for the first 27 months, then renews at $139.08 a year — about $11.59 a month, more than triple the price that got you in the door. And there is no free tier at all, not even a trial inside the extension; you pay before you can test a single connection.

ExpressVPN's weakness is dependence. Remote Control Mode is genuinely the best way to use a VPN in Chrome — one click protects the whole device — but it only works while the desktop app is installed and running. Close the app and the extension stops. Switch to Proxy Mode and you lose the device-wide protection, the kill switch, and the full encryption, and you are left with a browser proxy that ExpressVPN itself describes as limited. There is also no site exclusion in the extension, so if one site blocks VPN addresses, your choice is the whole browser on the VPN or the whole browser off it. And like NordVPN, there is no free plan: you subscribe first, then you find out whether you like it.

Surfshark's weakness is reliability at the edges. The feature list is the best here and the price is the lowest, but the store rating tells a story the feature list does not: connections that drop for no clear reason, sessions that log you out after a browser restart, and support threads that end without a fix. I hit one of these myself — a connection that dropped twice in one afternoon on a hotel network and needed a manual reconnect both times. The 96.9 MB install size is also worth knowing about if you run Chrome on a machine with limited storage or a slow connection: it is the heaviest extension in this group by a wide margin.

Proton VPN's weakness is what it leaves out of the browser. No ad blocker, no malware blocking in the extension, and no streaming on the free plan — the three things many people install a VPN extension to get. The free plan's random country assignment is also a real limitation in daily use: I was assigned a server in Japan one morning when all I wanted was a nearby connection for a video call, and there is nothing you can do about it without paying. And while the free plan is unlimited, it is one connection, so the moment you protect your laptop in Chrome, your phone is on its own.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The coffee-shop Wi-Fi test.** This is the scenario that started the whole project. I took each extension to the same coffee shop, joined the open network, and did an hour of real work: email, a client dashboard, and a bank login. All four connected in under ten seconds and none of them dropped once on a network that is usually flaky. Proton VPN's free plan handled it without complaint, which is the point: for the single most common reason people install a VPN extension, the free option works. The difference showed up in what happened around the edges — Surfshark's cookie pop-up blocker and breach warnings quietly cleaned up the messiest pages, while Proton's lack of an ad blocker meant the same pages loaded every tracker they wanted to.

**The streaming test.** After dinner I tried to open a streaming library that is only available in another country. NordVPN, ExpressVPN and Surfshark all opened it on the first try, and the video played without buffering on my home fibre. Proton VPN's free plan did not — streaming is a paid feature, and the free servers are not built for it. That is not a surprise; Proton says so plainly. But it is the difference between a free plan that covers browsing and a paid plan that covers everything, and it is worth knowing before you cancel a subscription expecting the free tier to do the same job.

**The banking site that blocks VPNs.** My bank refuses logins from VPN addresses, which is exactly the situation split tunnelling exists for. With NordVPN I excluded the bank's domain from the VPN and logged in normally while everything else stayed protected. Proton VPN's split tunnelling did the same. Surfshark's bypass list did the same. ExpressVPN could not: there is no site exclusion in the extension, so my choice was to disconnect the whole browser or to open the bank in a different browser entirely. For anyone whose bank, work portal or government site blocks VPNs, this single feature decides the category.

**The day I forgot the desktop app.** I deliberately tested what happens when the desktop app is not installed, because that is how most people actually use a browser extension — on a borrowed laptop, a work machine, a fresh Chrome profile. NordVPN, Surfshark and Proton VPN all connected and worked exactly as before. ExpressVPN in Remote Control Mode did not work at all: with no app to control, the extension had nothing to drive. Proxy Mode saved it, but Proxy Mode is a weaker product by ExpressVPN's own description, and switching modes is a setting most users will never find. If you want one extension that works everywhere with nothing else installed, ExpressVPN is the wrong pick.

**The free-tier week.** For the last week of testing I ran Proton VPN's free plan as my only protection, to find out what "free" actually costs. The answer: almost nothing, for browsing. Unlimited data meant I never thought about a cap, pages loaded at normal speed, and the medium-speed limit never showed up in ordinary reading and email. What I missed was choice — the country was picked for me, streaming stayed blocked, and there was no ad blocking. One morning the assigned server was far away and a video call stuttered; on a paid plan I would have picked a closer server in ten seconds. Free is real here, and it is genuinely good, but it is a browsing plan, not a everything plan.

## What to Watch Out For: Permissions and Privacy

A VPN extension is a proxy, and you should treat it like one. It changes where your browser traffic appears to come from. It does not encrypt your whole device, it does not cover your email client or your games, and it does not have a kill switch — if the connection drops, your browser traffic is exposed with nothing to catch it. The honest setup is both: the app for the device, the extension for quick switching inside Chrome. Every subscription here allows that, and it is how I run mine now.

The renewal prices deserve a second look before you buy. NordVPN's Basic plan costs $3.49 a month for the first 27 months and then renews at $139.08 a year — about $11.59 a month, more than triple the introductory price. ExpressVPN's Basic plan costs $2.99 a month for the first 28 months and then renews at $99.95 a year, close to three times as much. Set a calendar reminder for the month before renewal, or the cheap plan you bought becomes an expensive one you forgot about.

Then there is the question of who you trust. A VPN extension sees every site you visit in Chrome — that is literally its job — so the logging policy is not a footnote, it is the product. All four of these publish no-logs policies backed by independent audits: Deloitte for NordVPN and Surfshark, KPMG for ExpressVPN, and an independent audit plus fully open-source apps for Proton VPN. That is the bar. The Chrome Web Store is full of free VPN extensions from publishers you have never heard of, and most of them are ad-funded data collectors. A free VPN has to make money somehow, and if it is not charging you, it is very likely charging someone else for your browsing data. Urban VPN's Chrome extension was caught in July 2025 harvesting AI chat prompts from more than 8 million users and selling that data to third parties. That is the risk a free extension from an unknown publisher carries, and it is why the only free plan I recommend in this category is Proton's.

Two Chrome settings are worth changing whatever you install. Chrome's own DNS-over-HTTPS can send your lookups to Google instead of your VPN's resolvers, telling Google every site you open while the VPN is on — switch it off under Settings, Privacy and security, Security, Use secure DNS. And check the extension's permissions before you install: open chrome://extensions, click Details, and read what the add-on can access. All four here ask to read and change data on all sites, which a proxy requires. The differences are the tell: NordVPN wants download access, ExpressVPN wants to talk to native applications, and those are reasonable for what they do — but a free extension asking for your camera, microphone and cookies is not.

One rule I follow with all four: never do anything through a VPN extension that you would not do on an open network without one. It is a layer, not a cloak.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If you want a free extension and do not want to create an account at all, CyberGhost's free Chrome proxy is the simplest option in the store: eight servers in four countries — the United States, Germany, the Netherlands and Romania — with no signup, no email and no bandwidth cap stated. It is a plain proxy, not a VPN plan: no ad blocking, no malware blocking, and its own listing says it does not block WebRTC leaks. It changes your apparent location in Chrome and stops there. The paid CyberGhost plan behind it costs about $2.19 a month on its longest term, covers 10,000+ servers in 100+ countries, and comes with a 45-day money-back guarantee.

Windscribe is the other free option worth knowing about, and it is the most generous free tier after Proton's. The free plan gives you 10 GB of data a month if you confirm an email address, or 2 GB without one, across about 10 server locations — and the extension bundles its own privacy tools, including an ad crusher and a tracker eradicator, which Proton's free extension does not have. It is founder and employee owned, based in Canada, and the paid Pro plan costs $5.75 a month or $69 a year. The 10 GB cap is the limit that matters: it is plenty for a week of browsing, and it runs out fast if you stream video through it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a VPN extension as safe as the desktop VPN app?

No, and the difference matters. An extension is a proxy that protects only the traffic inside Chrome; the desktop app encrypts your whole device, covers every app, and has a kill switch that stops traffic if the connection drops. No extension in this group has a kill switch. Use the extension for quick switching in the browser and the app for real protection — every subscription here covers both.

### Is there a genuinely free VPN extension for Chrome?

Yes — Proton VPN. Its free plan works in the Chrome extension with unlimited data, no ads and no logs, and it only asks for a free Proton account. The limits are real: Proton picks your server from 10 countries, you get one connection at medium speed, and streaming is paid-only. CyberGhost's free proxy and Windscribe's 10 GB free tier are the other honest free options. Avoid free extensions from publishers you have never heard of — most of them fund themselves with your browsing data.

### Does a VPN extension slow down Chrome?

Barely, for browsing. In three weeks of daily use I never noticed pages loading slower through any of the four on my home fibre, and video calls stayed smooth. What you will notice is distance: a server on the other side of the world adds latency that a nearby server does not, so pick the closest location when speed matters. Proton's free plan assigns you a server, sometimes a distant one, and that is the one case where I felt the slowdown. Surfshark's extension is also the heaviest install here at 96.9 MB, which matters on a slow connection or a full disk.

### Can I use a VPN extension to watch Netflix in another country?

Usually, yes, on a paid plan. NordVPN, ExpressVPN and Surfshark all opened a geo-blocked streaming library on the first try in my testing. Proton VPN's free plan cannot — streaming is a paid feature, and the free servers are not built for it. Streaming services also block VPN addresses regularly, so a library that works today can stop working tomorrow, and no provider can promise otherwise.

### Will a VPN extension protect my whole computer?

No. It protects only the browser it is installed in. Your email client, messaging apps, games and anything else on the device keep using your normal connection while the extension is on. ExpressVPN is the partial exception: in Remote Control Mode its extension drives the desktop app, which does protect the whole device — but that only works while the app is installed and running. If you want device-wide protection, install the app as well as the extension.

### Which VPN extension should I choose if I only want a free one?

Proton VPN, without hesitation. It is the only free plan in this group with unlimited data, a no-logs policy backed by an independent audit, open-source apps, and a company based in Switzerland. Accept that Proton picks your country, that streaming is paid-only, and that there is no ad blocker in the extension. If the 10 GB monthly cap does not bother you and you want a built-in ad blocker, Windscribe's free tier is the second choice.

## Verdict

Install **NordVPN** if you want the best all-round VPN extension for Chrome. It has 15 million users, the widest coverage at 150 countries, it works entirely on its own in the browser, and its split tunnelling solved the one problem — banking sites that block VPNs — that the others either handle worse or not at all. At $3.49 a month on the two-year plan it is fairly priced, and its no-logs claim has been audited six times. Set a reminder before the renewal, because $139.08 a year is a very different price from the one that got you in.

Install **Surfshark** if you want the best value. At $2.49 a month with unlimited devices and the most features in the extension — ad blocking, cookie pop-up blocking, a bypass list — it gives you the most for the least money. Accept the lower store rating and the occasional dropped connection.

Install **ExpressVPN** if you already run its desktop app and want the best tracker blocking in the browser at 87%, the highest store rating, and the longest audit trail. Do not install it expecting a standalone extension, because in its default mode it is a remote control, not a VPN.

And install **Proton VPN** if you do not want to pay anything. Its free plan is the only genuinely free VPN extension I trust: unlimited data, no logs, no ads, open source, Swiss jurisdiction. Pay for Plus when you want to pick your country and stream.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep NordVPN — the extension that worked everywhere, on its own, without me thinking about it, which is the whole point of a browser extension.
