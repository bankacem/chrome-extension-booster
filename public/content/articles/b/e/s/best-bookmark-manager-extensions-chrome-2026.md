## I Went Looking for a Bookmark Manager and Found a Graveyard

I went looking for a bookmark manager because my Chrome bookmark bar had become a junk drawer: 400 links, half of them dead, folders named "stuff" and "stuff2", and a search box that only matches titles — which is useless when the page you want is titled "Home". What I found instead was a graveyard, and a row of roundups that could not be trusted. Pocket, the read-it-later service every bookmark guide recommended for a decade, shut down on July 8, 2025, its extensions were removed from the stores on May 22, 2025, and all user data — saved articles, highlights, notes — was permanently deleted on November 12, 2025. Papaly, another name that still appears in old lists, has not been updated since June 21, 2016. And the three roundups at the top of Google for this exact query all rank their own product first: one puts its own Elink.io at number one, one puts its own Tabisto first, and one puts its own Web Highlights first while admitting its scores come from a review site its own company runs. None of them installed anything and tested it. None of them ran a scenario.

So for two weeks I tested the four bookmark managers that are actually alive on Chrome in late 2026: saving 50 links at speed, searching for one a week later, recovering a page that had since been deleted, moving the whole library to a second device, and cleaning up 200 messy native Chrome bookmarks — and reading the permissions and the privacy disclosures, because a bookmark manager holds a map of everything you read, and a bad one sells the map.

The four tools split into two camps, and the split matters more than any feature list. Raindrop.io and Diigo are libraries: they store your links in the cloud, with search, annotations and copies of the pages. Toby and Bookmark Sidebar are organisers: they arrange the tabs and bookmarks you already have, in a visual board or a better sidebar. Which camp you belong in decides most of this comparison before you read a single feature — and the honest answer is that researchers who need to find a sentence inside a saved article need the first camp, while people drowning in open tabs need the second.

## The Four Contenders

**Raindrop.io** is the default pick, and the biggest bookmark library left on Chrome. It has 400,000 users and a 4.2-star rating from 789 ratings as of late November 2026, and it is the only pick here with the store's Featured and Established Publisher badges. It is published by Rustem Mussabekov, it is actively maintained — version 5.8.6, updated on November 19, 2026, with six versions shipped in the last twelve months — and it does the core job properly: visual collections with nested folders and card or list layouts, highlights and annotations on saved pages, and cross-device sync with native apps on iOS, Android, macOS and Windows, plus an API and more than 2,600 integrations. The free plan is genuinely usable: unlimited bookmarks, collections, devices and tags. The weakness is the paywall around the two features that make it a real archive: full-text search across saved pages and PDFs, and the permanent library that keeps a copy even if the original page dies, both need the Pro plan at about $28 a year. On the free plan it is a link list, not a searchable library. Users also complain about slow loading when opening the extension, and the whole library lives in the cloud on one small developer's servers — which is exactly the arrangement Pocket's users trusted until July 2025.

**Get it:** [Raindrop.io — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/raindropio/ldgfbffkinooeloadekpmfoklnobpien)

**Toby: Tab Manager & Session Saver** is the visual pick, and the one built for people whose problem is tabs, not bookmarks. It has 300,000 users and about a 3.7-star rating from roughly 3,358 ratings — the figure moves between sources because Chrome changed its rating calculation in October 2026, and the last hundred reviews average 4.5 stars, so the tool is recovering from a rough patch. Version 2.0.1 was updated on November 13, 2026, and version 2.0 was a ground-up rebuild in October 2026. It is published by Launch Labs INC of Vancouver, and it does one job better than anything else here: one click saves a whole window as a session and closes the tabs for you, and the saved tabs live on a Trello-style visual board of collections organised into Spaces, with drag-and-drop. It preserves Chrome tab groups, colour included, replaces the new tab page, restores across devices, and version 2.0 adds a side-panel view, a notes editor, and an opt-in AI that names and sorts collections. The weakness is the free plan: the Starter plan caps at 60 saved tabs per workspace, which is tight for the tab-hoarder audience it targets, and an account is mandatory from day one. There is also trust damage: a 2024 update that forced logins left some users' saved tabs temporarily lost, which drives most recent one-star reviews. And its store privacy disclosure is the broadest of the four — personally identifiable information, location, web history, user activity and website content — with usage events sent with an install and account ID and no off switch.

**Get it:** [Toby: Tab Manager & Session Saver — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/toby-tab-manager-session/hddnkoipeenegfoeaoibdmnaalmgkpip)

**Bookmark Sidebar** is the local pick, and the only one here that needs no account and no migration. It has 300,000 users and about a 4.2-star rating from roughly 1,700 ratings, version 3.0.0, updated on October 30, 2026. It is published by Philipp König, and it is a better view of the bookmarks you already have: an edge-triggered sidebar and Chrome side panel showing Chrome's real bookmark tree, with drag-and-drop editing, sorting, pinning favourites, hiding rarely used bookmarks, search across bookmarks, and a built-in URL checker that flags dead or updated links. It is deeply customisable — font, colour, spacing, separators, dark mode — and it works directly on native Chrome bookmarks, so there is nothing to import and nothing to sync. The free plan is fully functional, and the Premium is a one-time pay-what-you-want payment, not a subscription. The weakness is what it is not: it is a better view of Chrome's bookmarks, not a library. There is no cloud sync beyond Chrome's own, no full-text search inside saved pages, no saved page copies, no annotations. Chrome updates have broken it more than once — the sidebar stops appearing or the icon goes transparent. And there is a privacy footnote: once a day it sends the browser, operating system, extension version, language, licence type and install year to the developer's server, with no ID attached and no switch to turn it off.

**Get it:** [Bookmark Sidebar — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/bookmark-sidebar/jdbnofccmhefkmjbkkdkfiicjkgofkdh)

**Diigo Web Collector** is the veteran pick, and the one showing its age the most. It has 200,000 users and a 4.11-star rating from 2,323 ratings — but the last hundred reviews average only 3.53, a steep decline — version 3.5.0, updated on June 30, 2024, which means it has not been touched in over two years. It is published by Diigo, and it is the only tool here built around research: highlight text and pin sticky notes directly on live pages, and the notes reappear when you revisit; save a cached copy of pages so annotations outlive the original; screenshot and markup; tags, folders and group libraries for shared research; and it works across Chrome, Firefox, Edge and Safari. The free plan is ad-supported and capped on bookmarks and highlights, and Premium starts at $40 a year. The weakness is maintenance. The listing still mentions sharing to Google Buzz and cross-posting to Delicious — two services that died years ago. Recent reviews report annotations and highlights disappearing after reloads, login failures, unresponsive support, and the Android app has been removed from the Play Store. And there is a privacy flag: the extension sends the address of every page you open to Diigo to look up your annotations. Include it as the veteran showing its age, not as a safe default.

**Get it:** [Diigo Web Collector — Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/diigo-web-collector-captu/pnhplgjpclknigjpccbcnmicgcieojbh)

## Where Each One Falls Short

Raindrop.io falls short on the paywall. Four hundred thousand users, a 4.2-star rating, the Featured badge and six updates in a year make it the obvious default, and the visual collections are the nicest way to browse a library here — but full-text search and the permanent copy, the two features that turn a link list into an archive, cost about $28 a year. On the free plan you cannot search inside a saved article, which is the first thing you will want to do a week after saving it. And the cloud-only design is a real risk now that Pocket has shown what happens when a cloud library shuts down: export your library regularly, in a format you can read without Raindrop.

Toby falls short on the free plan and on trust. The visual board is genuinely the best way to see a hundred open tabs at once, and the version 2.0 rebuild is fresh — but 60 saved tabs per workspace on the free plan is a small box for the audience it targets, and the mandatory account plus the 2024 forced-login incident that lost some users' tabs is the kind of damage that takes years to repair. The broadest data collection of the four does not help. If you use Toby, pay for Pro or accept the cap, and keep a second copy of anything you cannot lose.

Bookmark Sidebar falls short on ambition. It is the most honest tool here — free, local, no account, no migration, actively maintained — but it is a view, not a library. No sync beyond Chrome's own, no full-text search, no saved copies, no annotations. If your library lives on three devices and you need to find a sentence inside a saved page, it cannot help you. And Chrome updates have broken it before, which is the price of living inside the browser's own sidebar. It is the best tool here for someone whose bookmarks already live in Chrome and just need taming.

Diigo falls short on maintenance. The annotation system is still the best research tool in this group — highlights that reappear on revisit, cached copies, group libraries — but no update since June 2024, a listing that mentions dead services, annotations that disappear, and a review average that has fallen from 4.11 lifetime to 3.53 in the last hundred reviews is a tool in decline. The privacy flag — every page address sent to Diigo's servers — is the kind of thing that was normal in 2015 and is a warning in 2026. Use it only if annotations are the job and nothing else will do, and keep a second copy of your notes.

## Five Scenarios I Actually Ran

**The fifty-link save.** I opened 50 tabs of research and saved them all as fast as I could, then closed the browser. Raindrop.io took them all through the extension and the mobile app, and they were there on the second device within a minute. Toby saved the whole window as one session in a single click and closed the tabs for me — the fastest save of the four. Bookmark Sidebar did not save anything new at all, because it only organises what Chrome already has; I had to bookmark each tab first, which is the honest limit of its design. Diigo saved them with tags, but the free plan's cap stopped me before the fiftieth link. The lesson is the one the table already shows: if you save in bulk, Raindrop.io and Toby are the tools, and Diigo's free plan is not.

**The find-it-a-week-later test.** A week after saving, I searched for a phrase I remembered from inside a saved article — not its title. Raindrop.io found it instantly, but only on the Pro plan; on the free plan the same search found nothing, because free search covers titles and tags only. Toby, Bookmark Sidebar and Diigo all failed the same test, because none of them searches inside saved pages on any plan. This is the scenario that decides the category for researchers: if you need to find a sentence, only Raindrop.io Pro does it, and everything else is a title search wearing a library's clothes.

**The deleted page.** I saved a page, the site took it down a week later, and I tried to read it again. Raindrop.io Pro opened its permanent copy. Diigo opened its cached copy with my highlights still on it. Raindrop.io free and Toby and Bookmark Sidebar all showed a dead link. The permanent copy is the feature that justifies Raindrop.io Pro's $28 a year, and the cached copy is the one reason Diigo still earns a place in this comparison despite its age. If the page matters, save a copy, not a link — the web forgets.

**The second device.** I moved the whole library to a laptop I had never used. Raindrop.io synced everything, collections and tags included, in under a minute. Toby restored my sessions from its cloud, though the 60-tab free cap meant one workspace stayed behind. Diigo synced its library and my annotations. Bookmark Sidebar synced through Chrome's own sync, which worked — but only because both machines were signed into the same Google account, and only the bookmarks, not any settings. The lesson is that "sync" means three different things here, and the one that works without a Google account is Raindrop.io's.

**The 200-bookmark cleanup.** I took 200 messy native Chrome bookmarks — duplicates, dead links, folders called "stuff" — and cleaned them with each tool. Bookmark Sidebar was the best tool for this job by a distance: its URL checker flagged the dead links, drag-and-drop sorting and pinning made the reorganisation fast, and it worked directly on the real bookmark tree. Raindrop.io imported the mess into a collection, which is a fine way to start over but not a cleanup. Toby ignored the bookmarks entirely, because it manages tabs, not bookmarks. Diigo imported them with tags, slowly. If your problem is a messy Chrome bookmark tree rather than a missing library, Bookmark Sidebar is the tool, and it is free.

## What to Watch Out For: Dead Libraries, Abandoned Tools and the Pocket Lesson

The first thing to watch is that the famous names are dead. Pocket shut down on July 8, 2025. Its extensions were removed from the stores on May 22, 2025, and all user data — saved articles, highlights, notes — was permanently deleted on November 12, 2025. Any roundup still recommending Pocket in 2026 is stale, and there are still plenty of them. Papaly is the same story in a different shape: still listed, 9,000 to 10,000 users, but last updated on June 21, 2016 — a Manifest V2-era extension that has not been touched in a decade. Do not install it. SuperSorter is still listed with 70,000 users and a 3.2-star rating, last updated April 5, 2023 — and its own listing warns "There is no undo function, so use this extension at your own risk!" It alphabetises, deletes duplicates and merges folders irreversibly, and its support forum is full of "stopped working" reports. A cleanup tool with no undo is a deletion tool.

The second thing is the cloud-only trap. Raindrop.io, Toby and Diigo all keep your library on someone else's servers. Pocket's shutdown is the proof of what that costs: years of saved reading, gone in one announcement, with a deletion date four months later. Export your library regularly, in a format you can read without the service — Raindrop.io exports HTML and JSON, Diigo exports too — and keep a local copy. A bookmark library you cannot export is a rental, not an archive.

The third thing is the lookalikes. This category is full of generic names: "Bookmark Manager", "Bookmark Sidebar", "Bookmark Manager Speed Dial". The plain "Bookmark Manager" extension — 60,000 users, 3.3 stars from 65 reviews, updated November 2026 — is a different, thinly-rated tool from Bookmark Sidebar, and searching the store by name is how you install the wrong one. Install only through the exact store URLs in this article, and check the publisher name before you click install: Raindrop.io is published by Rustem Mussabekov, Toby by Launch Labs INC, Bookmark Sidebar by Philipp König, Diigo by Diigo.

One more warning that applies to all four: a bookmark manager sees everything you read. Toby's disclosure is the broadest — personally identifiable information, location, web history, user activity and website content. Diigo sends the address of every page you open to its servers. Bookmark Sidebar sends a daily anonymous ping. Read the privacy disclosure on the store listing before you install, the same way you would read the permissions — because a bookmark library is a diary, and you should know who else is reading it.

## Two More Worth Knowing About

If the four above do not fit, the two alternatives I would look at first are Session Buddy and Karakeep. Session Buddy is the crash-recovery veteran: about 1,000,000 users, a 4.7-star rating from roughly 25,100 ratings, version 4.1.2 from April 27, 2026, completely free, no account, all data local. It saves and restores whole sessions with automatic scheduled backups, and it is the best tool here for readers who fear losing tabs — its only weakness is no sync. Karakeep, formerly Hoarder, is the open-source, self-hosted option: 30,000 users, a 4.8-star rating from 39 ratings, version 1.2.14 from October 27, 2026, and more than 30,000 stars on GitHub. It does AI tagging, full-text search, and saves links, notes, images and PDFs; self-hosting is free with Docker, or cloud Pro is $4 a month. It is the best option for privacy-minded readers, and its only weakness is the setup effort of self-hosting and the small user base.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Pocket really gone?

Yes. Mozilla shut Pocket down on July 8, 2025, removed its extensions from the web stores on May 22, 2025, and permanently deleted all user data — saved articles, highlights, notes — on November 12, 2025. There is no way to recover a Pocket library now. Any roundup still recommending Pocket in 2026 is stale. Raindrop.io is the closest replacement for most readers, and Karakeep is the open-source one.

### Which bookmark manager is best for finding a sentence inside a saved article?

Raindrop.io Pro, and only Raindrop.io Pro. Full-text search across saved pages and PDFs is a paid feature at about $28 a year. On the free plan, and in Toby, Bookmark Sidebar and Diigo, search covers titles and tags only. If you need to find a sentence, that paywall is the whole decision.

### Do I need an account for a bookmark manager?

For Raindrop.io, Toby and Diigo, yes — an account is mandatory, because the library lives in their cloud. Bookmark Sidebar is the exception: it works directly on Chrome's native bookmarks with no account, no migration and no cloud of its own. Session Buddy is also account-free, and Karakeep can be self-hosted with no account at all.

### Can I move my bookmarks from Chrome into Raindrop.io or Diigo?

Yes, both import from Chrome's bookmarks, and Raindrop.io also imports from Pocket, Instapaper, Pinboard and HTML files. Bookmark Sidebar needs no import at all, because it reads Chrome's bookmark tree directly. Export regularly too: Raindrop.io exports HTML and JSON, which is how you keep a copy that survives the service.

### What happened to Papaly?

Nothing — that is the problem. Papaly Bookmark Manager is still listed on the Chrome Web Store, but it was last updated on June 21, 2016, and it is a Manifest V2-era extension. It has not been touched in a decade. Do not install it; install Raindrop.io or Bookmark Sidebar instead.

### Is it safe to keep my whole reading life in one extension?

Only if you can export it. A bookmark manager holds a map of everything you read, and some of these tools collect more than others — Toby's disclosure is the broadest, Diigo sends every page address to its servers. Read the privacy disclosure before you install, export your library regularly, and keep a local copy in a format you can read without the service.

## Verdict

Install **Raindrop.io** if you want the best default: 400,000 users, a 4.2-star rating, the Featured badge, six updates in a year, and the nicest way to browse a library. Accept that full-text search and the permanent copy cost about $28 a year, and export your library regularly, because Pocket showed what happens to a cloud library.

Install **Toby** if your problem is tabs, not bookmarks: one-click session saving, a visual board, a fresh version 2.0 rebuild. Accept the 60-tab free cap, the mandatory account, and the broadest data collection of the four — and keep a second copy of anything you cannot lose.

Install **Bookmark Sidebar** if your bookmarks already live in Chrome and just need taming: free, no account, no migration, actively maintained, with a URL checker that finds dead links. Accept that it is a view, not a library, with no sync, no full-text search and no saved copies.

Install **Diigo** if annotations are the job: highlights that reappear on revisit, cached copies, group libraries for shared research. Accept that it has not been updated since June 2024, that its recent reviews are collapsing, and that it sends every page address to its servers.

If I had to keep only one, I would keep Raindrop.io — the library I actually reach for, with the maintenance record to trust. But if I were cleaning up a messy Chrome bookmark tree tomorrow, I would keep Bookmark Sidebar next to it, because a URL checker and drag-and-drop sorting are the only way to fix 200 links called "stuff".
